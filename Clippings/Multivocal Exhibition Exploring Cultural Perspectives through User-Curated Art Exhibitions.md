## Abstract

Recently, cultural institutions have seen a rise in citizen curation projects, where people actively engage in curating and interpreting cultural artifacts. Citizen curation involves using collective knowledge and shared interests to create rich narratives around heritage items. This approach values the diverse perspectives individuals bring to preserving and representing culture. In this work, we investigate the potential of citizen curation applied to the subjective and culturally bound topic of symbolism. To do so, we present a pilot study on the development and user testing of the multivocal exhibition application. This application allows users to curate their art exhibitions by picking one theme and three cultural contexts that spark their interest. The app then selects artworks and symbolic data from HyperReal (cultural symbolism knowledge graph), Wikidata, and the National Museum of World Cultures (NMVW) to create a 3D environment featuring three artworks that symbolically convey the chosen theme from the perspective of the selected cultural contexts. The purpose of this app is to foster an open dialogue about the uniqueness and similarities of cultures, and promote inclusivity and cultural awareness, by showing how artifacts and what they depict can be interpreted in different cultural contexts, according to their symbology. Multivocal exhibition was tested by 12 users divided into 6 groups. The evaluation is based on the interaction between the users and the application, the analysis of the dialogues that were prompted utilizing the app, and an interview after trying two scenarios. Our results show how serendipity and surprise emerge as central themes in the cross-cultural experiences of the users. These elements play an indispensable role in cultivating engagement through symbolism, enriching users’ comprehension of diverse cultures, and heightening overall engagement. Moreover, the cognitive gap intentionally created by the app—triggering questions about why specific symbols convey particular meanings within certain contexts—proves to be a potent stimulus for user curiosity and engagement. We report additional findings, lessons learned and future work at the end of the article.

## 1 Introduction

In an era characterized by globalization and digital interconnectedness, the appreciation of art goes beyond borders and cultures. Artworks, and their symbolism [<sup>1</sup>](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fn1) offer a profound window into the collective psyche of diverse societies \[[25](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0025)\]. Symbols within art, representing shared beliefs, histories, and worldviews, can serve as a bridge for intercultural dialogue and understanding \[[38](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0038)\]. Digital technology has emerged as a powerful tool to facilitate this dialogue, enabling us (1) to interact with art, cultures, and symbols irrespective of individuals’ physical contexts \[[36](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0036)\], and (2) to represent our interaction with art and our interpretations in a machine-readable format \[[29](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0029), [30](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0030)\]. Traditionally, when visitors encounter artworks in a museum setting, they are guided through the lens of the curators’ view, who often propose insights into the artist’s unique vision and intended symbolic meanings. While this perspective is undoubtedly valuable, it often neglects a crucial facet of art: the myriad interpretations it can elicit from diverse cultural standpoints. Many artworks possess symbolism that resonates differently across various cultures, unveiling layers of meaning beyond what a single perspective can encompass. Unfortunately, in conventional museum exhibitions, the opportunity to explore how different cultures might interpret the same work of art is frequently overlooked.

In this article, we introduce an innovative approach aimed at addressing the issues of univocal, authoritative curation in the domain of art exhibitions. Our solution facilitates the exploration of cross-cultural interpretations of artworks through the proposed application, “multivocal exhibition” ([Figure 1](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fig1)), which constitutes an interactive **three-dimensional (3D)** environment resembling a virtual museum exhibition. Its core objective is to offer diverse, multicultural symbolic interpretations of artworks, agnostic from the constraints of their original cultural contexts. A key distinguishing feature of this application is its utilization of **linked open data (LOD)** from various sources, ensuring the inclusion of minority cultural artworks. This digital approach empowers users to curate virtual exhibitions rooted in “multivocal” symbolic interpretations, guided by the principle of citizen curation. Through this participatory process, users actively engage in shaping the narrative of the exhibition, democratizing the act of meaning-making and fostering a heightened sense of global cultural awareness.

*RO1: Assess the validity of citizen curation to investigate symbol conceptions and interpretations*.

Our first objective aims to validate the effectiveness of using a Citizen Curation approach for probing the multifaceted realm of symbol conceptions and interpretations. Through the app, we examine how users’ perspectives and choices play a role in their understanding of established cultural symbolism, gaining insights into the educational potential of citizen curation for uncovering the rich tapestry of cross-cultural symbolism.

*RO2: Promote cultural awareness by presenting diverse cultural perspectives on artworks and their symbolism*.

Cultural awareness is paramount in our globalized world. This objective seeks to harness Multivocal Exhibition as a vessel for presenting users with a kaleidoscope of cultural perspectives. By showcasing artworks and their symbolism from various corners of the world, we aim to broaden users’ horizons and deepen their appreciation of diverse cultural narratives. This objective underscores the potential of art as a medium for fostering cultural awareness and understanding.

*RO3: Examine how user expectations align with or diverge from cultural-specific symbolism*.

*RO4: Compare and contrast user experiences and interpretations between interactive digital platforms and traditional art exhibitions*.

The fourth objective places Multivocal Exhibition in comparison with traditional art exhibitions. By inviting users to compare their experiences and interpretations across these two distinct contexts, we gain insights into the unique affordances of interactive digital platforms. Understanding how technology augments or challenges the traditional art-viewing experience informs the evolution of cultural engagement in the digital age.

*RO5: Investigate the factors enhancing user engagement with cross-cultural symbolism in art*.

Finally, our fifth objective delves into the factors that enhance user engagement in the realm of cross-cultural symbolism. By analyzing user interactions with the app and their feedback, we unravel the elements that drive curiosity, captivate attention, and facilitate deeper connections with art and culture.

Due to the exploratory nature of this study, we decided to cover a broad range of objectives. This work presents findings on these objectives, the extent to which we were able to achieve them, and offers insights into the integration of technology in citizen curation projects focused on symbolism and cultural perspectives. Its general goal is to advance our understanding of cross-cultural symbolism in art and the transformative potential of citizen curation in enhancing cultural awareness.

## 2 Theoretical Background and Related work

Citizen [<sup>2</sup>](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fn2) curation denotes the facilitation of individuals, often lacking prior exposure to curatorial methodologies, in the application of curatorial techniques during their interactions with archival materials housed within memory institutions, notably museums \[[5](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0005)\]. The objective of this participatory endeavor is to formulate distinctive interpretations, disseminate personal viewpoints, and cultivate an appreciation for diverse perspectives. As part of the process, it affords members of the public the opportunity to engage in curatorial-inspired activities, encompassing the creation of both physical and virtual exhibitions, as well as contributing to or selecting exhibition materials. This democratized approach enables museums to present a “multivocal” narrative concerning their collections, rather than constructing exhibitions from a singular authoritative point of view.

One crucial dimension of citizen curation lies in its potential for fostering social cohesion, participation, and inclusion within the realm of cultural engagement. While Diaz-Agudo et al. \[[10](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0010)\] have explored the technical underpinnings of digital tools aimed at enhancing inclusion and social cohesion, recent efforts have emerged to leverage citizen curation in enriching the user experience. These endeavors transcend the conventional museum-going audience to underrepresented groups in cultural activities. Mulholand et al. \[[22](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0022)\] showed how the citizen curation format (\[[23](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0023)\]) can be used to engage nonmuseumgoers using the correlation of visual art with music, thus crafting more immersive cultural experiences integrating both visual and auditory dimensions. Additionally, Damiano et al. \[[9](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0009)\] introduced an innovative reasoning system that classifies museum objects based on value-emotion associations to recommend artwork during exhibition, ultimately reinforcing a sense of belonging and identity for individuals. Finally, Vishwanath \[[39](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0039)\] showed how users of different age groups interacted with virtual reality technologies, highlighting the playful aspects for younger generations and more contemplative listening experiences for elde generations.

The utilization of digital tools for the presentation of collections within the exhibition spaces of galleries, libraries, archives, and museums (GLAM institutions) has become a prevalent and established practice in contemporary times. The integration of virtual reality and information and communication technologies in museums enhances Cultural Heritage understanding and promotion by enabling users to engage with artifacts interactively, empowering institutions to deliver more effective cultural experiences, reconstructing artwork contexts, and showcasing previously inaccessible works through virtual exhibitions \[[1](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0001)\]. Using a virtual exhibition, Monaco et al. \[[21](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0021)\] suggests a paradigm shift wherein museum visitors assume the role of exhibition curators, granting them the capability to craft personalized virtual exhibitions aligned with their unique interests and objectives. By transferring the exhibition into digital space, it liberates from the constraints of a physical museum and alters the dynamics of power and authority traditionally linked to the institution \[[18](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0018)\].

Art museum curators typically aspire to construct narratives that encompass individual artworks, thereby establishing meaningful connections among the exhibited pieces and ultimately enhancing the overall exhibition’s impact beyond the individual components. However, for visitors to art museums, these interconnections are rarely explicitly evident, and the acquisition of such insights has a significant potential to improve their overall museum experience \[[19](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0019)\]. The manual examination of all potential relationships between exhibited artworks, however, is impractical. On the other hand, crowd-based citizen participatory methods \[[3](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0003), [16](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0016), [24](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0024), [26](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0026)\] for exploring these relationships often generate a plethora of connections that can lead to a new challenge of data curation to make substantive interest to museum visitors \[[7](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0007)\]. To overcome this drawback, the enhancement of crowdsourced knowledge through the integration of domain expertise has proven to be effective \[[4](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0004), [17](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0017), [35](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0035)\].

Symbols hold profound significance within the realm of human expression and communication, and as such, they permeate a multitude of contexts, including but not limited to art, literature, and music. Symbolic knowledge can manifest in various forms: it may be encapsulated within a particular work of art, or it can be standardized and encoded within the domain of expert knowledge. Additionally, symbolic meanings can also find their place within dictionaries and other linguistic resources, where they serve as vital tools for deciphering and understanding the rich tapestry of human expression. In the scholarly work by Sartini et al. \[[32](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0032)\], they introduce the simulation ontology, a conceptual framework designed to encapsulate the foundational knowledge pertaining to symbolic meanings, and they employ this ontology to model symbolic knowledge extracted from heterogeneous resources, ultimately culminating in the construction of a **knowledge graph (KG)** called “HyperReal.” This KG has been used as the backbone for connecting artworks based on their symbolic meanings \[[27](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0027), [28](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0028)\]. In this study, we extend the capabilities of HyperReal by integrating it into a virtual exhibition experience. This integration serves the purpose of providing a platform for fostering cultural cohesion, primarily through educating museum visitors about the symbolic meanings embedded within artworks across diverse cultural contexts. Recent debates about ”cultural humility” and ”cultural competence” \[[12](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0012), [13](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0013)\] view culture as a fluid concept, making it difficult to pinpoint symbolism to a specific cultural background. While we agree with this ever-changing view of culture, we use the data of HyperReal as a starting point to make users reflect on different perspectives on symbolism.

Finally, our work addresses some of the challenges around a multi- or polyvocal semantic web described in \[[37](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0037)\], specifically where it concerns the acquisition and presentation of perspectives based on KG representations.

## 3 Data Sources

In this section, we will describe the sources of the data that were included in Multivocal Exhibition, focusing on their content and how we extracted it.

### HyperReal.

HyperReal is a comprehensive KG that comprises over 40,000 simulations. A simulation, in this context, refers to the relationship between a symbol (like a lizard), its symbolic meaning (for instance, benevolence), and the cultural context(s) in which this relationship is relevant (such as the Egyptian culture). The information stored in HyperReal follows the structure outlined in the Simulation Ontology framework. An example of the *lizard-benevolence* simulation can be seen in [Figure 2](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fig2). We decided to include HyperReal’s data in our app because of its richness, as it contains instances of symbolism from more than 300 cultural contexts. This aspect substantially enriches the user experience of our application, facilitating the exploration of artworks through a multifaceted cultural lens afforded by the diverse array of symbolism. We extracted the entire KG through its data dump, available at [https://w3id.org/simulation/data](https://w3id.org/simulation/data).

![](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/cms/10.1145/3679020/asset/3b7651e9-6b96-4446-9582-18e382f7e9a2/assets/images/large/jocch-23-0203-f02.jpg)

### National Museum of World Cultures (NMVW).

The Dutch Museum of World Cultures houses an extensive digital collection that boasts an impressive archive of metadata encompassing more than 450,000 ethnographic artifacts. This remarkable assemblage spans a diverse array of items, ranging from ancient relics and religious icons to intricate craft work, each representing a unique facet of global cultures. We chose this source because of the richness of its collection, which allows us to provide users with artifacts belonging to a heterogeneous set of cultures. We extract images, titles, and descriptions of these artifacts from a data dump of the collection.[<sup>4</sup>](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fn4)

## 4 Entity Linking and Reconciliation

We employed two distinct methodologies to connect our art-related data sources, Wikidata and NMVW, and our repository of symbolic data, HyperReal. To link artworks from Wikidata with their potential symbolic interpretations, we performed entity linking between the symbols contained in HyperReal and the depicted entities within the artworks of Wikidata. Initially, we identified entities that shared associations with the same WordNet synsets, as both data sources incorporate alignments to WordNet. In instances where no direct matches were found, we conducted a string matching procedure by comparing the English labels in Wikidata with those in HyperReal. This approach yielded 2,692 successful matches between the two datasets.

In the absence of a “depicts” property within NMVW, we established connections between HyperReal entities and NMVW by conducting a string search on both artifact titles and descriptions within the NMVW collection. To achieve this, we initially used the Dutch labels from the Wikidata elements already linked to HyperReal. Subsequently, we utilized the Google Translate API to translate the labels associated with the remaining symbols in HyperReal into Dutch, enabling us to complete the remaining stages of the string search process.

At the end of this process, we had two aligned datasets. The first contained information about the artworks, namely unique resource identifier, image, title, depicted symbol. The second contained information about symbolism taken from HyperReal, namely symbol (only for those symbols that were associated with at least one artwork), symbolic meaning, context(s) in which the symbol symbolizes the symbolic meaning. The filtered datasets are available at [https://github.com/br0ast/MultivocalExhibition](https://github.com/br0ast/MultivocalExhibition).

As NMVW regularly update their collections and periodically make the public-facing versions available, scalability becomes a key consideration for adding new artworks to the application in future iterations. Two potential methods can be considered for this purpose. Firstly, if the artwork includes a dedicated tag indicating the symbols it depicts, we can employ the entity reconciliation technique used for Wikidata. Alternatively, if the artwork lacks explicit tags, we can consider a list of artworks, including their images and descriptions of what they depict. While the former approach provides more precise information, the latter may be less accurate and would require evaluation, similar to described in the following section.

### 4.1 Evaluation of the Linking

Despite its capacity to establish more connections, the string search approach carries the potential for generating misleading outcomes, as content within an artwork’s description may not necessarily correspond to its actual depiction. Furthermore, translation processes can introduce errors into the matching procedure. In this section, we describe our approach to assessing the accuracy of this matching methodology. We chose to employ a cyclic evaluation framework in which two human annotators assessed the quality of a representative subset of matches.[<sup>5</sup>](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fn5) Following this assessment, we measured the precision of the associations (correct associations divided by total associations) and the inter-annotator agreement using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient \[[8](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0008)\]. We set our threshold for accepting the quality of the association at 0.75 for the precision and 0.5 (moderate agreement \[[20](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0020)\]) for the agreement. The first evaluation cycle scored low in precision (0.347), and the annotators commented on the presence of a series of ethnographic objects that were extremely hard to evaluate. We then singled out specific terms that always resulted in a wrong association, due to exhibiting ambiguity from translation issues. In the second cycle, we slightly increased the precision (0.531) but still received remarks about ambiguous terms and also artifacts that were hard to evaluate. For this reason, starting from the third cycle, we decided to only include in our NMVW dataset artifacts which were assigned, in the internal taxonomy of NMVW, as “visual artworks by medium.” [<sup>6</sup>](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fn6) Moreover, we also excluded all the artifacts under a non-Open license. With this filtering, we reduced the size of the dataset from 400,000 to around 27,838 objects. Subsequently, we continued to create representative samples iteratively (filtering out the previously found ambiguous words) and assessing them until we reached our quality thresholds. For a comprehensive overview of the evaluation cycles, we refer to [Table 1](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#tab1), while [Tables 2](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#tab2) and [3](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#tab3) provide a detailed account of the content of the data sets following the implementation of all evaluation-based filtering procedures. Given that currently many KGs and data sources describe iconographic features of their artworks in textual description \[[2](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0002), [31](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0031)\], this evaluation strategy can be applied to different LOD museum (or other cultural institutions) collections.

## 5 App Description

The application through which users can interact with the dataset was created using Unity Engine \[[34](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0034)\], a popular open source game development platform. The application can be roughly divided into three parts: (1) The initial **user interface (UI)**, (2) the background process by which the exhibition content is selected and retrieved based on user choices, and (3) the final virtual, 3D exhibition model that users can walk through and interact with in first-person-perspective using their mouse and keyboard. All code is written using the Unity scripting API (C#). [Figure 3](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fig3) shows examples of part 1 (UI) and 3 (3D exhibition). The application can be run on Windows or macOS, and it is available at [https://github.com/br0ast/MultivocalExhibition](https://github.com/br0ast/MultivocalExhibition).

### 5.2 Custom Exhibition

The custom exhibition is generated based on user choices. For each chosen cultural context, one museum wall is populated with three artworks that display a symbol relating to both the context and concept choice. [Figure 3D](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fig3) shows an example of a wall displaying paintings related to symbols of the concept of anger (e.g., tiger) in the Buddhist context. To create a wall, a background script first retrieves all symbols attached to a concept-context-pair in random order, iterating through symbols attempting to retrieve a related artwork until three artworks have been found in the database. The associated URLs are used to retrieve images of the chosen artworks and display them in the 3D exhibition. On one hand, this means that the application requires a working internet connection to generate an exhibition, and relies on image links being up-to-date with online collections. On the other hand, the application does not need to store image files locally and can stay light.

### 5.3 3D Exhibit

The final 3D exhibit, which users can “walk” through using a basic disembodied first-person player character (standard assets), consists of a corridor-like room with four sections, so not all walls are visible from the start. First, the user is faced with a wall that explains the content of the exhibition and provides a reminder of the selection made at the start. The following three sections of the exhibit each display a different cultural context. Each artwork is shown together with a clickable “QR code” that opens the associated artwork website in a browser window, as well as a title and description text that provides information on artwork symbol and source. This provides some explanation for why a given artwork was chosen, and allows users to obtain more information about a display by browsing the respective Wikidata or NMVW page at their own pace.

## 6 Evaluation and User Testing Setup

In our qualitative evaluation of the application, we engaged 12 users, organized into six pairs. Our participant selection criteria include adults aged 22–32, with balanced gender representation and with different nationalities (French, Italian, Dutch, German, and Chinese). We also ensured a diverse range of museum visitation frequency and art familiarity, from infrequent attendees to those visiting museums monthly. We decided to have both pairings of users with the same nationalities and also have pairings with different nationalities. Importantly, all participants had prior experience with computer applications, although it should be noted that our study did not focus primarily on the usability of the application interface and controls. During the testing, we recorded users’ interaction with the app following two scenarios, and then conducted individual interviews.[<sup>7</sup>](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fn7) Both the testing phase and the interview were performed virtually, on a communication platform. In the first scenario, we decided to set the concept to “fertility,” encouraging participants to choose three cultural contexts. The purpose of this controlled scenario was to establish a common starting point for all participants, facilitating comparative analysis. In the second scenario, users freely selected a concept and three cultural contexts. We transcribed the dialogues that emerged during this testing. The app testing phase was conducted in pairs to foster dynamic interactions among users as they deliberated and shared their thoughts, accompanied by real-time commentary during the auto-generated exhibition. We asked users to think out loud about their expectations and motivation for their choices. During the test phase, one user downloaded the application and ran it, sharing the screen with the other user, making decisions as a pair. In the interview phase, we gained deeper insights into their experiences by posing four sets of questions. The questions are detailed in [Table 4](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#tab4) with a reference to the research objective they target. In the context of this study, we decided to formulate precise questions to target our research objectives because the format of the interview let us answer users’ concerns about their clarity, to make sure they did not misunderstood the questions. After the testing and interviews, in the analysis phase, conducted qualitatively, we identified recurrent themes and patterns from the transcribed dialogues and interview responses. These thematic groupings, which can be found in [Section 7](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7) allowed us to effectively address our research objectives while considering the broader user experience aspects of the application, beyond its interface and controls. As 9 artworks were generated during each scenario, we recorded 216 interactions between users and artworks (18 interactions per scenario, 32 for each user testing session). We stopped the testing at 12 users because we reached a point of data saturation \[[33](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0033)\]. This decision was prompted by the emergence of distinct insights providing adequate material for discussion, alongside a consistent pattern in user feedback highlighting both strengths and weaknesses, which will be elaborated in the following section. Additionally, for this study, we were interested in understanding individual experiences within specific cultural contexts. The study focuses on qualitative insights into users’ interpretations and expectations regarding symbols in art through a digital, automatic approach based on user curation. We aim to achieve a profound understanding of these phenomena analyzing specific insights rather than a generalization of the results to a larger population, which can be the objective of a future study. The full methodology to develop, evaluate, and test (user testing) Multivocal exhibition is shown by [Figure 4](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fig4).

## 7 Result Analysis

In this section, the responses and observations collected during the user study will be discussed. The initial analysis will revolve around user behavior throughout the app walk-through, including their reactions during the process. Following that, we will report the answers provided for the four open-ended questions during individual interviews. To enhance clarity and organization, this section is divided into subsections, each dedicated to a distinct part of the user study. The primary aim is to provide a comprehensive and structured report of the findings derived from the study. Furthermore, within each subsection, the findings will be further classified into different themes for reporting purposes. We will use the term interaction as the act of commenting on an artwork, or discussing it with the partner.

### 7.1 Observation during App Walk-Through

Reviewing the experience recordings provided the following insights and observations:

- *User Expectations*: Users often had preconceived expectations based on their own cultural knowledge and personal experiences. These expectations played a significant role in their interpretations of symbols and concepts. Some users were surprised by unexpected symbols, while others found associations that aligned with their initial expectations.
- *Surprise Factor*: The surprise factor was notable when users encountered unexpected symbols or interpretations. For example, the use of the squirrel as a symbol of fertility in Japanese culture surprised some users. This element of surprise adds depth to the user experience, prompting them to reevaluate their preconceptions.
- *Cross-Cultural Interpretation*: In 106 out of 216 interactions with artworks, users attempted to interpret symbols and concepts from one culture’s perspective, which sometimes led to incongruities with the intended cultural context. This highlights the challenge of accurately transcending cultural symbols beyond the original meaning (of the creator), and the potential for misinterpretation.
- *Artwork Impact*: Some users found that the artworks themselves played a crucial role in helping them understand the symbolism, while others felt that the connection between the art and the concept was not always evident.
- *Color Symbolism*: Users occasionally mentioned color as a significant factor in their interpretations. For example, the color green was associated with decay, while white was considered a symbol of health. This suggests that color symbolism is an important element in cross-cultural understanding.
- *Historical and Mythological References*: Users, in 26 out of 216 interactions, drew upon historical or mythological references to make sense of symbols. This highlights the importance of cultural knowledge and context in interpreting art and symbolism. At the same time, it connects users’ previous knowledge of a culture with artistic expressions of a different culture, enhancing the cross-cultural understanding.
- *The Role of Context*: Users, in 56 out of 218 interactions, found it challenging to understand symbols without a broader cultural or historical context, or a description that explains the reasoning behind certain symbolic interpretations. This highlights the importance of providing users with context to enhance their understanding. This aspect is also often mentioned during the interview, with contrasting opinions.
- *Interactions between Users*: In all the testings, we report a continuous dialogue between users. They would help each other understand and interpret the works of art. 9 users out of 12 explicitly praised this aspect during the walk-through, mentioning that if they were to do this experience alone, they would not have spent the same amount of time in front of an artwork, thinking about the potential symbolic meanings of it. We also report no relevant differences in the interactions between pairs of the same nationalities and different nationalities.
- *Rationale for Choosing the Contexts*: When prompted to choose the cultural contexts for their exhibition, users followed different rationales. First, in all the tests, users mentioned that, for at least one of the chosen contexts, their choice was based on their limited knowledge of it, showing the willingness to learn more about cultures beyond their own background. Second, five pairs out of six decided to include at least one of the cultural contexts with which they were more familiar. Only one pair of users agreed on a general theme (ancient cultures) and chose contexts related to that specific subset, while still alternating familiar and unfamiliar contexts.

Overall, these insights underscore the complexity of cross-cultural interpretation and the role of user expectations, context, and surprise in the Multivocal Exhibition experience. It also highlights the potential for users to gain a deeper appreciation of the richness and diversity of symbolism across cultures.

### 7.2 Findings for Q1 on Symbolism

- *Complexity of Symbolism*: Half of the users found the interaction between different cultural contexts and chosen concepts to be interesting, with the other half mentioning that the symbolism was not always immediately clear. This suggests that the depth and complexity of cultural symbolism may require more background knowledge or explanations to fully understand. In fact, several users (5) expressed a desire for explanations or more information about why specific symbols represented certain concepts in different cultures. Providing educational context or descriptions could enhance users’ understanding and appreciation of the symbolism. In fact, some users noted that, as non-experts, they found it challenging to discern the symbolism.
- *Clarity of Artwork*: Users commented on the clarity of the artworks to convey the chosen concepts. 11 out of 12 users mentioned both artworks that did not clearly represent the concept, and others that were more effective in this task. This highlights the importance of selecting artworks that can vividly communicate the intended symbolism, which in this case is a random selection as long as the artwork contains a specific symbol related to one chosen cultural contexts. There is a notable variation in users’ understanding and interpretation of the artworks and symbols, which reflects the diversity of perspectives and experiences. This is a valuable aspect of the app, as it encourages users to explore and think critically about cultural symbolism. In fact, some users even reconsidered the interpretation of an artwork after learning about its symbolism from a different perspective. In the context of fertility in the Buddhist culture, a painting with a bull (symbol of fertility) was shown, but the artwork was the depiction of a *Corrida*. In this context, users noted how the artwork could be interpreted as ‘death of fertility’.
- *Cultural Awareness and Bias*: One user made apoint about how cultural bias was affecting interpretations. This underscores the idea that cultural context can significantly impact how individuals perceive and interpret symbolism.
- *Serendipity and Surprise*: Four users mentioned being pleasantly surprised by certain concepts or symbols while answering this question. This element of discovery and surprise will be recurrent also in other questions and could be leveraged as a key feature of the app to engage and educate users about cross-cultural symbolism.[<sup>8</sup>](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#fn8)

### 7.3 Findings for Q2 on Engagement Factors

- *Understanding Different Cultures*: Eight users found that the app enhanced their understanding of how different cultures interpret the chosen concepts, and they found this aspect particularly engaging. They appreciated the opportunity to compare and contrast symbols between cultures, which led to insights about cultural perspectives. In addition, a user noted that the app improved his understanding of how cultures associate symbols with concepts that may not have an obvious connection in their own cultural context. This highlights the educational potential of the app. The users appreciated the opportunity to discover unexpected symbolism, such as the association between a peach and marriage. This suggests that the app can be a valuable tool for challenging cultural assumptions and preconceptions.
- *Surprise and Curiosity*: Eight users mentioned that being surprised, and having their curiosity piqued, was a key element that stimulated discussions. The element of surprise seems to be a key factor in maintaining user engagement. One user emphasized the treasure hunt aspect of looking for specific symbols in different paintings (i.e, if the artwork is said to depict a squirrel, finding the squirrel in the artwork). This element of discovery and exploration adds a layer of excitement and engagement to the experience.

### 7.4 Findings for Q3 on Personal Curation

- *Control and Personalization*: 11 users out of 12 appreciated the level of control and personalization offered by the app, which allowed them to curate their own exhibition based on their interests and preferences. This aspect of choice was seen as a departure from the traditional museum experience. The ability to curate their own exhibitions seemed to enhance user engagement with the artworks. Users felt more invested in the experience and were motivated to explore and discuss the symbolism.
- *Surprise and Discovery*: Five users mentioned that the element of surprise and discovery was a key factor in the difference between the two types of experience. They enjoyed not knowing exactly what to expect and the opportunity to explore unfamiliar symbols and artworks. As usually in traditional exhibitions, you know more about what you are going to get. In fact, users appreciated the randomness and variety of the experience, which they found different from traditional curated exhibitions. This aspect added an element of excitement and unpredictability. Moreover, they appreciated the possibility to view artworks by relatively unknown artists, giving their creations the same level of attention of more known artworks.
- *Context and Information*: Five users expressed the need for more context and information, especially when symbols were not immediately obvious. Providing additional information, descriptions, or explanations could enhance the understanding of the artworks. In traditional exhibitions, some artworks (especially the most important) come with captions that can ease the understanding.
- *Loss of Original Meaning*: One user pointed out a potential concern that personal curation might lead to a loss of the original meaning of the artworks, as users search for connections to chosen concepts. This raises questions about the balance between personal interpretation and the artist’s intent. Generally, curated exhibitions also maintain a connection to the creator and their intent, which seems to be lost in the personalized exhibition, although users were able to know more about the original artworks through the link to the respective source pages. Integrating such information directly in the app would have potentially improved the user experience in this factor.
- *Opportunity for Revisiting*: Three users mentioned the potential for revisiting the app multiple times to explore different combinations of concepts and cultures, which adds to its replay value. This complete shift does not happen while seeing the same exhibition multiple times in a traditional setting.

### 7.5 Findings for Q4 on Cultural Understanding

- *Symbolism and Cultural Perspectives*: All the users found that the app provided insights into how different cultures express and interpret shared concepts through symbolism, with seven claiming to now have a deep level of connections with those culture, and five at least a superficial level of knowledge/connection. Users mentioned examples, such as the association of certain symbols like the peach with marriage or the bull with fertility in various cultures. They appreciated the visual and comparative nature of the app, which allowed them to view artworks from different cultures side by side. This comparison was seen as a valuable way to understand cultural perspectives and symbolism. Users highlighted that the app allowed them to see artworks from different cultural perspectives and challenged their preconceived notions about the meanings of symbols. Users generally expressed positive attitudes toward the app’s goal of promoting cross-cultural understanding through art and symbolism. They saw value in learning about different cultures and their interpretations of concepts.
- *Knowledge Gap and Cognitive Exploration*: Seven users noted that the app created a cognitive gap between art and its interpretation in different cultural contexts (given the fact that these cultural contexts were different from the cultural backgrounds of the users), prompting them to explore and reflect on the differences in interpretation between cultures. This gap was considered an interesting and stimulating aspect of the app.
- *Room for Improvement*: As in previous answers, some users (3) mentioned that the app could benefit from providing more context and explanations about why certain symbols are associated with specific concepts in different cultures. This additional information could improve understanding. Still, while these users felt that the app provided a surface-level understanding, they acknowledged that it added a layer to their appreciation of art and symbolism.
- *Desire for Further Exploration*: All the users expressed a desire to try the app again, possibly with different cultures and concepts, indicating that even the ones who did not appreciate all of its aspects, they still found the experience engaging and thought-provoking.

## 8 Discussion

In this section, we reflect on how the findings align with our initial objective of the article.

*RO1: Assess the Validity of Citizen Curation to Investigate Symbol Conceptions and Interpretations*.

As the results in [7.4](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-4) show, the diversity in the understanding and interpretation of the artworks and symbols of the users reflects the value of the app in encouraging users to explore and think critically about cultural symbolism. Moreover, users found value in the app’s capacity to grant them a high degree of control and customization, enabling them to craft their personal exhibitions in accordance with their individual interests and preferences. Finally, the interviews conducted revealed certain shortcomings and led to recommendations for potential future improvements. A prevalent observation among the participants was the perceived lack of immediate clarity and comprehensibility regarding the symbolism employed within the studied context, given the lack of explanations. At the same time, for some, the cognitive gap created by the app (and the lack of “expert” curators) was considered a positive aspect. Therefore, adopting an approach based on Citizen Curation for a very subjective and thought-provoking domain, such as art symbolism led to polarizing results, which will be taken into account for future developments of the app as mentioned in [Section 9](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-9).

*RO2: Promote Cultural Awareness by Presenting Diverse Cultural Perspectives on Artworks and Their Symbolism*.

As [Sections 7.1](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-1), [7.2](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-2), [7.4](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-4), [7.5](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-5) show, many users found the application positively impacted their understanding of how diverse cultures interpret various concepts. They valued the opportunity to compare symbols across different cultures, gaining deeper insights into cultural perspectives. This highlights the educational value of the application and suggests its potential as a tool for challenging and reevaluating cultural assumptions. In addition, the findings of the pilot user study reveal a significant observation regarding the strong influence of cultural bias on the interpretation of artwork. This underscores how cultural context profoundly shapes individuals’ abilities to perceive and decipher symbolism in art, with participants’ diverse cultural backgrounds influencing their interpretations and emotional responses. It becomes evident that symbols, carrying specific cultural meanings in one context, may hold entirely different connotations or even go unnoticed in alternative cultural settings. This intricate interplay between culture and art interpretation sheds light on individuals’ capacity to recognize and transcend inherent cultural biases that may affect their appreciation of art. Furthermore, users frequently compared symbols across different cultural contexts. For instance, some users noted the use of the peach symbol in both Japanese and Chinese cultures but identified nuanced differences and similarities in interpretations (fertility in the former and marriage in the latter). This allowed users to grasp how symbols can transcend cultural boundaries while retaining unique cultural meanings. Even with a limited user testing, the transcriptions of the dialogue during the app walk-through and the answers of the interview reveal a positive and thought-provoking reaction of the users. In fact, there is a clear transition from a passive contemplation of an artwork to an active challenge in understanding the symbols of different cultures. When we mix information about artworks with HyperReal symbols, we generate *potential* interpretations of them, with the purpose of stimulating users to actively think about these interpretations and (perhaps) challenge them. Although we did not record any specific challenge to the interpretations during the test, we expect that extending the tests to a wider audience would also lead to this results.

*RO3: Examine How User Expectations Align with or Diverge from Cultural-Specific Symbolism*.

[Sections 7.1](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-1), [7.2](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-2), and [7.3](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-3) reveal the complex interaction between prior knowledge and the diverse meanings assigned to symbols across different cultures when exploring user expectations and cultural-specific symbolism in cross-cultural interpretation. Users often brought their cultural background and personal experiences into their interpretations, shaping their initial expectations and understanding. The element of surprise was significant, as users sometimes encountered unexpected symbols, prompting them to reevaluate their assumptions. Translating cultural symbols accurately was challenging, and users occasionally interpreted symbols from their own cultural perspective, with different degrees of complexity in understanding the difference between their own background and what was being proposed by the application.

Cultural context played a pivotal role in symbol interpretation, even for seemingly universal symbols like fruit representing fertility. Users occasionally referenced historical or mythological sources to bridge understanding, emphasizing the importance of cultural knowledge and context in interpreting art and symbolism. Users also highlighted the need for a broader cultural or historical context to gain a deeper understanding of symbols. In essence, this exploration underscores the intricate dynamics of cultural perception, demonstrating how prior knowledge and expectations influence users’ interpretations of symbolism across diverse cultural contexts.

*RO4: Compare and Contrast User Experiences and Interpretations Between Interactive Digital Platforms and Traditional Art Exhibitions*.

As [Section 7.4](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-4) shows, user responses regarding their experience with personal curation, as opposed to traditional museum exhibitions, varied widely. Firstly, many users expressed appreciation for the personalized curation feature of the app. They valued the ability to curate their own exhibitions by selecting cultural contexts and concepts, finding it a refreshing departure from traditional museum visits, where experts typically make curatorial decisions. This freedom and control allowed them to explore specific themes aligned with their interests either by picking contexts they were famliar with or by exploring unknown ones. Additionally, users found the unpredictability and variety of the app compared to traditional exhibitions to be exciting, distinct, and engaging.

Conversely, some users raised concerns about the potential loss of an artwork’s original meaning when viewed through the lens of personal curation. They questioned whether the artist’s intended message might be obscured or altered when seen within the context of a chosen theme. This ambiguity introduced an element of uncertainty and prompted questions about the validity of personal interpretations. Other users felt lost without additional descriptions and explanations for the symbolism.

Furthermore, users mentioned the repeatability of the app. In a traditional setting, an exhibition is typically viewed once and remains the same. In contrast, the Multivocal Exhibition, even when prompted with the same combination of concept and contexts, provides a different result each time by selecting random artworks containing the concept. With numerous combinations between concepts (over 7,000) and cultural contexts (over 300), the Multivocal Exhibition offers an ever-changing experience, a crucial element in its repeatability.

In summary, user reactions were mixed. Some users found enhanced enjoyment and understanding through personal curation, while others grappled with concerns about potential misinterpretations. This underscores the complex interplay between individual agency in curatorial choices and the preservation of an artwork’s intrinsic meaning, a tension that sets this digital experience apart from traditional curated exhibitions. It is important to highlight how Multivocal Exhibition only allows users to personalize the thematic part of the curation. Art curation as a whole would include several different aspects, such as the position of artworks in the rooms, their availability and also special consideration for inclusivity from physical, congnitive and social points of view \[[6](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0006), [15](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0015)\].

*RO5: Investigate the Factors Enhancing User Engagement with Cross-Cultural Symbolism in Art*.

[Section 7.3](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#sec-7-3) reveals distinct themes in user feedback that explain the app’s high engagement levels. The first theme relates to the interactive interface, appreciated for enabling users to select concepts and cultures, tailoring their experience to their interests. Engaging with partners in the selection of the themes or discussing artworks within the app promoted social interaction. The second theme is serendipity and surprise. Users were pleasantly surprised by the symbols encountered, sparking curiosity and discussions. Unpredictability is key to maintaining engagement, similar to a treasure hunt for specific symbols. The third theme emphasizes the allure of unpredictability and variety. Users enjoyed not knowing what to expect, relishing the opportunity to explore unfamiliar symbols and artworks. Randomness and diversity made the experience distinctive, adding excitement and driving user engagement. Although we obtained this results for our study regarding symbolism, we believe the positive characteristics highlighted by the users can be generalized and taken in consideration by future studies regarding user engagement with art within and beyond symbolic aspects.

## 9 Future Work

### 9.1 Improvements to the Application

We here list potential improvements to the application.

First, The Multivocal Exhibition app revealed the importance of striking a balance between allowing users to appreciate the cognitive gap created by unexpected symbolism and providing explanations for those seeking a deeper understanding. Future iterations of the app could consider implementing an optional feature that reveals explanations behind symbol meanings along with information indicating where such symbols and/or objects have been exhibited in existing museums. By including information about the object’s current location in the museum, and any relevant thematic context, users would have the opportunity to delve deeper into the meaning of these concepts while integrating their exploration with expert curated knowledge.

Second, to deepen users’ connections with selected cultural contexts, future improvements should include the addition of more artworks within the exhibition. These artworks should originate from the cultural contexts chosen by users and depict symbols related to the selected concept. Future enhancement to the app may include research collaboration with experts to identify artworks from diverse cultural backgrounds featuring relevant symbols. Additionally, users could be incentivized to contribute artworks from their own cultures or suggest relevant pieces, thereby enriching the exhibition with diverse perspectives. However, while these strategies aim to offer users a more comprehensive and immersive experience, the effectiveness of these strategies in practice and their susceptibility to issues, such as authoritative bias or vandalism require exploration.

Third, implementing features that facilitate collaborative exploration can foster discussions and knowledge sharing among users. For instance, incorporating social features that enable users to share their findings or interpretations with others can enhance the app’s educational and community-building aspects. In this way, users would effectively start a dialogue with a broader audience, making the experience even more tailored to user curation. In a similar vein, this study elicited discussion between participant pairs who were using the app together during testing, which could be directly implemented within the app as multiplayer mode. Practically, the current development platform (Unity3D) does provide different multiplayer solutions that can be considered depending on the desired immersiveness of the experience, target group and use context. For instance, the app could allow multiple visitors to collaborate and explore the exhibit via an integrated chat, or even appear as avatars in a shared virtual space.

Fourth, this application has been created using visual symbols taken from various artworks, without relying on expert-curated tags to identify the intended meaning of art objects from the NMVW. Working with domain experts or curators can increase the educational value of the app. Creating a “curator mode” of the app, incorporating expert knowledge into symbol interpretations based on specific cultural contexts, can provide users with more comprehensive and accurate information, enhancing their cultural understanding. Even if this approach requires a more qualitative approach, the matching aspect of the application would remain the same. Moreover, the user-curated versions of the exhibition could also be released in LOD, modeling the user’s comments and perspectives following the structure of artistic interpretations ontologies \[[30](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0030)\].

Sixth, exploring partnerships with educational institutions, such as schools and museums, can help bring Multivocal Exhibition into formal educational settings. Tailored versions of the app, built on the same technology, could be developed to align with different museum collections, providing scholars, teachers, and students with an innovative tool for cultural and art education.

Seventh, establishing a robust feedback mechanism within the app can encourage users to provide input, suggestions, and critiques. Continuous user feedback can serve as a valuable resource for refining the features and content of the application.

Eight, multivocal exhibition, as of now, still runs via an executable file for Windows and macOS systems. Expanding the accessibility of the app across different platforms, such as web browsers, mobile devices, and virtual reality environments, can widen its user base and offer more diverse ways to engage with cross-cultural symbolism.

Ninth, another improvement would be expanding the app’s functionality to feed information about visitor’s interactions with the exhibit back to museums and curators. The innate benefit of using digital means is that—compared to non-digital exhibitions—it is easier to collect and store data about user behavior, e.g., their choices for concepts and contexts, journey through the virtual space, interaction with paintings, and even voice commentary. Adding the tracking and logging of session data could make the app helpful to museums, e.g., to find out which concepts or artworks draw most visitors’ attention. Moreover, feedback buttons could be included in the application as a way for users to agree or disagree with the associations between cultures and symbols. In this sense, we believe that this specific version of Multivocal Application could be used as an experiment for the theory of cultural humility \[[11](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/doi/10.1145/#core-collateral-Bib0011)\].

### 9.2 User studies

In terms of improvements or future versions of the user study, a more extensive one considering participants’ demographics in the interpretation of artwork, along with their reactions after receiving education on artwork symbolism in various cultural contexts, can provide deeper insights into the influence of cultural bias. The future research could involve a diverse group of participants, exploring how factors like cultural background, age, gender, and location affect initial interpretations. It would also assess how education about the symbolism in artworks from diverse cultures impacts subsequent interpretations and emotional responses. By examining these dynamics through qualitative and quantitative data collection, such a study can contribute valuable insights into mitigating cultural biases and fostering more inclusive art interpretations, benefiting art education and cross-cultural understanding. To draw correlations between cultural backgrounds and other demographic factors, we would need to substantially increase the number of participants in the study. In the context of a broader study, we would need to adjust the interview questions. In the work presented in this article, the questions had a great degree of complexity because we could address any concerns in real time. When expanding the study to a wider audience, interview questions may be asked through an open response questionnaire. In this case, having simpler questions that do not require extensive knowledge of art, cultural symbols, or cross-cultural perspectives would be more beneficial.

## 10 Conclusion

To conclude, this article represents a pioneering pilot study at the intersection of symbolism, art, LOD, digital exhibitions, and citizen curation through the development and testing of Multivocal Exhibition. This innovative initiative breaks down the traditional boundaries of museum settings and leverages digital technology to offer users a multifaceted perspective on symbolism in art. By empowering users to curate their own virtual exhibitions based on symbolic interpretations from diverse cultural backgrounds, the app not only broadens horizons but also nurtures empathy, understanding, and a profound appreciation for the art, cultures, and symbolism. Multivocal Exhibition’s user testing has revealed that serendipity and surprise are powerful tools in engaging users and stimulating cross-cultural dialogue. This pilot study lays the groundwork for future research into the dynamic intersection of art, symbolism, and technology, setting the stage for the use of digital technologies for a deeper understanding of how individuals from diverse backgrounds perceive and engage with the world’s cultural heritage. By openly releasing the app, and proposing several improvements, we envision different customized version of Multivocal Exhibition, tailored to specific studies or adapted to different collections and other data sources.
