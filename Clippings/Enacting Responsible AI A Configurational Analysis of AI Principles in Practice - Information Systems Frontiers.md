## Abstract

Responsible AI (RAI) entails developing, using, and governing AI in a human-centred way to ensure its trustworthiness and alignment with human values. Organizations attempt to enact RAI through guiding principles that aim to minimize threats such as bias and privacy violations and enhance outcomes such as transparency and fairness. In the empirical study we report in this paper, we share insights about how AI experts view RAI principles, including relationships among them, these principles, and the enactment of RAI principles. Employing a sequential mixed-methods approach, we analyse survey data from 135 AI experts from Europe and America using fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), complemented by follow-up interviews. Our analysis identifies five equifinal combinations of RAI principles associated with high overall RAI enactment. The findings reveal the crucial role of accountability and safety-reliability principles and highlight the contextual variability of other principles. This research contributes a nuanced understanding of RAI enactment, moving beyond individual principles to demonstrate their complex interplay and their contextual dependencies in achieving responsible AI practices.

## 1 Introduction

Across organisations and industries, Artificial Intelligence (AI) initiatives continue to grow in scale and importance. AI is used to diagnose and treat diseases, combat fraud, optimize crop yields, and more (Amugongo et al., [2023](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR4 "Amugongo, L. M., Kriebitz, A., Boch, A., & Lütge, C. (2023). Operationalising AI ethics through the agile software development lifecycle: A case study of AI-enabled mobile health applications. AI and Ethics. 1–18. 
https://doi-org.sire.ub.edu/10.1007/s43681-023-00331-3
"); Ho et al., [2019](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR34 "Ho, C. W. L., Soon, D., Caals, K., & Kapur, J. (2019). Governance of automated image analysis and artificial intelligence analytics in healthcare. Clinical Radiology, 74(5), 329–337."); Wall, [2018](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR76 "Wall, L. D. (2018). Some financial regulatory implications of artificial intelligence. Journal of Economics and Business, 100, 55–63.")). Organisations that roll out AI initiatives are, however, also aware of risks such as bias, privacy violations, and unintended consequences (Fumagalli et al., [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR23 "Fumagalli, E., Rezaei, S., & Salomons, A. (2022). OK computer: Worker perceptions of algorithmic recruitment. Research Policy, 51(2), 104420."); Mateen, [2018](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR43 "Mateen, H. (2018). Weapons of math destruction: “How Big Data Increases Inequality and Threatens Democracy.” Berkeley Journal of Employment and Labor Law, 39(1), 285–292."); Rinta-Kahila et al., [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR68 "Rinta-Kahila, T., Someh, I., Gillespie, N., Indulska, M., & Gregor, S. (2022). Algorithmic decision-making and system destructiveness: A case of automatic debt recovery. European Journal of Information Systems, 31(3), 313–338.")). Responsible AI (RAI) aims to address these risks by promoting the development, use, and governance of AI in alignment with human values (Vassilakopoulou et al., [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR74 "Vassilakopoulou, P., Parmiggiani, E., Shollo, A., & Grisot, M. (2022). Responsible AI: Concepts, critical perspectives and an Information Systems research agenda. Scandinavian Journal of Information Systems, 34, 3.")). Through RAI, organisations can harness AI’s benefits while mitigating its risks, fostering the ethical and responsible design and use of AI-infused technologies.

Scholars and practitioners suggest defining RAI through mechanisms such as guidelines and principles (Mikalef et al., [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR47 "Mikalef, P., Conboy, K., Lundström, J. E., & Popovič, A. (2022). Thinking responsibly about responsible AI and ‘the dark side’of AI. European Journal of Information Systems, 31, 257–268.")). These guidelines and principles often address aspects like fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, and safety, to tackle issues related to potential AI threats to both individuals and to society as a whole. They provide actionable guidance and norms for AI that will be “good” for those it affects (Papagiannidis et al., [2025](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR55 "Papagiannidis, E., Mikalef, P., & Conboy, K. (2025). Responsible artificial intelligence governance: A review and research framework. The Journal of Strategic Information Systems, 34, 101885.")). However, these guidelines and principles are often presented as disjointed elements and implemented in a piecemeal manner (Munn, [2023](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR52 "Munn, L. (2023). The uselessness of AI ethics. AI and Ethics, 3(3), 869–877.")). Enacting RAI with such guidelines and principles, therefore, requires a shift in thinking from individual principles to a more holistic approach. Pursuing this shift can be challenging because of the abstract nature of RAI principles and the difficulty of reconciling them amidst competing values, trade-offs, and contextual constraints in practical AI deployment (Munn, [2023](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR52 "Munn, L. (2023). The uselessness of AI ethics. AI and Ethics, 3(3), 869–877."), Sadek et al., [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR69 "Sadek, M., Kallina, E., Bohné, T., Mougenot, C., Calvo, R. A., & Cave, S. (2024). Challenges of responsible AI in practice: Scoping review and recommended actions. AI & Society, 1–17.")). Further, these principles, rooted in risk mitigation, often lack a theoretical grounding. Effective RAI enactment, thus, requires the enactment of multiple RAI principles and examining their interconnected impact instead of considering each principle separately.

Enactment is both the process of making ideas real by acting upon them and the outcome of this process (Czarniawska, [2005](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR10 "Czarniawska, B. (2005). Karl Weick: Concepts, style and reflection. The Sociological Review, 53, 267–278.")). Weick ([2000](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR78 "Weick, K. E. (2000). Making sense of the organization, John Wiley & Sons.")) explains enactment in terms of how organizations construct their realities. Enactment is inherently situated so that even when a universal structure is being enacted, it has to be particularized to some extent (Orlikowski & Yates, [1999](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR54 "Orlikowski, W. & Yates, J. (1999). It's about time: An enacted view of time in organizations. MIT sloan school of management, Cambridge, MA, Working Paper 4055.")). Ribes & Finholt ([2009](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR67 "Ribes, D., & Finholt, T. (2009). The long now of technology infrastructure: Articulating tensions in development. Journal of the Association for Information Systems, 10(5), 375–398.")) conceptualized enactment, acknowledging gaps between today’s planning and tomorrow’s actuality. The concept of enactment emphasizes that actors shape what is actualized rather than merely responding to guidelines. Drawing on this stream of research, this study examines RAI enactment, aiming to fill a significant gap. While some studies have examined individual RAI principles, like fairness and transparency, limited research addresses how they are combined to enact RAI in practice (Ghasemaghaei & Kordzadeh, [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR27 "Ghasemaghaei, M., & Kordzadeh, N. (2024). Understanding how algorithmic injustice leads to making discriminatory decisions: An obedience to authority perspective. Information & Management, 61(2), 103921."); Watson & Nations, [2019](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR77 "Watson, H., & Nations, C. (2019). Addressing the growing need for algorithmic transparency. Communications of the Association for Information Systems, 45(1), 26.")). We designed an empirical study investigating RAI enactment through three aspects: practitioner perceptions of RAI importance, alignment of AI initiatives with RAI, and RAI implementation.

Understanding RAI enactment contributes to academic discourse as well as practitioner understanding of RAI principles (Mittelstadt, [2019](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR49 "Mittelstadt, B. (2019). Principles alone cannot guarantee ethical AI. Nature Machine Intelligence, 1(11), 501–507.")), pointing to more responsible AI practices. Our work is theoretically grounded in Rawls'political theory of justice (Rawls, [2001](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR65 "Rawls, J. (2001). The law of peoples: With “The Idea of Public Reason Revisited.” Harvard University Press")), which can be used as a coherent framework for governing major societal constituents, such as AI technologies (Gabriel, [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR24 "Gabriel, I. (2022). Toward a theory of justice for artificial intelligence. Daedalus, 151(2), 218–231.")). Rawls'emphasis on fair distribution of benefits and burdens informs our investigation into how different combinations of RAI principles enable RAI enactment in practice. Specifically, we address the following research question: *Which configurations of RAI principles are related to RAI enactment?*

To answer this question, our research method consisted of primary data collection from expert practitioners. Specifically, we collected data from 135 practitioners with significant experience in AI and analysed the answers using fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) (Ragin, [2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press."); Pappas et al., [2016](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR57 "Pappas, I. O., Kourouthanassis, P. E., Giannakos, M. N., & Chrissikopoulos, V. (2016). Explaining online shopping behavior with fsQCA: The role of cognitive and affective perceptions. Journal of Business Research, 69(2), 794–803.")), during the analysis, we did not focus on individual principles. Instead, we adopted a configurational perspective to explore how the enactment of RAI principles is combined to pursue the outcomes of interest. This approach, based on conjunctural causation (Slager et al., [2021](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR71 "Slager, R., Gond, J., & Crilly, D. (2021). Reactivity to sustainability metrics: A configurational study of motivation and capacity. Business Ethics Quarterly, 31(2), 275–307.")), equifinality (Katz & Kahn, [1978](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR40 "Katz, D., & Kahn, R.L. (1978). The social psychology of organizations. In Organizational behavior 2, pp. 152–168. Routledge.")), and asymmetrical causation (Misangyi et al., [2017](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR48 "Misangyi, V. F., Greckhamer, T., Furnari, S., Fiss, P. C., Crilly, D., & Aguilera, R. (2017). Embracing causal complexity: The emergence of a neo-configurational perspective. Journal of Management, 43(1), 255–282.")), highlights that RAI enactment results from multiple principles, can emerge through different configurations, and involves distinct factors driving its presence or absence. Follow-up interviews added to the fsQCA findings to develop a nuanced understanding of the and gain deeper insight into identified configurations. The theoretical anchor for our work (Rawls, [2001](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR65 "Rawls, J. (2001). The law of peoples: With “The Idea of Public Reason Revisited.” Harvard University Press")) made it possible for us to specify the outcomes that would allow a more comprehensive understanding of how AI risks can be responsibly mitigated.

The key contribution of the work is the identification of five equifinal combinations of RAI principles associated with high levels of effectiveness for RAI enactment. The findings reveal the crucial role of accountability and safety-reliability principles while acknowledging the contextual variability of other principles in counterfactual cases. These insights contribute a nuanced understanding of RAI enactment, moving beyond individual principles to demonstrate their complex interplay and their contextual dependencies in achieving responsible AI practices.

## 2 Related Literature

### 2.1 Justice as Fairness: Theoretical Framing for RAI

Rawls'"justice as fairness"is a theoretical conceptualization to address the problem of social justice and opportunity distribution (Gabriel, [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR24 "Gabriel, I. (2022). Toward a theory of justice for artificial intelligence. Daedalus, 151(2), 218–231."); Rawls, [2001](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR65 "Rawls, J. (2001). The law of peoples: With “The Idea of Public Reason Revisited.” Harvard University Press")). It was developed as a prescriptive framework for arriving at moral rules or principles (Hoffmann, [2017](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR35 "Hoffmann, A. L. (2017). Beyond distributions and primary goods: Assessing applications of rawls in information science and technology literature since 1990. Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology, 68(7), 1601–1618.")). Rawls foregrounds liberty and equality as key tenets in his framework. Liberty is the first and most vital tenet, meaning everyone must have some key rights as fundamental freedoms (Rawls, [1999](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR64 "Rawls, J. (1999). A theory of justice: Revised edition. Harvard University Press."), p. 53). For example, education, health, and personal property constitute fundamental and inviolable freedoms and rights as part of the tenet of liberty. The second key tenet is Equality, subdivided into two conditions: (a) the fair equality of opportunity means that social/economic arrangements must allow fair opportunity of access to liberties (access to options as a ‘right’ and an ‘effective’ equal opportunity); (b) the differences principle, which regulates the distribution of advantages across the members of society and suggests that inequalities in benefits or obligations are permitted when they aim to bridge the gap with the least advantaged members of society (Rawls, [1999](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR64 "Rawls, J. (1999). A theory of justice: Revised edition. Harvard University Press."), p. 53). Liberty precedes equality, i.e., mechanisms must first secure basic liberties before pursuing equality.

Rawls´ work is not specific about technology; however, it has implications for technologies with pervasive impacts on people´s lives, such as AI (Gabriel, [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR24 "Gabriel, I. (2022). Toward a theory of justice for artificial intelligence. Daedalus, 151(2), 218–231.")). From a Rawlsian perspective, social justice entails fair access to resources, which involves designing institutions and systems that guarantee equal access (Baygi et al., [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR7 "Baygi, R. M., Introna, L. D., & Ostovar, M. (2024). Beyond categories: a flow-oriented approach to social justice on online labor platforms. MIS Quarterly. 48(4)")). Scholars have applied Rawls'concepts in the context of information technologies and AI (Ashrafian, [2023](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR6 "Ashrafian, H. (2023). Engineering a social contract: Rawlsian distributive justice through algorithmic game theory and artificial intelligence. AI and Ethics, 3(4), 1447–1454."); Hoffmann, [2017](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR35 "Hoffmann, A. L. (2017). Beyond distributions and primary goods: Assessing applications of rawls in information science and technology literature since 1990. Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology, 68(7), 1601–1618."); Leben, [2017](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR41 "Leben, D. (2017). A Rawlsian algorithm for autonomous vehicles. Ethics and Information Technology, 19(2), 107–115.")). When impactful technologies including AI get integrated into societies that aim for social justice, they need to be compatible and supportive to liberty and equality. A key reason making this lens attractive for theoretical framing RAI is its compatibility with different sociocultural contexts. Rawls formulated the theory of justice with the goal of making it justifiable to individuals with diverse beliefs and philosophical perspectives. However, it should be noted that as a political theory, it requires political power to be exercised legitimately; hence, it is more suited for democratic societies. Still, as the work of Rawls points to human rights, it is more robust than approaches based on ethics that tend to vary significantly across cultural contexts (Fukuda‐Parr & Gibbons, [2021](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR22 "Fukuda-Parr, S., & Gibbons, E. (2021). Emerging consensus on ‘Ethical AI’: Human rights critique of stakeholder guidelines. Global Policy, 12(S6), 32–44.")). Justice as fairness was conceptualized to be independent of any specific moral or philosophical doctrine.

The different AI threats to individuals and society, such as bias and privacy violations, can be mapped to the two key tenets of justice as a fairness theory: liberty and equality."Equality"encompasses not only fairness and equal treatment for everyone, regardless of characteristics such as race, gender, or other personal attributes, but also involves addressing biases by creating positive representation for underrepresented or disadvantaged groups (Coeckelbergh, [2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR8 "Coeckelbergh, M. (2020). AI ethics. MIT press.")). This means that AI should not discriminate against any group or individual and should take into account the diversity of the population. For instance, if generative AI presents images that rarely depict female math professors, the concept of “equality” could support prioritizing images of female math professors to challenge stereotypes, influence real-world perceptions, and address imbalances in datasets (Fry, [2018](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR21 "Fry, H., (2018). Hello world: Being human in the age of algorithms. NewYork: WW Norton & Company.")). AI can aim for equality, contributing to the fair allocation of opportunities (Dolata et al., [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR12 "Dolata, M., Feuerriegel, S., & Schwabe, G. (2022). A sociotechnical view of algorithmic fairness. Information Systems Journal, 32(4), 754–818.")). Inclusiveness is required to ensure equality. Furthermore, in the context of AI, liberty refers to providing individuals with control over AI and its impact on their lives. Hence, liberty relates to the right to understand AI and to be able to benefit from it. Transparency, explainability, interpretability, and accountability can be associated with this. To ensure that everyone has the right to benefit from AI and avoid risks and harms, important concerns from the literature include benevolence, non-maleficence, privacy, and security, as well as safety and reliability. Aiming for beneficial and controllable AI can contribute to reducing coerciveness in our societies and enhancing liberty.

### 2.2 Principles for Responsible AI (RAI)

Responsible AI (RAI) points to the importance of aligning the application of AI initiatives to human values. It is a term widely used in policy documents, practice guidelines, and corporate communications (European Commission, [2019](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR14 "European Commission. (2019). Ethics guidelines for trustworthy AI [Online]. Available: 
https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/ethics-guidelines-trustworthy-ai
. Accessed 30 November 2022."); Google, [2019](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR30 "Google. (2019). Responsible AI practices [Online]. Available: 
https://ai.google/responsibilities/responsible-ai-practices/
. Accessed 30 Nov 2022."); IBM, [2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR36 "IBM. (2020). AI ethics (IBM’s multidisciplinary, multidimensional approach helping advance responsible AI) [Online]. Available: 
https://www.ibm.com/artificial-intelligence/ethics
. Accessed 30 November 2022."); Microsoft, [2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR45 "Microsoft. (2020). Responsible AI (policies, practices, and tools that make up a framework for Responsible AI by Design) [Online]. Available: 
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/ai/responsible-ai
. Accessed 30 November 2022."); OECD, [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR53 "OECD. (2024). Artificial intelligence principles [Online]. Available: 
https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles
. Accessed 30 November 2024."), U.S. General Services Administration, [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR73 "US General Services Administration. (2022). Responsible and trustworthy AI implementation [Online]. Available: 
https://coe.gsa.gov/coe/ai-guide-for-government/responsible-ai-implementation/index.html
. Accessed 30 November 2022.")), intended to mitigate increasing concerns about the potentially adverse impact of AI. To design and deploy RAI, scholars and other responsible entities have suggested a number of “principles.” For instance, AI4People (a multi-stakeholder forum that brings together global businesses including Audi, Elsevier, Meta, Fujitsu, Google, Intesa SanPaolo, Johnson & Johnson and Microsoft, civil society organizations, academia, and government entities) proposed five principles: beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice/fairness, and accountability (Floridi et al., [2018](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR19 "Floridi, L., Cowls, J., Beltrametti, M., Chatila, R., Chazerand, P., Dignum, V., Luetge, C., Madelin, R., Pagallo, U., Rossi, F., Schafer, B., Valcke, P., & Vayena, E. (2018). AI4People—an ethical framework for a good ai society: Opportunities, risks, principles, and recommendations. Minds and Machines, 28(4), 689–707.")). Another example is a set of principles with four core requirements: “Be socially beneficial”, “Avoid creating or reinforcing AI bias”, “Be built and tested for safety”, and “Be accountable to people” (Google, [2019](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR30 "Google. (2019). Responsible AI practices [Online]. Available: 
https://ai.google/responsibilities/responsible-ai-practices/
. Accessed 30 Nov 2022.")). Yet another set suggests principles for the responsible development and deployment of AI systems: privacy and security, transparency, fairness, reliability and safety, accountability, and inclusiveness (Microsoft, [2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR45 "Microsoft. Although there are multiple sets of RAI principles, they do overlap significantly and converge to an easily identifiable set (Fjeld et al., [2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR17 "Fjeld, J., Achten, N., Hilligoss, H., Nagy, A., & Srikumar, M. ### 2.3 A Configurational Approach to RAI Enactment

This study examines RAI enactment, focusing on how RAI principles become part of practices within organizations. Drawing on enactment research, we investigate how RAI gets enacted through three aspects: practitioner perceptions of RAI importance, alignment of AI initiatives with RAI, and RAI implementation. The concept of enactment emphasizes that actors shape what is actualized rather than merely responding to guidelines. So, practitioners´ perceptions are key. Weick theorized about enactment to explain how organizations construct their realities (Weick, [2000](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR78 "Weick, K. E. (2000). Making sense of the organization, John Wiley & Sons.")). Enactment is inherently situated and contextual; even when universal structures are being enacted, they get adapted and particularized to some extent (Orlikowski & Yates, [1999](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR54 "Orlikowski, W. & Yates, J. (1999). It's about time: An enacted view of time in organizations. MIT sloan school of management, Cambridge, MA, Working Paper 4055.")). It involves navigating the complexities of translating abstract ideas into operational realities by bridging the gap between notional and operative systems, planning for scalability and long-term sustainability, and ensuring that the resulting structures are both functional and usable within their intended environment (Grisot & Vassilakopoulou, [2013](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR32 "Grisot, M., & Vassilakopoulou, P. (2013). Infrastructures in healthcare: The interplay between generativity and standardization. International Journal of Medical Informatics, 82(5), e170–e179."); Ribes & Finholt, [2009](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR67 "Ribes, D., & Finholt, T. (2009). The long now of technology infrastructure: Articulating tensions in development. Journal of the Association for Information Systems, 10(5), 375–398.")). We draw from this prior research on enactment to investigate and contextualize the enactment of RAI in actual organizational settings.

Specifically, how RAI gets enacted can be observed through practitioners´ perceptions along three key aspects: a) do they perceive RAI as an important consideration in their organization? b) do they perceive AI to be aligned with RAI? c) do they perceive RAI being implemented? The first aspect is about reconciling notional objectives with actualized ones. In this process, parts of what is conceptualized may remain underutilized (for instance, some RAI principles may not be followed through). The second aspect concerns being prepared for evolution, envisioning a space of possibilities for RAI. This relates to planning for evolution and growth. The third aspect concerns the process of achieving the practical fit required for harmonizing technologies with RAI. This relates to making RAI available to situated practices.

RAI entails enacting multiple different principles. Rather than focusing on the net effect of individual principles, a configurational perspective allows us to explore how they combine to produce the outcome of interest, that is, RAI enactment. Examining configurations allows acknowledging conjunctural causation (Slager et al., [2021](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR71 "Slager, R., Gond, J., & Crilly, D. (2021). Reactivity to sustainability metrics: A configurational study of motivation and capacity. Business Ethics Quarterly, 31(2), 275–307.")), equifinality (Katz & Kahn, [1978](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR40 "Katz, D., & Kahn, R.L. (1978). The social psychology of organizations. In Organizational behavior 2, pp. 152–168. Routledge.")), and asymmetrical causation (Misangyi et al., [2017](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR48 "Misangyi, V. F., Greckhamer, T., Furnari, S., Fiss, P. C., Crilly, D., & Aguilera, R. (2017). Embracing causal complexity: The emergence of a neo-configurational perspective. Journal of Management, 43(1), 255–282.")). In the context of RAI, this approach highlights that enactment results from multiple principles, can emerge through different principles´ configurations, and that distinct factors may drive enactment presence or absence. Equifinality means that different configurations can lead to successful RAI enactment, offering multiple pathways to the same outcome. For our analysis, we apply fsQCA (Ragin, [2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.")) following the guidelines of Pappas et al. ([2016](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR57 "Pappas, I. O., Kourouthanassis, P. E., Giannakos, M. N., & Chrissikopoulos, V. (2016). Explaining online shopping behavior with fsQCA: The role of cognitive and affective perceptions. Journal of Business Research, 69(2), 794–803.")), Park & Mithas ([2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR58 "Park, Y., & Mithas, S. (2020). Organized complexity of digital business strategy: A configurational perspective. MIS Quarterly, 44(1), 85–127.")), and Mattke et al. ([2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR44 "Mattke, J., Maier, C., Weitzel, T., Gerow, J. E., & Thatcher, J. B. (2022). Qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) in information systems research: Status quo, guidelines, and future directions. Communications of the Association for Information Systems, 50(1), 8.")). Our research model (Fig. [2](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#Fig2)) illustrates how different principle configurations contribute to overall RAI enactment, shifting the focus from isolated principles to their collective impact.

The configurational approach aligns with Rawls' conceptualization of justice as fairness through their shared emphasis on interdependent conditions that collectively shape outcomes. Rawls argues that justice emerges as a conjoint effect of liberty and equality. The Rawlsian perspective, thus, guides us to investigate configurations of principles rather than the effects of single principles. Liberty relates to principles that ensure AI upholds human rights, such as privacy, security, benevolence, transparency and accountability. Equality emphasizes fairness and non-discrimination, relating to principles ensuring that AI fosters inclusivity, mitigates biases, and distributes opportunities equitably.

## 3 Methods

In this study, we employed a combination of quantitative and qualitative research to address our research question, integrating quantitative data collected through a survey with qualitative insights obtained from follow-up interviews. This study uses an exploratory sequential design to understand how firms achieve higher RAI enactment (Fig. [3](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#Fig3) provides an overview). This approach involves conducting a quantitative study first, followed by qualitative interviews with a sample of the same respondents (Ragin, [2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press."); Woodside, [2014](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR79 "Woodside, A. G. (2014). Embrace perform model: Complexity theory, contrarian case analysis, and multiple realities. Journal of Business Research, 67 (12), 2495–2503.")). By following this mixed-methods approach, we aim to discover how configurations of key RAI principles translate into overall RAI enactment. The empirical part of this study is split into two steps. In the first step, data are collected via an online questionnaire sent to experts in the EU and the US (study 1). In a second step, to interpret and extend the explanatory power of the results of fsQCA, a qualitative follow-up study with experts that displays features of each one of the configurations identified through the earlier quantitative study is conducted (study 2).

### 3.1 Survey Sample

Our survey targeted AI experts involved in AI solution development and integration as an AI initiative in organizations. The participants consisted of managers, AI governance experts, AI developers, and other relevant positions within these organizations. We identified and contacted potential respondents through professional groups on LinkedIn, such as the “Artificial Intelligence and Business Analytics” group and the website “Ethical AI Database”. This approach ensured a robust and representative sample for our study.

We reached out to selected respondents via email, specifically targeting those in high-level technology management roles who possessed knowledge of RAI operations and practices. Following an initial invitation and three subsequent reminders, each one week apart, we sent a total of 600 email invitations from September to December 2023 to potential participants experienced in AI initiative projects. To avoid sampling error bias, two preliminary questions were asked to make sure that the respondents selected for the study were appropriate: 1) Do you contribute, manage, or consult to AI projects? and 2) How familiar are you with the concepts of responsible or ethical AI? From these invitations, we received a total of 135 complete and 22 incomplete responses, primarily due to respondents' unfamiliarity with RAI practices in organizations (Table [2](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#Tab2)).

### 3.3 Survey Instrument Development and Validation

#### 3.3.1 Instrument Development

The survey instrument development commenced with the conceptualization of the constructs representing RAI principles and enactment, drawing from literature as outlined in Appendix A. The enactment of individual RAI principles is conceptualized as a condition for overall RAI enactment as an outcome. Enactment at the principle level and for RAI as a whole is evaluated through three metrics: perceived importance, alignment, and implementation of RAI principles. To articulate each principle, we analysed the literature and also engaged a panel of six experts with substantial academic and practical experience in responsible AI. Four of these experts had over 15 years of industry experience in data science and AI, while the remaining two were senior academics specializing in Information Systems in organizations. We provided the experts with definitions of each principle and asked them to answer the survey questions. Additionally, we sought their recommendations for improving or refining questions. Their insights led to minor modifications, particularly in clarifying distinctions between closely related principles such as explainability, intelligibility, transparency, and safety. These refinements enhanced the clarity of definitions and survey questions, ultimately strengthening the content validity of our instrument.

We then distributed the revised survey instrument to four C-level technology managers. These managers were selected from companies that specialize in responsible AI solutions for AI firms and possess extensive experience in implementing RAI principles. Taking their input into account, we carefully revised and refined the definitions of the principles to ensure they were more concise and understandable. A 7-point Likert scale anchored from 1 to 7 is employed. To evaluate enactment, we surveyed experts about their perceptions of 1) the importance organizations place on each RAI principle, 2) the alignment of RAI principles with organizational AI processes, and 3) the level of RAI principle implementation within existing AI initiatives.

#### 3.3.2 Instrument Validation

Before conducting the fsQCA analysis, we validated the measurement instruments which are related to the enactment of individual principles. This is done in four steps reporting on all principles. Beginning with indicator reliability, we evaluate how well each principle measured its associated constructs. All the measured constructs have had three indicators. The analysis reveals that all the outer loadings (OL) were above the recommended level of 0.60, which is acceptable for explorative research (Hair et al., [1998](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR33 "Hair, J. F., Anderson, R. E., Tatham, R. L., & Black, W. C. (1998). Multivariate data analysis. Prentice-Hall. Upper Saddle River, NJ, 730.")). However, we have found that indicators used to measure explainability and intelligibility load under the same components. The same issue occurred with indicators measuring fairness and inclusiveness. This suggests that participants perceived the indicators of these principles to measure the same constructs. Second, according to the statistical reports, which show a high correlation, we decided to merge intelligibility and explainability in one construct, that is “meaningful explainability”, as well as fairness and inclusiveness, into “Inclusive Fairness”.

In the final step of instrument validation, we tested the reliability and validity of the model using Cronbach’s α coefficient and composite reliability (CR), which should be greater than 0.8 (Gefen et al., [2000](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR25 "Gefen, D., Straub, D., & Boudreau, M.-C. (2000). Structural equation modeling and regression: Guidelines for research practice. Communications of the Association for Information Systems, 4(1), 7.")). CR is greater than 0.90 for all constructs, which indicates sufficient internal consistency. All Cronbach’s α values are greater than 0.8, indicating high reliability. Moreover, we assessed the convergent validity through the constructs average variance extracted (AVE). Convergent validity evaluates the amount of variance captured by a construct´s indicators related to the amount of variance that is caused by measurement error. The recommended threshold for AVE is 0.50 (Fornell & Larcker, [1981](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR20 "Fornell, C., & Larcker, D. F. (1981). Evaluating structural equation models with unobservable variables and measurement error. Journal of Marketing Research, 18(1), 39–50.")), and all AVE values were above, which means that various caused by random errors did not challenge the validity of the model. Also, Table  [4](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#Tab4) presents the descriptive statistics, AVE, and correlations for the study constructs. The results confirm that the square root of each construct’s AVE is greater than its correlations with other constructs, further supporting discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, [1981](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR20 "Fornell, C., & Larcker, D. F. (1981). Evaluating structural equation models with unobservable variables and measurement error. Journal of Marketing Research, 18(1), 39–50.")). Furthermore, correlations among constructs did not exceed 0.8, suggesting low multicollinearity and ensuring clear differentiation between constructs. ### 3.4 Survey Data Analysis

#### 3.4.1 Use of fsQCA

We applied fsQCA by following guidelines and best practices in IS (Mattke et al., [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR44 "Mattke, J., Maier, C., Weitzel, T., Gerow, J. E., & Thatcher, J. B. (2022). Qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) in information systems research: Status quo, guidelines, and future directions. Communications of the Association for Information Systems, 50(1), 8."); Pappas & Woodside, [2021](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR56 "Pappas, I. O., & Woodside, A. G. (2021). Fuzzy-set Qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA): Guidelines for research practice in information systems and marketing. International Journal of Information Management, 58, 102310."); Park & Mithas, [2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR58 "Park, Y., & Mithas, S. (2020). Organized complexity of digital business strategy: A configurational perspective. MIS Quarterly, 44(1), 85–127.")). Grounded in set theory, fsQCA is particularly suited for this study for three key reasons. First, it allows for the analysis of each case (an organization response) as a collection of conditions (principles) and assess how different combinations of these conditions contributed to the observed outcome. Each case was evaluated based on its degree of membership in various causal configurations. After estimating causal recipes, fsQCA enables researchers to return to the empirical data and interpret the configurations derived from the analysis. This allows for deepening the understanding of findings by combining configurational analysis with case-level insights (Greckhamer et al., [2018](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR31 "Greckhamer, T., Furnari, S., Fiss, P. C., & Aguilera, R. V. (2018). Studying configurations with qualitative comparative analysis: Best practices in strategy and organization research. Strategic Organization, 18(3), 482–495.")).

As a data analysis technique, fsQCA has become increasingly popular in configurational approaches across both business and behavioral sciences, applied at individual and organizational levels. The configurational perspective is built on the principle of conjunctural causation, which posits that conditions leading to an outcome do not act in isolation but rather in combination (Slager et al., [2021](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR71 "Slager, R., Gond, J., & Crilly, D. (2021). Reactivity to sustainability metrics: A configurational study of motivation and capacity. Business Ethics Quarterly, 31(2), 275–307.")). Additionally, fsQCA facilitates the examination of equifinality (Katz & Kahn, [1978](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR40 "Katz, D., & Kahn, R.L. (1978). The social psychology of organizations. In Organizational behavior 2, pp. 152–168. Routledge.")), by revealing multiple pathways that can lead to the same outcome. Finally, asymmetrical causation in fsQCA acknowledges that causal relationships are often not symmetric, meaning the factors leading to an outcome may differ from those leading to its absence (Misangyi et al., [2017](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR48 "Misangyi, V. F., Greckhamer, T., Furnari, S., Fiss, P. C., Crilly, D., & Aguilera, R. (2017). Embracing causal complexity: The emergence of a neo-configurational perspective. Journal of Management, 43(1), 255–282.")). Additionally, fsQCA distinguishes between necessary and sufficient conditions, offering a deeper understanding of how these conditions can combine to explain an outcome (Ragin, [2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.")). These conditions can be present, absent, or on a “do not care” state, that is when the outcome will occur regardless of their presence or absence. This distinction also leads to identifying core and peripheral elements, where core elements are strongly associated with the outcome, while peripheral elements have a weaker connection (Fiss, [2011](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR16 "Fiss, P. C. (2011). Building better causal theories: A fuzzy set approach to typologies in organization research. The Academy of Management Journal, 54(2), 393–420.")). This approach is beneficial for small or medium-sized datasets and offers insights into the diverse paths that can lead to a given outcome (Fiss, [2007](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR15 "Fiss, P. C. (2007). A set-theoretic approach to organizational configurations. Academy of Management Review, 32(4), 1180–1198.")).

We intentionally selected professionals with RAI expertise, as their knowledge is crucial for understanding how RAI is enacted in practice. Hence, our sample purposefully includes only organizations that have advanced in their RAI journey. Overall, random sampling tends to be inappropriate for fsQCA for two reasons: first, it is based on generalizations on properties of central tendency, variability, and the shape of sampling distributions (Ragin, [2000](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR61 "Ragin, C. C. (2000). Fuzzy-set social science. Chicago, IL: University of Chicago Press.")). Second, random samples risk overlooking unique or rare cases that may be crucial in explaining the outcome (Greckhamer et al., [2018](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR31 "Greckhamer, T., Furnari, S., Fiss, P. C., & Aguilera, R. V. (2018). Studying configurations with qualitative comparative analysis: Best practices in strategy and organization research. Strategic Organization, 18(3), 482–495.")). Unlike statistical methods that rely on random sampling for generalizability, fsQCA is case-oriented and requires theoretically relevant cases. Expert-driven sampling ensures that all cases in our dataset provide meaningful, high-quality insights into RAI enactment. Therefore, we carefully identified and targeted experts whose perspectives contribute to a nuanced, contextually rich analysis. FsQCA is designed to work with moderate sample sizes, as it identifies empirical patterns across cases rather than testing each theoretical combination. As Ragin ([2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.")) and Schneider & Wagemann ([2012](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR70 "Schneider, C. Q., & Wagemann, C. (2012). Set theoretic methods for the social sciences: A guide to qualitative comparative analysis. Cambridge University Press.")) note, fsQCA does not require all logically possible configurations to be present in the dataset. We followed fsQCA best practices for calibration and we present the approach followed in the following subsection. We tested the stability of our results with different calibration thresholds and we present the findings of sensitivity analyses in Sect. [3.4.3](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#Sec15).

#### 3.4.2 Calibration

As a first step for applying fsQCA, calibration is essential when assigning set-membership scores to antecedents and outcomes in fsQCA (Schneider & Wagemann, [2012](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR70 "Schneider, C. Q., & Wagemann, C. (2012). Set theoretic methods for the social sciences: A guide to qualitative comparative analysis. Cambridge University Press.")). Calibration determines the degree to which a particular case belongs to a set. It involves defining three reference points: full membership, full non-membership, and the crossover point, based on both empirical evidence and theoretical understanding of the context and cases (Ragin, [2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.")). Case membership is represented by a numerical value ranging from 0 (indicating complete non-membership) to 1 (full membership). All constructs in this study were measured using a 7-point Likert scale. Following the calibration guideline for calibration, we define three anchors: a value of the 95% percentile for full membership, 5% percentile for full non-membership, and 5% percentile as the crossover point (Ragin, [2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.")).

These anchors were chosen in several iterations based on theoretical knowledge and the distribution of the dataset. As our dataset was skewed more to the higher scales and by choosing, for instance, 2 as full non-membership, we would have fewer cases with lower functions. We employed percentile-based anchors to provide a statistical, data-driven, and context-sensitive approach that more effectively captures the variability in how respondents perceive RAI principles. For example, by setting the 5 th percentile for full non-membership, we ensure that significantly underperforming cases in RAI enactment are accurately identified (as demonstrated in Table [4](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#Tab4), where non-membership is high for most principles). This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of the response distribution. The 50 th percentile represents the crossover point, while the 95 th percentile reflects high levels of RAI enactment, enhancing both the precision and validity of the analysis. The calibration anchors of the constructs are shown in Table  [5](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#Tab5).

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Configurations for RAI Enactment

Truth table analysis was adopted to identify combinations of RAI principles that are sufficient for RAI enactment (Ragin, [2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.")). In fsQCA, counterfactual analysis is inherently involved whenever researchers draw causal inferences from “naturally occurring” social data where limited diversity is common. This means addressing hypothetical scenarios—absent combinations of causal conditions—that do not appear in the dataset (Ragin & Sonnett, [2005](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR60 "Ragin, C. C., & Sonnett, J. (2005). Between complexity and parsimony: Limited diversity, counterfactual cases, and comparative analysis. In S. Kropp & M. Minkenberg (Eds.), Vergleichen in der Politikwissenschaft (pp. 180–197). VS Verlag für Sozialwissenschaften.")). Three types of solutions, the complex, parsimonious, and intermediate solutions, can be obtained through fsQCA. A complex solution includes configurations without any counterfactuals, meaning all possible combinations of conditions are explicitly presented. A parsimonious solution includes configurations with both “easy” and “difficult” counterfactuals, which means solutions incorporate existing knowledge and empirical evidence that are both straightforward to justify (easy counterfactuals) and those that may require stronger theoretical or empirical justification (difficult counterfactuals) (Ragin, [2008](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR62 "Ragin, C. C. (2008). Redesigning social inquiry: Fuzzy sets and beyond. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.")). An intermediate solution includes configurations only with “easy” counterfactuals (Fiss, [2007](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR15 "Fiss, P. C. (2007). A set-theoretic approach to organizational configurations. Academy of Management Review, 32(4), 1180–1198.")). Elements that are part of both parsimonious and intermediate solutions are called core conditions, which have a stronger relationship with the outcome as they cannot be left out of any configuration. Elements that only appear in the intermediate solution but not in the parsimonious solution are peripheral conditions, which suggest a weaker relationship with the outcome. The output of the fsQCA analysis, including both the parsimonious and intermediate solutions, is presented in Fig. [5](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#Fig5) in Appendix C.

### 4.2 Analysis of Qualitative Follow-Up Interviews

Our qualitative data analysis provides complementary insights into the experts' perceptions regarding RAI enactment. We employed a bridging approach to align the findings from fsQCA with those from the in-depth qualitative study (Venkatesh et al., [2013](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR75 "Venkatesh, V., Brown, S. A., & Bala, H. (2013). Bridging the qualitative-quantitative divide: Guidelines for conducting mixed methods research in information systems. MIS Quarterly, 37(1), 21–54.")). This method allowed us to cross-validate the fsQCA results with interview data and thorough analysis. The bridging process revealed significant convergence between the two sets of findings, reinforcing the overall conclusions. For the analysis of the interviews, we used an open-coded approach to identify emerging themes from the data, which were then used to frame the propositions and recommendations for RAI enactment.

The qualitative findings confirmed all five configurations identified by fsQCA and also provided deeper insights into the role certain conditions play in specific contexts. We interviewed Case 135 from **configuration 1**, which involves an expert working on an e-commerce platform that provides product recommendations to customers. The expert in this case perceived that the system is designed to enhance user experience by suggesting relevant products based on browsing and purchase history. The developer’s focus on benevolence and reliability (ensuring recommendations are beneficial, harmless, and accurate) and the reliance on user behavior data make fairness a"do-not-care"condition, as the perceived risk of systemic discrimination is minimal, and its potential impact on users is limited. The expert emphasized this perspective, stating:

> *“Our mission is providing accurate recommendations that are beneficial and cause no harm. The recommendations are based on user behavior and preferences, making biased outcomes unlikely in this setting.” (case 135, configuration 1).*

The reasoning behind fairness being a"do-not-care"condition stems from the fact that the recommendation system does not rely on demographic attributes. Instead, it is based on user interactions—such as browsing and purchase history—thus avoiding bias. The expert noted:

> *“We ensure that our recommendation system avoids discrimination by relying solely on user behavior rather than demographic attributes, this approach helps mitigate proxy biases* ” *(case 135, configuration 1).*

Moreover, the expert noted that the system includes features that allow users to adjust their recommendations, for instance, by disliking suggestions, and users have control over the recommendations they receive:

> *“We obtain users'consent before recommending products based on their activity on our platform. If they come across a suggestion they don’t like, they can simply dislike it, and we will adjust recommendations accordingly” (case 135, configuration 1).*

The interview with case 69 (**configuration 2**) highlighted that since they do not work with sensitive personal data, privacy is considered a less pressing issue in their RAI practices, making it a peripheral condition. Additionally, they view their well-established pre-existing IT infrastructure as providing a strong foundation for security, which is why they consider security a"do-not-care"condition. However, this does not mean security is unimportant. Rather, they believe their organization already has well-established infrastructures and security frameworks in place, allowing them to focus more on operational efficiency in achieving RAI enactment. The expert stated:

> *“Our organization focuses on building a scalable AI solution to cut costs and improve efficiency. While privacy and security matter, they aren’t our main priorities since our model mainly deals with production metrics, which keeps privacy risks low. With a strong IT infrastructure and solid security protocols already in place, we do not see them as a big concern in our current work.”(case 69, configuration 2).*

The interview with case 12 (**configuration 3)** involves a startup that develops an AI-powered gaming platform for educational purposes, focusing on engagement and learning outcomes. ## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 Theoretical Implications

Our work responds to scholarly calls on the importance of translating RAI principles into practice (Arbelaez Ossa et al., [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR5 "Arbelaez Ossa, L., Lorenzini, G., Milford, S. R., Shaw, D., Elger, B. S., & Rost, M. (2024). Integrating ethics in AI development: A qualitative study. BMC Medical Ethics, 25(1), 10."); Mikalef, [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR46 "Mikalef, P. (2024). Responsible AI governance: From ideation to implementation. In I. Constantiou, M. P. Joshi, & M. Stelmaszak (Eds.), Research Handbook on Artificial Intelligence and Decision Making in Organizations (pp. 126–142). Edward Elgar Publishing."); Munn, [2023](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR52 "Munn, L. (2023). The uselessness of AI ethics. AI and Ethics, 3(3), 869–877.")). The theoretical contributions of this study are twofold. First, we extend the current RAI literature by conceptualizing RAI enactment, grounding this in Rawls's theory of justice as fairness (Rawls, [2001](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR65 "Rawls, J. (2001). The law of peoples: With “The Idea of Public Reason Revisited.” Harvard University Press")). RAI principles are rooted in risk mitigation and have been lacking a theoretical framing that could help organizations make sense of them as a coherent whole. This is the first study introducing an overarching theoretical framing. This framing aligns with prior literature pointing to the importance of ensuring that AI initiatives uphold individual rights and promote equal access to opportunities (Baygi et al., [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR7 "Baygi, R. M., Introna, L. D., & Ostovar, M. (2024). Beyond categories: a flow-oriented approach to social justice on online labor platforms. MIS Quarterly. 48(4)")). Taking a Rawlsian perspective on RAI orients attention to interdependent conditions that collectively shape outcomes. Rawls argues that justice emerges as a conjoint effect of liberty and equality. Liberty relates to principles ensuring that AI systems uphold human rights, such as privacy, security, benevolence, transparency, and accountability. Equality relates to fairness and non-discrimination, linking to principles for AI inclusivity and bias mitigation. A Rawlsian perspective is also well-aligned with the use of fsQCA for data analysis as it supports equifinality and asymmetrical causation.

Rawls acknowledges that different structures can achieve justice as long as they uphold fairness (Rawls, [2001](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR65 "Rawls, J. (2001). The law of peoples: With “The Idea of Public Reason Revisited.” Harvard University Press")). Asymmetrical causation highlights that the factors driving successful RAI enactment may not be simply the inverse of those leading to its absence. Equifinality suggests that multiple pathways can lead to successful RAI enactment, so different combinations of principles can produce responsible AI.

Second, using a configurational approach (fsQCA), we identify how combinations of principles contribute to RAI enactment, shifting attention from individual principles to configurations of principles. Our findings show that accountability and safety-reliability are present in all configurations as core principles, implying that these need to be tied with other principles in order to create a successful RAI strategy. It is important to highlight that while accountability and safety-reliability are core principles, they are not sufficient on their own for achieving RAI enactment in any configuration. Our findings regarding the role of accountability and safety-reliability extend arguments that have been used in prior work to explain the effect of accountability mechanisms and of measures to ensure the reliability of AI-based recommendations or predictions (Díaz-Rodríguez et al., [2023](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR11 "Díaz-Rodríguez, N., Del Ser, J., Coeckelbergh, M., López de Prado, M., Herrera-Viedma, E., & Herrera, F. (2023). Connecting the dots in trustworthy Artificial Intelligence: From AI principles, ethics, and key requirements to responsible AI systems and regulation. Information Fusion, 99, 101896.")). Empirical research has highlighted the importance of these two principles for AI initiatives. The configurational approach provides new insights indicating that the key to RAI enactment lies not in the two individual principles alone but rather in how these core principles are configured together with other principles. By conducting interviews with experts from each configuration identified in our fsQCA findings, we obtain a deeper understanding of the contextual nature of RAI principles and the inherent tensions in their implementation. These interviews provide valuable insights, helping to explain why certain principles are present, absent, or considered irrelevant in different cases. Through this analysis, we contribute to the broader discourse on the enactment and operationalization of RAI principles (Advenica, [2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR1 "Advenica. (2020). Digital responsibility – the only viable way forward. 
https://www.advenica.com/digital-responsibility-only-viable-way-forward
. Accessed 15 Dec 2023."); PwC, [2020](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR59 "PWC. (2020). Digital ethics – operation, values and attitudes for a digital world. 
https://www.pwc.de/en/consulting/42013_WP_Digital_Ethics_EN_neu_200401_SCREEN_GESCHUETZT%20(2).pdf
. Accessed 15 Jan 2022."); Rakova et al., [2021](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR63 "Rakova, B., Yang, J., Cramer, H., & Chowdhury, R. (2021). Where responsible AI meets reality: Practitioner perspectives on enablers for shifting organizational practices. Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 5(CSCW1), 1–23.")). Our findings provide empirical evidence on how experts within organizations perceive the enactment of RAI principles by revealing the contextual rationales and organizational trade-offs that explain why principles are present, absent, or treated as regardless in daily practices. Building upon these insights, we propose the following:

### Proposition 1. The Principles of Accountability and Safety-Reliability are Pivotal for RAI Enactment

This proposition suggests that accountability mechanisms and mechanisms to prevent failures and accidents are consistently necessary across configurations for high RAI enactment. Thus, effective harmonization of accountability and safety-reliability with other principles is essential for RAI enactment.

Furthermore, our study reveals that the comprehensive enactment of all RAI principles isn't necessary. Reviewing the five distinct configurations identified, we see that high RAI enactment is achievable even when certain principles are absent. For instance, high enactment was observed even when omitting security, privacy, or benevolence and non-maleficence, provided other principles were present. These findings suggest that RAI enactment is less about individual principles and more about achieving a specific configuration of several principles within specific contexts, supporting a more holistic and nuanced understanding of RAI. This aligns with earlier arguments from the literature indicating that the impact of individual RAI principles on RAI enactment is multifaceted and context-dependent (Floridi & Cowls, [2022](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR18 "Floridi, L., & Cowls, J. (2022). A unified framework of five principles for AI in society. In S. Carta (Ed.), Machine Learning and the City (pp. 535–545). Wiley.")). Based on this, we propose the following:

### Proposition 2. Context-Dependent Configurations of Principles Drives RAI Enactment

This proposition suggests that RAI enactment depends on specific contextual configurations, not on the comprehensive enactment of each and every individual principle. Effective RAI enactment arises from diverse configurations of principles, rather than a single optimal configuration, instead, multiple effective configurations exist.

The findings also show that RAI enactment can be achieved without enacting the principles of transparency and meaningful explanations when the potential for confusion, privacy/security risks, or legal non-compliance outweighs their benefits. While most RAI principles, such as fairness, accountability, and safety-reliability, consistently contribute to a stronger RAI enactment, there can be instances where transparency and the provision of meaningful explanations may threaten the privacy or security of users and organizations or raise legal concerns. In such cases, the presence of these two principles, in conjunction with the others, may not necessarily lead to a higher RAI enactment. For example, some explanations could potentially expose sensitive user data or reveal vulnerabilities that could be exploited. Similarly, complete transparency about the data sources and algorithmic processes used may not be feasible if they involve proprietary information or raise legal compliance issues (Watson & Nations, [2019](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR77 "Watson, H., & Nations, C. (2019). Addressing the growing need for algorithmic transparency. Communications of the Association for Information Systems, 45(1), 26.")). Therefore, we propose the following:

### Proposition 3: Balancing Transparency and Explanations with Liberty Protection is Inherent in RAI Enactment

This proposition suggests that organizations need to carefully balance the implementation of RAI principles, recognizing that the presence of all principles may not always result in the desired outcome of RAI enactment. This seemingly counterintuitive finding can also be explained from a Rawlsian perspective: mechanisms must first secure basic liberties, and in that sense, security and privacy can be prioritized over transparency and explanations. This balance, and how it is exercised, is guided by specific contexts, i.e., how the balance is achieved may be different in one context compared to another. This observation ties third proposition to the second proposition by further acknowledging the need to choose the manner in which different principles will be operationalized and rolled out in the organization.

### 5.2 Practical Implications

A key challenge for enacting RAI lies in bridging the divide between abstract principles and practical application (Mikalef, [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR46 "Mikalef, P. (2024). Responsible AI governance: From ideation to implementation. In I. Constantiou, M. P. Joshi, & M. Stelmaszak (Eds.), Research Handbook on Artificial Intelligence and Decision Making in Organizations (pp. 126–142). Edward Elgar Publishing."); Mittelstadt, [2019](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR49 "Mittelstadt, B. (2019). Principles alone cannot guarantee ethical AI. Nature Machine Intelligence, 1(11), 501–507.")). This study provides actionable insights for organizations striving to effectively enact RAI. First, the findings highlight the central role of accountability and safety-reliability in all successful configurations of RAI enactment. Organizations should prioritize these principles as foundational components of their AI governance frameworks, ensuring clear accountability mechanisms and robust reliability measures to build trust and mitigate AI risks. Second, the study shows that RAI enactment does not require implementing all principles with equal emphasis and uniformly. Depending on the context, certain principles (such as privacy, fairness, or explainability) may take precedence, while others may be less critical or even counterproductive in specific settings. This insight aligns with the recent ([2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR2 "AI4Peopl.e (2024). Towards an ethics by design approach for AI. 
https://ai4people.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Towards-an-Ethics-by-Design-Approach-for-AI.pdf
. Accessed 20 Sept 2024.")) guidance issued by AI4People which states that lists of principles should always be tailored to reflect the context of use as it is not uncommon for some principles to have higher priority in certain domains than in others (AI4People, [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR2 "AI4Peopl.e (2024). Towards an ethics by design approach for AI. 
https://ai4people.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Towards-an-Ethics-by-Design-Approach-for-AI.pdf
. Accessed 20 Sept 2024.")). Drawing from our findings we encourage organizations to adopt a flexible, context-driven approach, tailoring their RAI strategies to industry regulations, and risk profiles. By leveraging context-specific configurations, AI practitioners can develop more effective, adaptable, and ethically sound AI governance frameworks that maximize AI’s benefits while minimizing risks.

The findings also have implications for policymakers. The configurational approach provides insights for RAI policy-making by promoting the development of customizable, risk-based regulatory frameworks. This has also been highlighted in the literature as an important concern (Giffen & Ludwig, [2023](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR28 "Giffen, B., & Ludwig, H. (2023). How boards of directors govern artificial intelligence. MIS Quarterly Executive, 22(4), 7.")). Rather than applying a universal set of rules, this approach demonstrates the value of identifying necessary conditions and understanding how different combinations of principles function across varied contexts. This understanding allows for adaptive regulatory measures that can be tailored to specific industries or applications, focusing on configurations that best mitigate risks while fostering innovation. Regulators can ensure that essential principles are enforced without imposing unnecessary compliance burdens, thereby striking a balance between responsible AI deployment and promoting technological progress. This adaptability is key to creating regulatory systems that evolve alongside the rapid developments in AI technologies.

## 6 Conclusion

This study offers valuable insights into the enactment of responsible AI by identifying five distinct configurations of RAI principles associated with high RAI enactment within organizations. The findings highlight the crucial role of AI accountability and safety-reliability principles across configurations, emphasizing their consistent importance in fostering high RAI enactment. Furthermore, the study demonstrates the contextual variability of RAI principles, showing how their influence and relative importance shift depending on the context. This underscores the need for a nuanced, context-aware approach to RAI implementation, moving beyond generic checklists of principles. The theoretical grounding in Rawls'"justice as fairness"provides a robust framework for understanding the interconnectedness of these principles, linking them to the broader concepts of liberty and equality. From a practical standpoint, our findings suggest that organizations should adopt a flexible, context-driven approach to RAI implementation, focusing on principle configurations that align with their operational and regulatory landscapes. Overall, our study responds to emerging scholarly calls for examining what it means to be responsibly digital providing insights on the enactment of responsible AI in practice (Recker et al., [2023](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR66 "Recker, J., Chatterjee, S., Sundermeier, J., & Tarafdar, M. (2023). Digital responsibility: Social, ethical, and ecological implications of IS. Journal of the Association for Information Systems."); Zamani et al., [2024](https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/article/10.1007/s10796-025-10618-x#ref-CR80 "Zamani, E., Angelopoulos, S., & Conboy, K. (2024). Information systems frontier special issue on: Being responsibly digital. Springer. 
https://link-springer-com.sire.ub.edu/collections/gaihdbdhjg
. Accessed 15 Aug 2024.")).

While the study provides significant contributions to the field, limitations exist, including the specific sample of experts and geographical focus. Future research could expand to include a broader range of organizations and locations beyond Europe and America. Furthermore, our sample consists primarily of organizations with relatively mature RAI practices. This methodological choice has influenced the observed co-occurrence of conditions. Organizations at earlier stages of RAI adoption may exhibit greater variation in their enactment pathways. Expanding the sample to include such organizations can provide additional insights. Further investigation into the specific mechanisms through which configurations lead to RAI enactment would also enrich the understanding of this complex phenomenon. Further studies could also examine longitudinal changes in RAI enactment and investigate how emerging regulations shape the practical adoption of RAI principles. By deepening the empirical investigation of RAI enactment, future research can continue to bridge the gap between ethical AI ideals and real-world RAI enactment.

### Appendix F: Follow-up interview guideline

- **Personal background**

Could you tell us about your academic and professional background?

How long have you been part of the AI projects, and how long have you held your current position?

- **ML and AI projects at your organization**

Could you please describe the main ways your organization utilizes artificial intelligence technologies?

Could you please describe the AI projects that you are involved with?

What kind of AI models are used in these projects?

- **Experience of RAI principles**

How do these projects in which you have been involved do you think are following the responsible AI principles?

1. a)
	**If yes,** describe how you are using them (for what purpose); please explain your experience on how RAI principles are defined in your organization.
2. b)
	**b) If not but planning to employ it,** why are you planning to employ such systems?
3. c)
	**c) If not and no plan to employ it,** why are you not using these principles in AI systems?

Based on your experiences, do you think some RAI principles are more important than others in your case? How do you balance principles like privacy and understandability?

- **Reasons for operationalizing RAI principles in organizations:**

Can you explain the primary motivations behind your organization's decision to operationalize RAI principles?

What specific benefits or goals does your organization aim to achieve by implementing RAI principles?

Have there been any external factors, such as regulatory requirements or societal expectations, influencing your organization's commitment to RAI principles?

- **Implementation and practice in organizations**

How understandable are the decisions of the AI used in the projects you are involved?

Have you encountered a case in which you needed to explain a particular AI decision?

Have these explanations been documented?

What are the practices or producers in cases explanations are requested by the users, could you give concrete examples of these practices?

To what extent do you think the decisions made by the algorithms in your projects are fair?

Have you encountered a case or cases in which the AI decision was unfair?

what were the procedures and practices to identify and solve these issues?

To what extent do you think the decisions made by the algorithms in your projects are following the privacy and security regulations?

Have you encountered a case or cases in which an AI decision put users at risk?

What kinds of risks have you encountered? Could you give concrete examples of them?

What were the procedures and practices to identify and solve these issues?

In your projects, have you encountered situations where RAI principles conflict with each other? For example, how do you handle scenarios where privacy regulations prevent from providing explanations (or other principles)?
