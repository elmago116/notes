Abstract:  
This work presents the link between artificial intelligence (AI) and her meneutics 
through the common interest they have about the study of language and the 
interpretation of texts. Also, it aims for questioning the level of understandi ng that 
a machine has about the meaning of words and propositions of a language. By 
presenting the computational hermeneutics model, the interpretation of digital texts, 
and its techniques, we will observe an analogy between the traditional hermeneutic 
circle of understanding-explaining in philosophy and philology, with the informatics 
process of bootstrapping. We will see that the traditional hermeneutic c ircle is 
renewed into a human-machine collaboration defined as a “hermeneutic network”: 
here, the machine produces a grammatical explanation of the text, and humans 
(philosophers, experts, documentalists, etc.) interpret the results. There is a virtuous 
circle: humans obtain a clarification of the meaning of the text, and the machines are 
learning from the data that humans provide them. We focus on a historical approach 
of the subject in two parts: a first starting from the birth of AI in 1930-1950, with 
the critics it has faced in 1960-1980; a second presenting the emergence of 
computational hermeneutics models from 1985 to 2018. At the end of this work, we 
will present the computer ontologies: AIs able to provide a semantic representation 
of the content of a text usable to apply the hermeneutic network. The challenge will 
be to show how the principles of traditional hermeneutics allow an approach to AI  
that considers the philosophical criticism that it has encountered throughout its 
history. 
Keywords: artificial intelligence – hermeneutics – philosophy of language – semiotics – computer 
ontologies – human-computer interaction

Foreword 
 
The title of this work might be surprising because we propose to link two fields which are, at 
first sight, if not necessarily opposed, at least distant from each other. 
 Hermeneutics has a historical background that presents it as the field of interpretation 
of sacred texts in order to reveal their secrets.  It is based on a principle of interpretation which 
is called the “hermeneutic circle” that is trying to put in relation the universal with the particular. 
This circle takes place between the notions of “explaining” and “understanding”: to understand 
a text we need to explain it, and to explain it we need to understand it. For example, to 
understand the hidden meaning of the sacred texts we need to explain their grammatical 
structure, but to explain this grammatical structure and all of its parts and techniques of writing 
(metaphors, parables, allegories, symbolism) we need to understand the general meaning of the 
sacred texts.  
 During the 19 th and 20 th centuries, hermeneutics will be developed by philosophy 
(particularly phenomenology) and becomes the interpretation of life, existence, and world 
experiences of a subject. Here, the hermeneutic circle aims now to understand the inner thoughts 
and life of a subject by explaining it through an analysis of how it experiences the world around 
him. The circularity takes now into consideration the phenomenological experiences that a 
subject may have about the world and its own existence. Artificial intelligence, however, even 
if its purpose can be highly philosophical, has no real interests in such mystical or metaphysical 
considerations. Its field of action is way more formal and mathematical and seeks objectivity 
where hermeneutics put subjectivity in a central position. To say it briefly the opposition is the 
following: one, hermeneutics, is looking for intentional subjectivity far away from any logical 
formalism, and on the other hand, AI is looking for mathematical objectivity far away from any 
psychological bias.  
 What both could have in common then? At least, we can state that hermeneutics and AI 
have texts and interests in the problem of language in common. We will see that this sharing is 
enough to combine some principles in order to focus on the definition of what “understanding” 
could mean for humans and machines. In this sense, the purpose of this work aims to present 
how the principles of hermeneutics focused on the grammatical analysis of a text and the 
Natural Language Processing (NLP) of artificial intelligence can match in order to clarify the 
fields of science.  

 Indeed, there is since the end of the 20 th century and the beginning of the 21 st century 
new models of interpretation of texts using a collaboration of humans and machines which are 
called “computational hermeneutic models”. These computational hermeneutics models aim to 
support humans’ text interpretations by their power of analysis of the content of a text. With a 
correct formalization of this content, humans are then able to realize a more efficient 
interpretation of the different meanings a text could have or speculate on the intentions and the 
thoughts of the authors. For this, computational hermeneutics models are aiming to renew the 
hermeneutic circularity of explaining-understanding with the analogy of the informatics process 
of bootstrapping.  As a common expression, to “pull oneself up by one’s bootstraps” refers to 
an impossible task to do, or to something redundant and absurd. But it also means to be able to 
analyze oneself and re solve one’s problems by unaided efforts. This expression can be 
illustrated by a metaphor with “ The Surprising Adventures of Baron Münchausen ” of Rudolf 
Erich Rasp, where the Baron Münchausen saves himself and his horse out of a swamp by pulling 
on his own hair. 
 In computer sciences, bootstrapping also implies a circularity: it is a self-starting process 
without external input where a smaller software launches a larger program, which loads the 
exploitation system of a computer, and so on. This phenomenon is when an operating system 
is able by its own effort to start a chain of events. The analogy takes place here because both, 
hermeneutic circle and bootstrapping, aim to be a virtuous circle in which one can improve its 
own understanding of a problem and find a sufficient explanation in order to resolve it. The 
main purpose here is to apply a method that involves both humans and machines in order to 
extract the different meanings of a text, of its grammatical entities, to map the several 
acceptations of a word and the linguistic items. Such grammatical analysis will then provide a 
clarified basis for the interpretation of the text of the expert.  
 The questions that can be asked here: (i) are computational hermeneutics models 
improving the dream of pioneers in AI of an automatic understanding similar to humans? or (ii) 
is the purpose of AI only here is to be used as tools able to support humans’ cognitive faculties 
and texts’ analysis? 
In this work, we are going to defend the second point. We will present it in two parts:  
1) How historically the idea of AI has emerged and why the dream of AGI is not possible 
due to the incapacity of a machine of understanding the contextual uses and meaning of 
words and natural languages.  

2) Why this conclusion implies for AI research to take into consideration some 
philosophical interrogations about the contextual features of a language in linguistics, 
logic, and semiotics. This is here where we will see that we can reinvest the traditional 
hermeneutics in order to improve machine analyzes of texts.  
After presenting AI in general, we will mainly focus on Symbolic AI that makes a syntactic and 
semantic use of signs in order to interpret the propositions of a language. We aim to present 
how from the history of philosophy, anthropology, and linguistics, artificial intelligence has 
been able to provide collaboration between social sciences and formal sciences with 
computational hermeneutics. One of the main tasks of this work is to justify why this model 
could be pertinent for text analysis and NLP, and why it could be an answer to the tr aditional 
seek of a machine which reasons like a human.  
For this, we are going to see that what can reasonably be asked to a machine to 
“understand” is only the grammatical structure of a text and not any feelings, mental states, or 
meaningful living experiences. The answer we will propose may not be poetic and can even be 
disappointing for the one who, like us, is dreaming of such singularity as an AI endowed with 
consciousness. But even a modest tool can be useful for greater purposes, and the one of 
clarifying science and to unify its domains shouldn’t be ashamed in front of the myth of AGI.
