### Abstract

![](https://dl-acm-org.sire.ub.edu/cms/asset/55260e1c-eceb-44ed-9a6a-5e59a938ec73/3514094.3534162.key.jpg)

Word embeddings are numeric representations of meaning derived from word co-occurrence statistics in corpora of human-produced texts. The statistical regularities in language corpora encode well-known social biases into word embeddings (e.g., the word vector for family is closer to the vector women than to men). Although efforts have been made to mitigate bias in word embeddings, with the hope of improving fairness in downstream Natural Language Processing (NLP) applications, these efforts will remain limited until we more deeply understand the multiple (and often subtle) ways that social biases can be reflected in word embeddings. Here, we focus on gender to provide a comprehensive analysis of group-based biases in widely-used static English word embeddings trained on internet corpora (GloVe 2014, fastText 2017). While some previous research has helped uncover biases in specific semantic associations between a group and a target domain (e.g., women - family), using the Single-Category Word Embedding Association Test, we demonstrate the widespread prevalence of gender biases that also show differences in: (1) frequencies of words associated with men versus women; (b) part-of-speech tags in gender-associated words; (c) semantic categories in gender-associated words; and (d) valence, arousal, and dominance in gender-associated words. We leave the analysis of non-binary gender to future work due to the challenges in accurate group representation caused by limitations inherent in data.

First, in terms of word frequency: we find that, of the 1,000 most frequent words in the vocabulary, 77% are more associated with men than women, providing direct evidence of a masculine default in the everyday language of the English-speaking world. Second, turning to parts-of-speech: the top male-associated words are typically verbs (e.g., fight, overpower) while the top female-associated words are typically adjectives and adverbs (e.g., giving, emotionally). Gender biases in embeddings also permeate parts-of-speech. Third, for semantic categories: bottom-up, cluster analyses of the top 1,000 words associated with each gender. The top male-associated concepts include roles and domains of big tech, engineering, religion, sports, and violence; in contrast, the top female-associated concepts are less focused on roles, including, instead, female-specific slurs and sexual content, as well as appearance and kitchen terms. Fourth, using human ratings of word valence, arousal, and dominance from a ~20,000 word lexicon, we find that male-associated words are higher on arousal and dominance, while female-associated words are higher on valence. Ultimately, these findings move the study of gender bias in word embeddings beyond the basic investigation of semantic relationships to also study gender differences in multiple manifestations in text. Given the central role of word embeddings in NLP applications, it is essential to more comprehensively document where biases exist and may remain hidden, allowing them to persist without our awareness throughout large text corpora.

This research provides a comprehensive examination of gender bias in widely-used static word embeddings trained on internet corpora, specifically analyzing GloVe and fastText. The study goes beyond previous research by investigating gender bias across multiple dimensions including word frequency, grammatical structure, semantic content, and psychological dimensions of word meaning.

The analysis reveals that the most frequently occurring words in English embeddings are predominantly associated with men rather than women, demonstrating what researchers term a masculine default in online language. Approximately 77 percent of the top 1,000 most frequent words in GloVe embeddings show stronger association with male attributes, a pattern that persists though diminishes at lower frequency levels. This frequency disparity matters because high-frequency words exert greater influence on downstream language processing applications, potentially amplifying masculine bias throughout systems that rely on these embeddings.

Beyond frequency, the research identifies systematic differences in how men and women are linguistically represented. Male-associated words are predominantly verbs suggesting action and agency, while female-associated words are more often adjectives and adverbs, suggesting that women are described through attributes rather than actions. This linguistic marking reflects and reinforces gender stereotypes about who acts versus who is acted upon.

The study employs unsupervised clustering to identify semantic domains associated with each gender without researcher preconceptions. Male-associated clusters involve professional and technological domains including big tech, engineering, sports, violence, and religion. Female-associated clusters reveal troubling patterns including explicit sexual profanities and adult content, alongside appearance and domestic concepts. Notably, approximately 62 percent of words associated with big tech show male bias, revealing gender imbalance even within the technology industry that creates AI systems.

Examining psychological dimensions of word meaning, the research finds that male-associated words score higher on arousal and dominance scales, conveying activity and control. Female-associated words score higher on valence, reflecting pleasantness but potentially reinforcing passive characterizations. These patterns suggest gender bias permeates fundamental semantic properties of language.

The researchers acknowledge limitations in studying only binary gender representations due to methodological constraints with underrepresented groups. They emphasize that understanding the comprehensive scope of gender bias in word embeddings is essential for developing effective bias mitigation strategies and reducing propagation of harmful stereotypes into downstream applications. The findings highlight how social biases become embedded in the statistical patterns of human-generated text and subsequently encoded into machine learning systems, requiring deliberate intervention to address.
