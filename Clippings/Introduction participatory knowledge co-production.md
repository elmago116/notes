## ABSTRACT

This special Forum presents three articles that demonstrate efforts at participatory knowledge co-production. The three experiences were successful in the modest sense that they provided meaningful agency for participants and their knowledge roles, though in different ways. All three articles comment on the participatory turn that emphasizes an increasing role in public participation in scientific and policy data knowledge production and acknowledge that participation can have diverse meanings, contingent on the interests of the groups involved. This can mean a performative participation with coercive means to achieve particular goals with the participants or the groups that they represent; alternatively, participation can mean liberatory approaches that are designed to remove oppressive structures from disenfranchised populations and shift power to them. The cases here add nuance to critiques of power. Two cases demonstrate how participants are molded to conform to institutional power structures, though at least some participants find that shift (and recognition of their contribution) legitimizing and desirable. While each considers the relevance of participation for democracy, they highlight differences in values related to democracy, as regards whether participation is the endpoint value in democracy, or else whether democracy is a larger process towards redistributing power towards achieving equity and justice.

## Introduction

Academic researchers have a long experience in co-producing knowledge with practitioners. This has often aimed to contest the dominant expertise, to shift power imbalances and thus to empower groups of many kinds. These encompass subaltern groups contesting inequities of race, class, gender, etc. In such ways, participatory knowledge co-production has sought to promote societal transformation.

Despite these lofty goals, participatory approaches have frequently been reduced to yet another data-collection technique. Or they have been assimilated and mainstreamed into neoliberal agendas: participation might well be a mask that is not necessarily coincident with democratic traditions. This illusory role has prompted calls to repoliticize participatory knowledge co-production for its original counter-hegemonic, emancipatory aims. Given its organic connections with contemporary social movements, such efforts hold out the possibility of reviving participatory research approaches that are not only counter-hegemonic, but also which might prefigure new forms of social organization and practice that will stem – even turn the tide against – the neoliberal globalization of the past three decades (Jordan and Kapoor, [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 9).

In this forum we pose the following questions:

- How have researchers co-produced knowledge with practitioners to inform their collective action to shift power? For resisting oppressive arrangements and/or building liberatory alternatives?
- How has this process facilitated or strengthened practitioners? As a collective subject of strategic action?
- What internal tensions have arisen between researchers and practitioners? E.g. in scientizing dissent? Or in highlighting the implicit politics of knowledge?
- What have been the strategies to shift epistemic authority and institutional power?
- How have such efforts promoted social learning from outcomes, towards more effective strategies?

The cases here add nuance to critiques of power. Two cases demonstrate how participants are molded to conform to institutional power structures, though at least some participants find that shift (and recognition of their contribution) legitimizing and desirable. While each considers the relevance of participation for democracy, they highlight differences in values related to democracy, as regards whether participation is the endpoint value in democracy, or else whether democracy is a larger process towards redistributing power towards achieving equity and justice.

In passing, we mention that this special Forum had a Call for Papers attracting many more submissions about efforts at knowledge co-production. They emphasized barriers to participatory knowledge co-production, which remained too marginal or elusive for inclusion here. We asked ourselves whether ‘successful’ cases are rare exceptions.

Subsequent sections outline dominant approaches to participatory methods, summarize each contribution to the forum, and finally offer our insights from the collection.

## Participatory methods for knowledge co-production

For participatory knowledge co-production, the literature elaborates two main approaches: Participatory Action Research (PAR) and Community-Based Participatory Research (CBRP). They have different origins. PAR arose from efforts to strengthen a collective subject that learns how to act more effectively for its aims, generally against an oppressive power. CBRP arose in contexts where scientific knowledge – its assumptions or limitations – played a central role in a societal conflict. In practice, CBRP may include aspects of PAR. Next, they are outlined in turn**.**

### Participatory action research (PAR)

Participatory Action Research (PAR) originated in Latin American struggles against the hegemonic power with its everyday neocolonial oppression and exploitation. There the research method has had various forms and names, especially *Investigación Acción Participativa* as an umbrella concept. This aims to ‘transform and re-enchant our plural worlds’, according to the Colombian sociologist Orlando Fals-Borda ([Citation 2001](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). He popularized the aim for a *sociología sentipensante*, integrating ‘reason and love, the body and heart’, as a crucial basis of liberatory knowledge for collective action (Fals Borda, [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 8).

The original methods were adapted elsewhere by researchers bringing social justice perspectives to political-economic-environmental conflicts in diverse contexts. In PAR, participants should be empowered to play the role of ‘change agents.’ Creating conditions for such partnership helps ‘generate a sense of connection that better ensures benefit of the collective good’, initially among participants (Bradbury, [Citation 2010](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 98, 106).

Those aims need not mean a parity of influence within an inquiry process. More modestly and feasibly, the PAR process needs ‘a sufficient degree of inter-dependent collaborative reflection and management, for the research to be genuinely with people, and not about them or on them’ (Heron and Reason, [Citation 2006](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 151). Research ‘with people’ entails a collective subject, e.g. a specific group which plans and implements practical interventions. This role provides the context, aim and incentive to participate in a research process. Although the collective subject needs to have some prior existence, it can become more cohesive and stronger through a PAR process. This generates social learning whereby participants act, interpret outcomes and refine their strategy; they develop new relationships and understandings.

==Such learning may undergo repeated cycles of 4 stages: planning, acting, observing, and reflecting on outcomes==. The sequence helps practitioners to learn collectively from outcomes, as a basis to clarify strategies for the next cycle (Reason and Bradbury, [Citation 2008](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Action researchers plan for cycles of action and reflection and thereby must be reflexive about how change efforts are unfolding, and the impact that our presence (the intervention) is having …. Researchers need to discuss and shape our research question and design with the practitioners (Bradbury, [Citation 2010](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 98).

Here the relevant knowledge encompasses political-economic forces which may facilitate or impede the collective aims, alongside epistemic conflicts over valid knowledge. Beyond discrete stages as above, the PAR process generates an ‘extended epistemology’, which combines experiential, presentational, propositional and practical ways of knowing (Reason and Bradbury, [Citation 2008](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 366). When co-inquirers are working together, there is a dynamic interplay between their actions and their state of being, mediated by intuitively grasping a significant pattern in their current behavior and by conceptually naming the quality it reveals. Once this quality is identified and agreed, the inquirers can negotiate action to enhance or modify it (ibid: 378).

In this sense, the PAR process facilitates a collective subject to be strengthened or reconstructed.

Through PAR, stakeholder networks have generated knowledge contributing to research agendas and empirical knowledge, going beyond the normal scope of academic research. Immersed in intervention contexts, the researcher can identify aspects that were not actively sought or not seen by many participants; this has been a key feature of action research (Huxham, [Citation 2003](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 246). Such inside knowledge has relevance for interventions into policy issues and thus collective reflection on strategies.

### Community-based participatory research (CBRP)

Community-Based Participatory Research (CBRP) initially emerged from environmental health issues, where people’s experiences contradicted official expert claims (Bradbury and Reason, [Citation 2003](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Its roots lie within PAR and the critical pedagogy of Paulo Freire (Wallerstein and Duran, [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). In particular, scientists and community members who have engaged in CBPR have sought to democratize knowledge production in ways that transform research from a top-down, expert-driven process into one of co-learning and co-production. This has entailed infusing local, community-based knowledge with tools and techniques from disciplinary science, often constructively improvising and shifting the research process to better address community-identified concern (Balazs and Morello-Frosch, [Citation 2012](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 9). By acting on science, this engagement process arguably has improved its rigor, relevance and reach (ibid.). This has brought an epistemic shift in what counts as valid knowledge and its producers.

Through a strongly participatory science, affected communities have conducted ‘undone science’, i.e. addressed questions and issues that were neglected by the official science (Hess, [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). ‘Local residents can add great value to environmental health science – both in the kinds of questions and ways of knowing – that increases the understanding of population health in polluted communities. By producing relevant science in contested environments, knowledge justice can address power inequities (Allen, [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Undone science has been done through various strategies – e.g. a triad of lay/local, scientific, and legal experts – as means to overcome the expertise barrier (Arancibia and Motta, [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Using this approach, some activist-researchers have contested the dominant epistemological paradigm that attributes cancer to individual behavior and genetic deficiencies (Zavestocki, [Citation 2004](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

Issues have gone beyond environmental health, encompassing development decisions and pathways, e.g. in technoscientific innovation in renewable energy and genomics. As a temporal obstacle, however, the knowledge necessary to inform participation in decision making often does not exist at the time of decision making. Therefore, procedural justice should include proactive knowledge production to fill in knowledge gaps, and ongoing opportunities for communities to contest the presence of hazards as local knowledge emerges and scientific knowledge changes (Ottinger, [Citation 2013](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). An entry point has been scientific dissent, which has provided a basis for alternative knowledges and their co-production with practitioners; but dissenters have had diverse positionings in relation to the dominant knowledge; these differences matter for practitioners’ roles in contesting power (Moore and Strasser, [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

Beyond acting on science per se, such efforts have sought to shift institutional power and to gain public accountability for its role. Yet such collaborations have experienced tensions between researchers and practitioners, e.g. as regards the use and control of new knowledge, conformity with dominant standards of knowledge, relationships with decision-makers, etc. (Conde and Walter, [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). In some cases, the collaboration between scientists and local groups has scientized dissent, in ways excluding knowledges, cultures and values of communities (Kinchy, [Citation 2010](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Such tensions have hindered a collective subject from developing knowledge-strategies to realize their aims. Such relationships and action strategies have been made more explicit by concepts of Participatory Action Research (PAR).

## Three forum contributions

In the three cases presented here, participation was driven by diverse forms of agency and actors’ aims. These exemplify the different priorities and aspects of PAR and CBRP, sometimes in combination.

In the first case, political protest around the Flint water crisis led to a community-based participatory research (CBPR) inquiry focused on contesting official discourses of resident agency. Its priorities offered the opportunity to advance a shared community narrative of resident agency, autonomy, and collective action. This related knowledge co-production and community priorities for environmental public health with popular education towards collective action, thus linking aspects of CBRP and PAR.

In the patient engagement case, drug developers’ aims drove patient-participants, who foresaw their role in drug development as potentially benefiting future patients. Beyond that interface, the article-writing process developed a knowledge co-production process to understand the relationships. The former instance exemplifies a common limitation in CBPR projects: although achieving knowledge co-production, they often do not directly work towards a direct social intervention that can shift fundamental power structures. The latter case only hints at the power shifting potential of PAR praxis.

In the final case on environmental monitoring, participation by amateur meteorologists was jointly driven by them, by state scientists and by environmental administrators. From their different motives, these groups reached common criteria for including amateurs’ knowledge. Here the focus on knowledge co-production achieved a meaningful impact for amateur meteorologists by incorporating their data within official systems, thus aligning somewhat with PAR. Yet this alignment does not fundamentally transform systems of knowledge legitimization; it exemplifies a disconnect between full inclusion, true liberation, and actor priorities for disrupting power structures. Next let us review each case in turn.

#### Flint water crisis

Carrera et al. describe in their article, ‘Flint community science leadership: co-production of knowledge around environmental and public health action,’ the collaborative efforts of their team, which is comprised of one non-community academic partner and six community scientists (including two community academics). Their work situates a community science collaboration that was emergent from the Flint water crisis (which erupted in 2014). In response to minimizing narratives of community mobilization, their project works to produce a community identified inquiry process that builds from resident expertise, concerns and curiosities. They describe their collaboration, which spanned more than five years, as an effort to align their research process with the critical pedagogical model put forward by Paulo Freire, as interpreted and applied to public health interventions by Wallerstein and Bernstein ([Citation 1988](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Through focus groups, workshops, a mobile application co-development pilot study, and interviews they framed their process as knowledge co-production through a listening, dialogue, action iterative cycle of inquiry. They detail a sustained effort at fostering community-led science, developing community scientists, co-learning about environmental quality and concerns, and producing relevant resources that are meaningful to community members.

This article contributes important insights related to participatory knowledge co-production. First, it offers a different perspective on academic benefits that are traditionally approached as contradictory to community benefits. Specifically, the non-community academic partner (PI) on the project received a career award, produced multiple manuscripts, and received four awards for the work, all of which could imply an extractive relationship. At the same time, the role of the non-community academic partner was recognized in this work due to the project leader’s insistence on shared recognition and intellectual contribution with the team of community scientists. The career award included a community mentor (who is one of the co-authors), all awards were shared as a team, and all articles were co-authored. Rather than academic success as counter to community goals, the community scientists emphasized the importance of cultivating academics who engage in liberation science.

The work models knowledge co-production as an instance of action towards community identified goals. In doing so, it shows how the process produced impacts of information sharing with community members to learn about issues that community members cared about and could use to support decision making related to household water safety. Team members described the work as healing through deep listening to and validation of community concerns. Frequently, academic scientists struggle with understanding community benefit and worry that benefits are strictly programmatic and monetary, which are essential and core to equitable processes of co-inquiry; this work also shows how the processes of co-production of relevant knowledge and fostering humane inquiry are important impacts themselves.

#### Patient engagement

In their article ‘Patient engagement in drug development: dialogically problematizing participation,’ Egher et al. analyse participatory knowledge co-production at two levels: the research project studying the inclusion of expert patients in drug development and the reflexive process of writing the manuscript by the blended team of two academics and two patient experts. In the former lens, they discuss the process involved for patients to become experts about their conditions and to contribute to knowledge co-production in the development of drug interventions related to their conditions. The authors explain that the rationale by government and the pharmaceutical industry for patient inclusion in drug development is partly framed as democratizing drug development, but this democratization occurs only under very specific constraints. Such as, the authors characterize the requirements of becoming an expert patient and what limited kinds of positionalities allow for one to become an expert patient. Additional rationale for patient inclusion is technocratic, arguing that patient involvement leads to more marketable products. Patient participation in drug development allows for developers to increase their profile as responsible social actors and they hope to increase profits by better tailoring products for target consumers/patients. This article highlights the challenges of becoming an expert patient and the lack of awareness or willingness of professional healthcare practitioners and drug developers to change their practices to make drug development accessible to patients. Rather, patients are included only when they can perform in the institutionally recognized ways, while the institutions themselves experience no meaningful transformation.

In writing the paper, the co-authors reflect on what it takes for patients to become expert patients: having a certain level of health to participate, learning the language and professional acculturation of the healthcare industry, and having the time to learn and commit to being involved in the research and development process. Their reflexive approach to developing the paper considered the processes through which the data was collected (by one of the academics), interpreted and negotiated by the collective group. This navigation left the academic who collected the data to confront their positionality in sharing and defending the data with the group. Within the team, the academic partners sought to engage in knowledge co-production with patient experts so as to minimize extraction and promote inclusion beyond consultation by offering co-authorship to expert patient experts.

The expert patients participated to increase patient involvement in healthcare knowledge production and engage in the process of democratizing knowledge. In doing so, the expert patient co-authors learned about their own privilege as expert patients. From the co-writing process the team gained insights about differences between lay and expert patients. As they recognized, the distinction between the lived experiences and constraints of lay and expert patients may not achieve the goal of delivering better targeted drugs, given the narrow selection bias for who can become an expert patient. The authors question the real democratizing potential of expert patient inclusion in drug development. As co-author Tamas stated, ‘It is unlikely that real equity and equality can be achieved unless there is a strong global movement or organization behind patient education and empowerment efforts.’

#### Environmental monitoring platform

Finally, in ‘The ‘obligatory passage point’ in knowledge co-production: Italy’s participatory environmental monitoring platform,’ Minniti and Magaudda describe the case of scientists and public institutions working with amateur and practitioner meteorologists to develop a process for incorporating amateur collected meteorological data into official data and governance. As the authors describe, citizen participation in environmental monitoring is increasingly accepted as a means for expanding low-cost data collection, but there remains a validity issue in legitimizing publicly collected data. With declining central funds for environmental monitoring, Italy had several civil meteorological networks that could contribute to real time updates of weather events and exchange of data between citizens and institutions.

The authors detail the process that led to the successful collaboration between the state, scientists, and amateur meteorologists and the validation of amateur data. Subsequently, the legitimization of amateur data resulted in the development of the MISTRAL portal, which today allows users to better manage their personal weather data collections and integrate that into the larger meteorological data networks. The authors identify this as an instance of epistemic inclusion that allowed non-scientists to contribute to environmental monitoring data that is used to inform environmental governance in Italy.

Minniti and Magaudda apply Actor Network Theory to the case by drawing on Michael Callon’s translation theory. The authors understand knowledge co-production in meteorology through the lens of an obligatory passage point which allows for the translation and alignment of different actors’ perspectives. The authors describe the alignment of different interests across disparate actors through 15 interviews of stakeholders in three interest groups: Italian state environmental protection agencies (ARPA), who were interested in reducing operating costs; academic environmental scientists (Cineca), who wanted to expand a communication infrastructure affordably using reliable data; and amateur meteorologists (MeteoNetwork), who hoped to legitimize their meteorological activities, automate data acquisition, and standardize data quality control.

Using Callon’s translation theory to study the network of meteorological actors, the authors examine how knowledge is negotiated and what convergences must happen for non-institutional actors to make contributions for formal meteorological knowledge. They identify tensions around judging publicly collected scientific data as reliable and reconciling different terminologies. The authors argue that an obligatory passage point is a helpful frame for understanding how amateur data could be accepted as reliable and therefore legitimate data to inform environmental decision making.

## Conclusion

In this Forum the word ‘participation’ appears across all the various stakeholders’ efforts. The contributions indicate the importance of recognizing its plasticity and roles: participants should be clear about how they use the term in relation to other participants’ aims, so as to ensure alignment of goals and priorities. This is particularly true where powerful stakeholders interpret and shape the term ‘participation’ with practical consequences for vulnerable subaltern groups.

Does participation help to transform power relationships? Does participatory knowledge co-production play a role which is liberatory, co-optive, or even coercive? As these three cases show, successfully achieving knowledge co-production may produce diverse outcomes. In the paper by Minniti and Magaudda, participatory knowledge co-production did serve as a mechanism for further aligning participants with power structures, although amateur meteorologists saw this as favorably legitimizing and recognizing their work. The article by Egher at el., comparing two contexts, describes two outcomes, indicating trade-offs under different conditions. Finally, Carrera et al. highlight the liberatory potential of participatory knowledge co-production through a relatively horizontal partnership contesting the dominant knowledge.

Together these cases help push the debate beyond the above questions. They illustrate how even projects that may have successfully achieved knowledge co-production all lie on a spectrum of potential outcomes ranging from retrenching to transforming existing power structures. We can ask: Under what conditions does participatory knowledge co-production play specific roles, perhaps even contradictory ones? What shapes the outcomes? What do these patterns mean for democratization? And, finally, under what conditions do democratic processes genuinely lead to undoing systems of oppression?
