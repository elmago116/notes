## Abstract

This study aims to identify engagement profiles in the citizen humanities and assess whether these profiles match those found in prior studies on crowd-based projects in the natural sciences. To this purpose, we use the log data from the citizen humanities project ‘Historical Database Suriname and the Caribbean’, in which volunteers transcribe the civil records of Suriname, and analyze the differences between engagement in data entry and forum activity. We identify seven engagement profiles, six of which are similar to the profiles found in crowd projects in the natural sciences. However, their pattern of occurrence differs with a more equal distribution of effort. Additionally, we discuss implications for project design and recommend choices that match project goals and foster engagement diversity.

## Introduction

The application of crowd science, or online citizen science, in scientific research projects of different disciplines is increasing (Sauermann and Franzoni [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Strasser et al. [Citation 2023](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). This approach to conducting research entails the online participation of citizens in research projects that are usually initiated by professional scientists. Citizen science has become a method to gain access to previously unattainable forms and collections of data. Moreover, online citizen science can offer an efficient approach to engaging a considerable number of people in scientific research, tapping into their existing knowledge and skills, while simultaneously facilitating dissemination and raising awareness about the topic of research (Nov, Arazy, and Anderson [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Troll et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

However, in practice it is difficult to get a citizen science project to run smoothly. Capturing the interest of citizens to participate in citizen science and keeping them engaged for the duration of a lengthy project are important challenges for project organizers (Frensley et al. [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); West and Pateman [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). The citizen science literature provides recommendations for recruitment and engagement strategies (Crall et al. [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); West and Pateman [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). The most important recommendation is taking into account the different motivations of citizen volunteers and using engagement strategies accordingly (Fraisl et al. [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 5; Ponciano and Brasileiro [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

Understanding motivations is particularly important when designing a citizen science project (Fraisl et al. [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), yet grasping participants’ engagement behaviors throughout a project is equally vital to inspire new strategies for sustaining their engagement (Ponciano and Brasileiro [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Therefore, we follow prior studies that have measured volunteers’ engagement in terms of their actual participation behavior, examined participation patterns and used these to create different engagement profiles (Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Jackson et al. [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Ponciano and Brasileiro [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). So far, engagement profiles derive from participation in projects in the fields of astronomy and natural sciences, in which the most common task is the annotation of images. In other disciplines, tasks may differ and lead to different patterns of engagement. In order to examine the generalizability of prior findings (Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Ponciano and Brasileiro [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), we focus on the engagement patterns in the citizen humanities.

Citizen humanities refers to the collaboration between citizens and academics in humanities research projects, i.e., citizen science applied to the humanities (Heinisch et al. [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Tasks carried out by citizens in such projects may be simple like tagging and classifying images, but also more time-consuming and knowledge-intensive, requiring a specific set of skills and interests, such as the transcription and annotation of handwritten historical manuscripts (Prats López et al. [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Some well-known examples of citizen humanities projects are *Transcribe Bentham* [Footnote <sup>1</sup>](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#) and *Old Weather.[Footnote <sup>2</sup>](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)*

The aim of our research is to study the participation patterns in the citizen humanities and to create engagement profiles that can inspire the design of new and more tailored engagement strategies for the citizen humanities. Specifically we want to answer the following research questions:

1. *In what kind of activities do participants engage in a citizen humanities project?*
2. *Which engagement profiles can we discern within a citizen humanities project?*

To this purpose, we make use of the log data on transcriptions and forum activity of the citizen humanities project ‘Historical Database of Suriname and the Caribbean’ (Mourits, Van Oort, and Van Galen [Citation 2023](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). The objective of this project is to create a database of the population of the former Dutch colonies of Suriname and the Netherlands Antilles from the years 1830 to 1950, by digitizing and transcribing civil registers, such as birth, marriage, and death certificates (Van Galen et al. [Citation 2023](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). This database will be open access available to both academics and the public in general to facilitate genealogical research, educational and cultural projects and the study of social processes and diversity in colonial society (Van Galen, Mourits, and Rosenbaum-Feldbrügge [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Langefeld et al. [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Rosenbaum-Feldbrügge et al. [Citation 2023](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

Our study examines the patterns of engagement of participants in the transcription project behind the creation of this historical database. We measure the degree and duration of participation in the project by focusing on two main activities: transcription of civil registers and forum interactions. These two activities require different levels of involvement and time investment. As a result, we are able to identify different profiles based on the degree, duration and type of activity, and we compare them with profiles developed in studies in the natural sciences. The identification of profiles allows us to develop more specific recommendations for engagement strategies than would be possible if we only evaluated the activity of the project as a whole. In the discussion, we reflect on the implications of using engagement profiles to develop more targeted engagement opportunities, as well as acknowledge the importance of every profile contribution.

## Defining and measuring engagement

One essential factor for the success of crowdsourcing and citizen science is the long-term engagement of citizens contributing to a project (Johnson and Liew [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Rosenblatt et al. [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Engagement refers to a cognitive and emotional state of mind, with its behavioral manifestation involving some degree of effort and immersion in performing an activity over a period of time (de Moor et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); de Vreede, de Vreede, and Reiter-Palmon [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), de Vreede et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). In the crowdsourcing literature, engagement has been conceptualized as an iterative process in which participants evaluate their experience or interaction with the crowdsourcer, the platform, task and materials, and other participants (Troll et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Positive experiences during a project are likely to lead to engagement as a state of mind, and to continued contributions as the behavioral expression of such engagement (Troll et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Therefore, from a behavioral perspective, we can see engagement as the level of contribution, contact, action and participation that individuals show in a particular context (Johnston [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

In the context of citizen science, engagement is not limited to the time an individual remains connected to a project, or to the amount of data contributed. It also includes other ways of participating, such as communicating with other participants, participating in training sessions, talking to friends and family about the project, and recruiting and training new participants (Phillips et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). These other forms of participation may also be relevant for increasing the general public’s knowledge on the topic of research.

Moreover, crowd science typically has a skewed distribution of contributions, most of which coming from a small group of citizen scientists. Furthermore, the vast majority of participants only engage briefly in a project and contribute sparingly (Benyei et al. [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Fischer, Cho, and Storksdieck [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Sauermann and Franzoni [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Therefore, it is important to ensure that participants remain engaged in a project until its completion. In recent years, researchers have proposed strategies for recruiting and keeping citizens engaged (Crall et al. [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); West and Pateman [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Van Galen [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). However, given the multiple motivations of citizens to participate (Nov, Arazy, and Anderson [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) and the skewed distribution of contributions, these strategies will not be equally effective for everyone. Understanding engagement profiles of participants can help tailor such strategies taking into account different types of contributors.

### Measuring engagement

One way to study engagement profiles in citizen science is to use participants’ self-reported information to classify or cluster them into groups. For instance, Fischer, Cho, and Storksdieck ([Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) surveyed participants of the NASA GLOBE Observer program, inquiring both their frequency and rate of contribution to the program, i.e., their total number of contributions and the time since their last contribution. Using these data, the authors grouped them into five profiles, from ‘initial droppers’ who never reported an observation to ‘hooked participants’ who reported several and had been active in the last six months. Similarly, in projects where recreation and citizen science intersect, such as eBird, participants have been categorized as ‘occasional’, ‘moderate’ and ‘active’ based on a survey including questions about behavior, motivations and specialization dimensions (Rosenblatt et al. [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). The downside of clustering participants based on self-reported engagement data is that it is susceptible to biases, such as recall bias, where respondents may struggle to remember their exact past behavior (Osborn and Matlock [Citation 2010](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). One way to avoid this issue is to examine the patterns of participants’ actual behavior by using the log data of their online activity in a project.

Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) defined four quantitative metrics to identify engagement patterns and create different types of profiles. These metrics indicate the differences in intensity and length of participation in a citizen science project. **Relative activity duration** refers to the ratio between the days a participant is in the project and the total number of days he/she could participate until the end of the project. **Activity ratio** is a ratio between the days a participant is active (i.e., makes at least one contribution) and the total number of days the participant was connected to the project. **Variation in periodicity** is the standard deviation of the time between consecutive active days, where the lower the value the more regular participation is. **Daily devoted time** refers to the average number of hours a participant spends on a task in his/her active days. This measure can only apply when the log data has a high level of granularity, that is, when the system tracks every minute or step of a participant’s activity.

A few studies have used these metrics to explore engagement in other citizen science projects. Drawing on data from several natural history projects, Boakes et al. ([Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) used three of the same metrics as Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) study, specifically activity ratio, relative activity duration and variation in periodicity, while also incorporating the total **number of observations** submitted by a volunteer. They also included an analysis on the spatial distribution of volunteers, linking the online and ‘in the field’ activities of biodiversity citizen scientists. A further difference in their approach is that, while Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) only included volunteers that had been active in a project for at least two days, Boakes et al. ([Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) also included those active for only one day.

Both of these aforementioned studies specifically focussed on engagement in project tasks, like annotating images or contributing observations. Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples ([Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) expanded prior research on engagement by taking into account a broader set of participants’ activities, namely: performing a task, posting and commenting on a forum. They also added a new measure (lurking ratio) and collected survey data on motivations and attitudes to help characterize engagement profiles. They defined the **lurking ratio** as the proportion of days that participants visit a project without contributing. However, they only included participants who had contributed on at least two days.

Recently, de Moor et al. ([Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) have applied the measures proposed by Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) and Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples ([Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) on a citizen humanities project to study the dynamics of engagement over time rather than the identification of engagement profiles. In our research, we concentrate on the quantitative metrics that can be applied using the available log data from the project ‘Historical Database of Suriname and the Caribbean’. These are activity duration, activity ratio, periodicity and, to some extent, daily devoted time.

### Engagement profiles

The development of engagement profiles is a helpful strategy when aiming to understand different ways in which people engage in projects. Clustering participants into a finite set of profiles strikes a helpful balance between seeing all participants as the same, which is never the case, and trying to personalize engagement to each individual volunteer, which is not feasible for large online projects. Engagement profiles thus offer a helpful middle ground in the pursuit to make engagement strategies more targeted.

Based on the metrics described above, Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), Boakes et al. ([Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) and Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples ([Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) identified several engagement profiles in citizen science. Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) found five profiles and proposed that, to determine whether these profiles are generic, the same metrics should be applied to identify profiles in other projects. Boakes et al. ([Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) and Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples ([Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) applied the same metrics and were able to identify three and five profiles, respectively.

Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples ([Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) described two profiles of people who visited without making an active contribution: the lurker and the visitor. The ‘Lurker’ has a high relative activity duration and lurking ratio, combined with low variation in periodicity and low activity ratio. Participants in this profile engage for quite a while and visit regularly, yet they usually visit without contributing. The ‘Visitor’ rarely contributes at all and has a very short relative activity duration, meaning they do not engage for a long time. This group also has the largest lurking ratio (0.5), showing that on half of their visits they do not make a contribution. Interestingly, the majority of respondents in the study of Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples ([Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) fell into this profile, especially newer members, which is why they later distinguished three sub-categories (active, hesitant and observing visitors). The ‘Dabblers’ profile in Boakes et al. ([Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) bears some similarities to ‘Visitors’ in the sense that they made up the largest group in their respective studies and have a low relative activity duration, thus they are not active for a long time. Dabblers’ high variation in periodicity means they are irregular contributors, but their high activity ratio indicates that they contribute often during the time they engage with the project. While these individuals made up the largest group, they contributed the lowest total number of observations.

‘Moderate’ is a label used by Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) for project participants with intermediate values for each of the metrics used in the analysis. These individuals formed the largest participant segment in the projects studied by Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), at around 30%. Boakes et al. ([Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) used the term ‘Steady’ to describe a similar moderate engagement profile.

Two profiles that describe volunteers who contribute substantially but who only engage with the project for a short period of time are: the hardworking and the spasmodic. ‘Hardworking’ (Ponciano and Brasileiro [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) describes participants with a very high activity ratio, low relative activity duration and low variation in periodicity. Participants in this profile do not engage with the project for an extended period, yet during this time, they contribute regularly and at a high volume. ‘Spasmodic’ engagement (Ponciano and Brasileiro [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) is similar to ‘Hardworking’, in the sense that participants stay in the project for a limited time but contribute often. The difference is that ‘Spasmodic’ participants have a lower activity ratio and a somewhat higher average variation in periodicity. In other words, they have a more irregular activity pattern.

An opposite pattern involves people remaining active for a long time but visiting irregularly and only occasionally making active contributions. ‘Lasting’ engagement (Ponciano and Brasileiro [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) is characterized by low activity ratio, moderate variation in periodicity and high relative activity duration. They are attached to the project for a long period but only contribute for a relatively small number of days. ‘Persistent’ engagement has similar low activity ratio, but a higher relative activity duration and variation in periodicity. These participants stay involved in the project for a long time, though most days without making an active contribution. However, they are active when they do visit the project, as indicated by their moderately low lurking ratio (Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). In Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) research, the ‘Persistent’ group was relatively small (around 15% of volunteers) but responsible for most of the contributions. The ‘Enthusiast’ profile is very similar to the ‘Persistent’: low activity ratio, high relative activity duration and high variation in periodicity. Despite being the smallest group (between 1% and 4% of volunteers), ‘Enthusiasts’ were responsible for a significant number of contributions to each of the three projects in Boakes et al. ([Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

Finally, the ‘Loyal’ profile (Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) is quite different from other profiles. It features a very high relative activity duration with low variation in periodicity and intermediate activity ratio. In other words, these volunteers engage regularly over a long period, contribute moderately often and their low lurking ratio indicates that whenever they visit they usually contribute to project tasks.

### Our contribution

The studies discussed above demonstrate the applicability of engagement metrics and provide different engagement profiles. However, it is still too early to determine whether these profiles are generic for citizen science, as only three of them have been recognized in more than one study. Additionally, these engagement profiles have been derived from projects in the fields of astronomy, biology and meteorology (Heinisch et al. [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Tauginienė et al. [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), which involve relatively straightforward tasks. We do not yet know if the same types of profiles emerge in projects with more complex tasks, or whether these profiles may be generalizable to the citizen humanities.

Historical citizen humanities projects involve tasks that are time-consuming and require less common knowledge and skills, such as annotating and transcribing old handwritten manuscripts, which are difficult to decipher and interpret (Prats López et al. [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). At the time of writing, and to the best of our knowledge, no study has identified the engagement profiles present in a citizen humanities project. In the remainder of this manuscript, we examine the patterns of engagement in a citizen humanities project. We also assess whether previously described profiles can be identified in this field of research.

## Methods

To test the applicability of existing engagement metrics on a citizen humanities project and to examine whether we find engagement patterns that do not fit the profiles listed in , we selected an active citizen humanities project coordinated by the Radboud University in the Netherlands. It concerns a transcription project in which information from Civil Registers from Suriname are entered in an online form. The project’s log data cover the period from the project launch in September 2021 until the completion of the first batch of birth certificates in February 2022, of which the resulting database was published on the website of the National Archives Suriname (Rosenbaum-Feldbrügge et al. [Citation 2023](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

### Project description: historical Database Suriname and the Caribbean

The Historical Database of Suriname and the Caribbean (HDSC) reconstructs the life courses of inhabitants of Suriname and the former Netherlands Antilles between 1830 and 1950. Its goal is to create databases of historical records of former Dutch colonies in the Caribbean that will be available in open access to the wider public and scholars. During our research period, the HDSC was digitizing the complete civil registry of Suriname, which consists of over 350,000 birth, marriage and death certificates in total. The researched project is a follow-up of an earlier citizen humanities project that resulted in the transcription of the Surinamese slave registers (Van Galen and Hassankhan [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Van Galen [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

Project participants were recruited in various ways: through partnerships with relevant organizations such as the Foundation for Surinamese Genealogy (SSH), *via* wide publicity in Dutch and Surinamese media, social media, and through a series of online and on-site events. To avoid privacy infringements, we collected no personal information of participants such as gender, age, address or ethnicity. Nonetheless, after several years of interactions between participants and project coordinators, it is clear that the volunteers are a mixed group in terms of ethnicity: about half of them have their family roots in Suriname or the Dutch Caribbean. The majority of participants reside in the Netherlands, while those from Suriname and the Caribbean islands constitute a smaller group. One should take into account that Suriname has a population of approximately 600,000 and Curaçao around 150,000, compared to nearly 18 million inhabitants in the Netherlands. In general, participants are over fifty years of age and more often female than male.

In order to participate, volunteers needed to register on the Dutch citizen science platform *Het Volk [Footnote <sup>3</sup>](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#) \[The Crowd\]*, where the transcriptions took place. Since this platform lacked forum functionality, a separate website called *Ning* [Footnote <sup>4</sup>](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#) was used. New participants were directed to this website to sign up for the project. Manuals and support material were also provided *via Ning*. Although the forum could be accessed without an account, registration was required in order to post messages. Communication with the community mainly took place through a weekly newsletter and the forum. This forum also served as a medium for volunteers to communicate with each other.

For this study, we focus on the data entry of the Surinamese birth certificates. The platform displayed a scan of a birth certificate and a form with all the fields for volunteers to transcribe (). Though participants were not required to have specific skills to participate, except for the ability to read Dutch, some participants possessed or developed capabilities for deciphering difficult-to-read handwriting and records on degraded paper. Two randomly assigned citizen participants transcribed each certificate separately. Transcription was actively monitored by the HDSC academic team to guarantee data quality. After all certificates had been transcribed twice, a group of about 20 participants, whom we call controllers, checked all the fields that showed differences between the two transcriptions. These controllers were selected based on the volume and quality of their previous data entry work.

### Data description

The dataset includes anonymized metadata from *Het Volk* and *Ning* (Mourits, Van Oort, and Van Galen [Citation 2023](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). At the start of the project, the HDSC data manager prepared the dataset by omitting all transcription tasks and forum activity of the HDSC team, and then matching user accounts between both platforms. Accounts were matched *via* the user name and/or e-mail address and then manually checked in case the username or e-mail address varied between *Ning* and *Het Volk*. The data were then anonymized by removing user names, e-mail addresses, location information and other personal information. For this study, we had access to the following metadata for each data entry on *Het Volk*: date, time, batch, and anonymized user. For forum entries on *Ning*, we collected the date, time, post, thread, order, and anonymized user. Since we only knew when volunteers completed a task or uploaded a post, we defined sessions as a series of entries with less than 60 min between each consecutive entry. Additionally, we calculated active time per day as the total duration of all users’ sessions on a given day.

There were 660 volunteers in total, of which 489 were only active on *Het Volk*, six were only active on *Ning* and 165 contributed to both platforms. All these volunteers finished at least one data entry on *Het Volk* or made one forum entry on *Ning*. It was not possible to retrieve information about volunteers who registered for *Het Volk* but never completed an entry or for individuals who visited the forum but never entered a post or reply on the forum.

The 654 volunteers on *Het Volk* transcribed 163,840 birth certificates between September 13, 2021 and February 12, 2022, when transcription of the final batch of birth certificates was completed (). On average, each participant skipped, rejected or transcribed 250 certificates. There were however considerable differences in how active volunteers were: 106 people only entered one piece of data, whereas the top transcriber made 7,378 data entries of birth certificates. This is a pattern commonly seen in citizen science projects, in which a small share of the participants is responsible for most of the data entry (de Moor et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Sauermann and Franzoni [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). However, a Gini coefficient of 0.52 shows that data entry was much more equally spread between volunteers than in other citizen science projects, whose Gini coefficients range between 0.77 and 0.91 (Sauermann and Franzoni [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

### Analysis method

Our analysis followed the measures proposed by Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)): activity duration, activity ratio, periodicity and daily devoted time. These variables were centered and scaled to ensure that the cluster analyses were unaffected by differences in the measurement unit, range, and variance between variables. The same measures were applied to analyze forum activity. However, the available data did not allow us to measure the lurking ratio, that is, the passive use of the forum, as suggested by Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples ([Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

*Activity duration* is defined as the number of days between the first and last entry plus one, so that individuals who started and stopped on the same day get a score of 1 rather than 0. In contrast with the literature, we defined the end of the active period by the last entry day rather than the end day of the project. Using the last day of the project would suggest that a fictional volunteer who entered data for 7 days in a row at the start of the project would be active longer than a volunteer who entered data for 7 straight days halfway the project. This would misrepresent the considerable number of volunteers who were active for a limited number of days over the course of the project.![](https://.sire.ub.edu/) ![](https://.sire.ub.edu/)

The *activity ratio* is the number of data entry days divided by the activity duration in days, which provides a score between 0 and 1. A score of 1 indicates that for every day in the activity duration, a volunteer transcribed at least one certificate (data entry) or posted at least one message on the forum (forum entry). The score goes down as the number of inactive days between the first and last entry increases. Again, we defined the end of the active period by the last entry day rather than the end day of the project as suggested by Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). According to their measure, a volunteer who entered data at the beginning of the project would have a lower activity ratio than an individual who entered data later in the project. For example, in a project lasting 92 days, a fictional volunteer who entered data for 7 days in a row at the start of the project would get an activity ratio of 0.076, while a volunteer who joined the project halfway and showed the same behavior would get an activity ratio of 0.152. Following our definition, both volunteers would get an activity ratio of 1 with an activity duration of 7 days, as they entered data on all 7 days that they were active.![](https://.sire.ub.edu/) ![](https://.sire.ub.edu/)

*Periodicity* is the number of inactive days between sessions. We calculated both the mean and standard deviation to measure both how long it took volunteers to return and whether they returned on a regular basis. As shown in appendix and , the variables turned out to be highly similar, having a correlation of 0.88 among the transcribers and 0.84 among forum participants. Therefore, we only used mean periodicity, as we deemed it a more informative and intuitive measurement than the standard deviation. Hence, periodicity reflects the mean number of days that a person was inactive between entry sessions.![](https://.sire.ub.edu/) ![](https://.sire.ub.edu/)

*Daily devoted time* is the mean of the time volunteers spent on *Het Volk* or *Ning* on days when they were active on the respective platform. Our score shows how many minutes an individual spent transcribing civil certificates or posting on the forum. Entry time was approximated by calculating the time between completed entries within a session, as we only knew when volunteers completed their task or uploaded their post.![](https://.sire.ub.edu/) ![](https://.sire.ub.edu/)

Activity duration, activity ratio, periodicity and daily devoted time were calculated for all volunteers who entered more than two transcriptions or entered more than two posts on the forum, as the required statistics cannot be calculated for 1 or 2 entries. However, we consider participants who contributed 1 or 2 entries as important clusters by themselves. For all other volunteers we used the described metrics for a cluster analysis. We used k-means clustering because it is the only clustering method that returned cohesive and intuitive clusters with little overlap.[Footnote <sup>5</sup>](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)

We ran two separate analyses to identify engagement profiles for the data entries and the forum entries. The optimal number of clusters was chosen by comparing the ‘between sum of squares’ to the ‘total sum of squares’ to determine whether adding an extra cluster would explain extra variation in the data or just random noise. We ran k-means clustering algorithm ten times to test whether the results were robust, as the quality of the retrieved clusters is dependent on randomly chosen starting points in the data. When these outcomes produced similar results, we checked whether the retrieved clusters were cohesive, showed little overlap, and made sense theoretically. When all criteria were met, we accepted the suggested number of clusters. Otherwise, we tested whether decreasing the number of clusters by one would yield a better solution.

## Results

Here we report the results of the cluster analysis. First we show the profiles resulting from the analysis on data entry (i.e., transcriptions), then we report on the profiles that emerge from the data on forum participation, and finally we combine the insights.

As we centered and scaled the data, the results show how groups scored relative to one another. A score of 0 means it is close to the average of the population, a negative score below average, and a positive score above average. We translate these numerical results to four labels: low, moderate, high and very high to improve the intelligibility of our results and make them comparable to the overview of the literature in . For example, a group with an activity duration of −1.18 scores low, a group with a score of 0.05 moderate, and a group with a score of 0.87 high.

We report the retrieved engagement profiles with low, medium, high, and very high scores on activity duration, activity ratio, periodicity, and daily devoted time ( and ). The extended results are available in in the appendix. The retrieved profiles are named in line with the discussed profiles in if they have a similar activity duration, activity ratio and periodicity, which is the case for all but one profile. We test the quality of the profiles by looking at the group size, number of data entries and number of forum entries per profile ( and ). The combination of the four statistics of data entry and forum entry results in a set of seven engagement profiles of citizen scientists.

### Data entry: transcription tasks

The size of the profiles resulting from the analysis of transcription tasks are quite close to each other, ranging from 11% to 27% of participants per profile (). Most of the identified profiles closely align with those found in previous research, with the exception of the ‘Occasional’ profile.

The first two profiles are varieties of the ‘visitor’ profile in . The ‘one-time visitor’ and ‘one-day visitor’ profiles consist of people who respectively entered no more than one transcription task, and those who entered more than one task but only for a single day. In other words, these ‘visitors’ do not remain active in the project after a first encounter, although they could still be interested in and following the project, for instance *via* the newsletters.

The ‘Intermittent’ profile accounts for 24% of the participants, exhibit high activity ratios and low periodicity, with a low duration of activity. That is, these participants remain active for a relatively short period, but during this period they are quite productive, have few inactive days, and a steady work rhythm, as they are responsible for 9% of the data entries.

The ‘Consistent’ profile is characterized by high values in the activity duration, activity ratio, and daily devoted time, so these citizen scientists remain active for a long period and during this period spend many days and hours on the project. This profile counts the highest share of participants (27%), and is responsible for 84% of the data entries.

Finally, we have identified a profile that does not fit with the existing types. The ‘Occasional’ contributors deviate to some extent from all existing typologies.[Footnote <sup>6</sup>](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#) This group remains involved for quite a long period, but does not contribute very actively nor very regularly. In the studied project, the ‘Occasional’ are both in size (21% of participants) and in output (6% of the entries) a significant group.

### Forum entry

The results of the analyses of the forum entries ( and ) show more or less the same groups as in the data entry. The main difference is the ‘Spurt’ profile. The ‘Spurt’ and ‘Intermittent’ groups have a similar activity duration and periodicity, but ‘Spurt’ contributors have a slightly higher activity ratio and daily devoted time. We also find a group of ‘Persistent’ contributors who remain active for a long time, but have a low activity ratio and high periodicity.

Furthermore, it is interesting to note that the one-time and one-day visitors of the forum are responsible for a combined 7% of forum entries (). A further 63% of forum entries was created by a small group of ‘Consistent’ contributors that consist of 15% of persons active on the forum.

### Combined insights

In a final step, we combined the data entry profiles and forum activity profiles (). Not surprisingly, ‘Visitors’ that enter data for no more than one day are hardly active on the forum at all. The largest profile identified is the ‘Consistent’ engagement. Participants in this profile remain active in a project for an extended period and contribute the most in terms of data entries and forum posts. Though 51% of ‘Consistent’ data transcribers never posted on the forum, all the most prolific forum contributors belonged to the ‘Consistent’ data entry profile. The ‘Occasional’ engagement profile involves participants who enter data on an irregular basis. Although most of these participants do not actively contribute to forum discussions, they remain involved in the project over an extended period with an average level of activity. The ‘Intermittent’ profile includes people with a relatively short period of engagement but with a considerable level of activity both in terms of data entry and forum posts.

## Discussion, conclusions & Recommendations

The aim of our study was to analyze participation patterns and to create engagement profiles in a citizen humanities project, in order to assess the generalizability of profiles identified in crowd-based natural science projects. We wanted to understand the activities that characterize these profiles, and discuss the implications for new engagement strategies. Therefore, we took into account two types of participation tasks: data entries (i.e., transcriptions) and forum entries.

We have identified seven engagement profiles: One-time visitor, One-day visitor, Intermittent, Spurt, Occasional, Persistent and Consistent. All these profiles, with the exception of the ‘Occasional’, are consistent with the engagement profiles observed in crowd-based natural science projects. However, unlike those projects, our findings indicate that no single profile dominates; instead, the profiles are relatively equal in size. Three of the profiles − the Consistent, the Occasional and the Intermittent − are relatively large, in contrast to typical crowd-based natural science projects, where the ‘Visitors’ group tends to be the largest (Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Sauermann and Franzoni [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

We distinguish four implications from these findings. First, the existence of three large profiles who regularly or irregularly contribute for a period of time indicates a *more equal distribution of effort* in the studied project than we have seen in other projects (de Moor et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Sauermann and Franzoni [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). The identification of these profiles and their particular size may also explain why the Gini coefficients in the studied project are significantly lower than those reported in studies on crowd projects in the natural sciences (Sauermann and Franzoni [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). The Gini coefficient was 0.52 for the number of data entries (i.e., transcribed certificates) and 0.72 for the number of forum entries (i.e., posts). These values are substantially lower than previously reported Gini coefficients for citizen science projects in the natural sciences, which average around 0.85 (Sauermann and Franzoni [Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

Second, our research implies that *all roles matter.* That is, while the data analysis shows that ‘Consistent’ contributors do the bulk of the work in terms of data entries, we believe the project would likely not be nearly as successful without the presence of other engagement profiles. The profiles show that while some participants contribute great amounts of entries (and may additionally play a role in the quality control of others’ contributions), other participants are less active in terms of data entry but are very active on the forum. Moreover, other profiles such as ‘Visitors’ do not contribute much data or forum posts, but they may contribute to the project in other ways, such as mobilizing others in their social networks, increasing the reach of a project by sharing news updates, contributing to fundraising, or helping others follow-up on promising leads. We should always be mindful that citizen science can do more than generate data (Sauermann et al. [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) and thus different ways of engaging should be valued. Overall, developing profiles based on engagement behaviors instead of demographics helps enrich our understanding of the different roles people may take up in citizen science projects.

Given the diverse roles and motivations for participation in a citizen science project, we want to emphasize that *language matters* in both communication with citizens and the scholarly dialogue on citizen science. This requires a critical reflection on the terminology used to describe engagement profiles. The terminology used in prior studies is sometimes normative rather than descriptive, which seems to value some engagement profiles above others. In this manuscript, we offer some updated names for engagement profiles that focus on activity patterns and avoid a language of value judgments.

Finally, knowledge about the variety of engagement profiles in the citizen humanities can support decision making when searching for methods for transcribing historical records. Transcriptions can be performed by citizen scientists or by machine learning tools that focus on training models to increase the amount and speed of transcription. Citizen science is in large part about *community building* (Trouille, Lintott, and Fortson [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Van Galen [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), as the variety of profiles and forum activity observed in our research indicates. Focusing on community building means that citizen science projects cannot process the same volumes of data as machine learning projects. However, community building can create a reciprocal relationship between researchers and the public, where they jointly learn how to interpret the sources by discussing and sharing insights. That is, citizen science creates a level of hermeneutic learning and provenance that machine learning cannot attain. Humans continue to outperform software in interpreting language and symbolism (Korteling et al. [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), particularly when contextual knowledge and reflection are needed (Cibotaru [Citation 2024](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), such as in understanding historical sources and the intentions behind people’s past writings. Therefore, the differences between both methods do not preclude hybrid approaches, where output harvested through machine learning is processed by citizens, or where citizens provide training data used in machine learning applications. Yet, the advantages of machine learning and citizen science do not necessarily reinforce one another or mitigate the disadvantages of either method. For example, citizens might lose interest if they only train machines and sparsely process or discuss historical sources (Ceccaroni et al. [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Lotfian, Ingensand, and Brovelli [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Ponti and Seredko [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Trouille, Lintott, and Fortson [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Hence, the decision to employ either machine learning, citizen science or a combination should be determined by the goals of the project.

### Recommendations for project design

One of the aims of our research was to reflect on how engagement profiles can support a tailored design of engagement strategies in citizen science projects. First, even short, small or irregular contributions can add up to a major contribution. Therefore, we recommend a project design that ensures that data entry tasks are short in terms of required time commitment, such as in simple image annotation. In the case of more complex entry tasks, such as transcriptions, offer the option to save a task and resume it at a later stage. In this way, project design can facilitate the diversity of engagement and lower barriers to participation.

Second, in terms of recruitment, Ponciano and Brasileiro ([Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) argue that different channels (e.g., word-of-mouth or through reaching out to existing online communities) could affect the balance of engagement types in a project. Understanding the engagement profiles in a project can help determine the balance between the different types of engagement among volunteers, and whether this balance matches the goals of the project. For example, if the analysis indicates that a project heavily depends on a small group of dedicated volunteers who are only active for a limited time, this may be suitable for a short-term project but less appropriate for projects seeking to build a community over an extended period.

Data on engagement profiles could also be used to inform strategies to maximize engagement, for instance by motivating participants to contribute more or more frequently. However, when designing engagement strategies, project managers need to carefully consider the effect that these may have on the metrics that characterize each profile (Ponciano and Brasileiro [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Understanding the differences between profiles can help optimize and balance engagement. For instance, if the analysis indicates a project has many volunteers with a ‘Spurt’ profile, strategies to further increase their daily time commitment could be counterproductive; instead, encouraging them to stay in the project longer may be more feasible and effective.

Moreover, given the variation in participants’ activity and periodicity within a project, project staff should strive to be welcoming not just to newly recruited or interested citizen scientists, but also to those who participated in the past and might be willing to rejoin the collective effort. Project managers should thus consider design choices that facilitate rejoining their project. For instance, offering regular online training could lower the barrier to entry not just for new participants, but also for contributors who might have been away for a while and would like to contribute once again. Research on an earlier, related project stressed the relevance of community building instead of competition. The emphasis on all contributions, great and small, increased the ongoing involvement of volunteers in the project (Van Galen [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

### Recommendations for future research

While our study offers more insights into the dynamics of engagement in the citizen humanities, our work also suggests some promising avenues for future research. The analysis of engagement profiles in citizen science is a small but emerging field, and many questions remain to be answered, for instance regarding how different recruitment and retention strategies affect different participant profiles.

Our study combines engagement metrics from both data entry and forum posts. Means of interaction between project participants, such as fora or chat functions, are important social technologies used in projects to turn anonymous online crowds into online communities that motivate commitment (Baudry, Tancoigne, and Strasser [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). However, participants use such social technologies not just for social bonding, but also to exchange knowledge and insights, ask questions and develop research ideas; in other words, actions that are part and parcel of doing research. The measures we used in our study tell us little about the content of social exchanges in the project and the roles that different participants adopt in the community. Some authors offer more detailed analyses of these content dimensions; for example Torres et al. ([Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) used a content analysis of community posts to map out the types of topics participants discussed, such as more biological or more methodological topics. Such in-depth analyses of the content of social interaction in online citizen humanities could be a promising next step for the HDSC as well.

Furthermore, while we included metrics used in previous studies to understand engagement, other relevant metrics could be included in future research. For example, in citizen science projects, data quality assurance and control procedures are an important component. De Moor, Rijpma, and Prats López ([Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) included quantitative metrics of data quality in their study of engagement in an online humanities project. More studies using such metrics would allow for case comparisons of interventions to optimize data quality in citizen humanities projects. Other metrics could be based on demographic data and examine the gender and age composition of each engagement profile across different types of projects.

Although our quantitative metrics offer interesting insights into patterns of engagement, they reveal little about the demographic characteristics, the underlying motivations of participants, or the barriers they might experience to deepen their engagement. For instance, given the similarity between ‘one-time visitors’ and ‘one-day visitors’, the distinction could be deemed unnecessary or qualitative research could shed light on their differences and reasons for such short engagement. We believe that qualitative and quantitative approaches can strengthen each other. That is, patterns of engagement can support sampling for a qualitative follow-up research and qualitative data can help to flesh out engagement profiles and understand why people engage in a certain way (Aristeidou, Scanlon, and Sharples [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#), p. 254), and why their engagement might change over time. In addition, our study offers a snapshot in time; as a project continues it can be valuable to repeat analyses such as these to keep track of how engagement shifts and how participants evolve in their role over time.

Finally, it is important to keep in mind that our study concerns engagement in a single citizen science project. Allf et al. ([Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) caution that much of the literature on citizen science is based on a ‘project-centric’ point of view, i.e., assuming that contributors are not concurrently engaged in other projects. On the contrary, their large-scale study of citizen science showed that only a minority of contributors took part in only one project, and that about half of those taking part in multiple projects did not limit themselves to one discipline either. This raises important implications for interpreting engagement data. For example, a decline in time spent on a project could be interpreted as reduced ability or interest to participate, but it could also mean a shift from engagement in one project to another. We ought to be mindful of the fact that engagement profiles are relative and reflect engagement within a project. Future studies on engagement patterns could follow the approach of Allf et al. ([Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) and track engagement of participants across projects, for example within multi-project platforms.
