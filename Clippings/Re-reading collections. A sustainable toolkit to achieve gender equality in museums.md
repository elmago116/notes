## ABSTRACT

During the last century, collections in heritage institutions have been constructed with heteropatriarchal bias, considering women as desired objects rather than creators, patrons, and artists as active protagonists of historical narratives. Excluding them as exceptions creates an important bias in how culture is approached. Nonetheless, some heritage institutions are eager to transform their sustainability aspirations and commitments into action. In this context, the Re-reading Collections Toolkit was produced to address Sustainable Development Goal 5 (part of UN Agenda 2030) ‘Achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls’. This paper presents the rationale behind the development of such a toolkit and its application in the National Museum of Ceramics (Spain). This paper presents an initiative that aims to pave the way for gender equality in the Cultural Heritage sector at the time and provides credible evidence to demonstrate that heritage collections can contribute significantly and measurably to sustainable development

## 1\. Introduction

In the 1970s, Linda Nochlin asked herself why there had been no great women artists, inaugurating a feminist trend in art history ([Citation 1971](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). In her essay, she excludes the genius theory, which, of course, was used (and is sometimes still used) to refer to male artists and called upon understanding the social context in which art was created, including race and class. Her essay and further studies demonstrate how, during the last century, collections in heritage institutions have been constructed with a patriarchal bias, considering women as desired objects rather than creators, patrons, and artists as active protagonists of historical narratives.

In fact, research has traditionally been conducted by men who intentionally or not excluded many female artists from artistic pages. They appeared as muses, wives, or mistresses or as isolated cases throughout the history of art rather than as a constant. Excluding them as exceptionalities creates an important bias in the way culture is approached. If we ask who invented abstract art, the answer is usually Kandinsky, rather than Hilma af Kint, the real creator of this movement. If we ask about still-life artists, few people will talk about Clara Peteers. If we talk about the Bauhaus, people talk about them; even today, many museums have their cartouches wrong, as in the case of the Barcelona chair, attributed entirely to Mies Van der Rohe when it was designed by his associate, Lily Reich (Bergdoll and Dickerman [Citation 2009](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#))

The fact is that it is not enough to add women’s names to cultural history to achieve equality; it is to deconstruct the roles and stereotypes of femininity and masculinity that are at the origin of the sexual division of labour and are the cause of male domination and structural inequality. Although the glass ceiling has shown some cracks, it remains far from being shattered.

Nonetheless, some heritage institutions are eager to turn their sustainability aspirations and commitments into action as they have the potential to make positive contributions to humanity. In this context, the Re-reading Collections Toolkit was produced to address Sustainable Development Goal 5 ‘Achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls’. This paper presents the rationale behind the development of such toolkit and its application in the National Ceramics Museum (Spain) and provides credible evidence to demonstrate that heritage collections can contribute significantly and measurably to sustainable development.

## 2\. State of the art

Since the famous Guerrilla Girls question ‘Do women have to be naked to get into the Metropolitan Museum?’ to publications and exhibitions with a gender-critical approach (Kosut [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), feminism is slowly trying to incorporate into collection-based organisations (CBOs). These studies have shown how women have been depicted in museums through patriarchal dominations, that is, making men a central figure in the stories told through exhibitions and displays, pushing women to the otherness and exceptions (Bergsdóttir [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), thus generating more inequalities produced by the complexities of the discursive production of cultural practices (Barrett [Citation 1985](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Jones [Citation 2010](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Pollock [Citation 2023](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Woodhull [Citation 2004](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

As Hilde Hein ([Citation 2010](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) suggests, feminist theories and museums can influence each other in positive ways, while Amelia Jones ([Citation 2010](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) stresses that feminism broadens models when analysing gender in culture by including aspects such as ethnicity, race, nationality, class, and sexuality. Along this line, Community-based organisations’ (CBOs) spaces can be used to explore feminist theories by using their collections and objects as case studies and grounds for research, as they should be as plural as possible, including feminist, interracial, and multicultural experiences, excluding the traditional patriarchal canon and including the expressions of the other half of the population.

However, previous research has addressed feminist perspectives from several perspectives. By exhibiting more female artists in their rooms, they actively worked with the collections. Exhibitions have been one of the main actions taken to achieve gender equality. In fact, since the iconic exhibition Women Artists 1500–1955 (1976), curated by Linda Nochlin and Anne Sutherland at the Los Angeles County Museum (Gosling, Robinson, and Tobin [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), there have been numerous exhibitions focusing on women artists as a group or large solo exhibitions; for example, the Prado exhibition *Invitadas* (2020) or the *HERE WE ARE! Women in Design 1900 – Today* (2024, Wien). Nonetheless, these are merely examples that do not represent half of the population, as the 2020 report of WOMarts ([Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) shows the percentage of women solo exhibitions between 2007 and 2014 was less than half compared to male ones. Thus, CBOs urgently need to incorporate female artists in their rooms.

Representation is another way that CBOs can address gender. CBOs have traditionally facilitated the dynamics of patriarchal persistence, where gender, race, and sexual identities usually represent those of the privileged (male, white, and abled). Moreover, as Hein suggests ([Citation 2007](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) the adoption of ‘masterpiece’, ‘scientific breakthrough’ or ‘historic moment’ preserves what should be seen as better perpetuating the dynamics of the privileged ones, which in many cases unfortunately, include the white, straight, middle-class and able-bodied women, excluding other realities in favour of universal womanhood (Hooks [Citation 1981](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#))

In this regard, and related to the research presented in this paper, Bergsdóttir ([Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) refers to diffraction, compelling visitors to use their construction to consider how they see, and how our visions are framed and allow different perspectives. As such, feminism challenges CBOs, their users, and their staff to acknowledge themselves as gendered, not neutral subjects, by de-neutralising and transforming conventional spaces (Hein [Citation 2007](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), questioning their role in gender disparity.

Gender can be used as an analytical tool for collections in CBOs (Cuesta Davignon [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). According to Scott ([Citation 2009](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), gender is an essential aspect of social interactions rooted in the perceived distinctions between males and females, and serves as a reflection of power dynamics. Hence, using gender in collections makes it possible to enquire into the manner and circumstances in which the various roles and duties have been assigned to each sex; how manhood and womanhood change according to history, context, and location; how symbolic structures impact people’s lives and practices; and how sexual behaviour and identities have been shaped according to or in opposition to social norms.

Previous examples of using gender and collections as analytical tools can be found in activities held by the Västergotlands Museum in Sweden. In 2004, launched a project aiming to better register their photographic collection as it reflected conservative, traditional, and gender-normative catalogues, including adding new words to their datasets or changing some, for example, using couples not only for heterosexual partners (Hartig [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Years later, the National Museum of Ceramics of Spain launched a campaign in 2014, where a guided tour was held for students aged between 14 and 18 years with the aim of showing them pre-selected pieces from a gender perspective (Cuesta Davignon [Citation 2014](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

## 3\. Re-reading collections: changing the gender bias

CBOs should act as a reflection of human diversity expressed by tangible and intangible heritage, and how we address these inequalities is crucial for creating a non-gender-biased world. Artistic studies and criticism of the promotion of policies that encourage women entrepreneurs to play a positive role in addressing social equality. 

These issues are aligned with the Sustainable Development Goal 5 (SDG5), whose main objective is to ‘achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls’. The SDGs are part of UN Agenda 2030 which was published in 2015 and adopted by 193 countries worldwide in 2016. In its broadest sense, the 2030 Agenda is ‘a plan of action for people, planet and prosperity’ which aims to achieve sustainable development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. The overarching goals of the 2030 Agenda and the SDGs that underpin it can be summarised in the ‘5 Ps’: people, prosperity, peace, planet and partnership (Tremblay et al. [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). In this complex puzzle, true sustainability has all five dimensions within which heritage collections have much to contribute. Many CBOs are already working to address these challenges (Loddo et al. [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); McGhie [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Pereira Roders and Van Oers [Citation 2011](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Petti, Trillo, and Makore [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). From acting directly in the ‘container’ (the building) to the ‘content’ (the collection) or their users these organisations are improving their current practices and processes focusing on a activities, tools and methods that help to make a sustainable future a reality.

In this context, Our Collections Matter program (OCM) (ICCROM [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) developed by the International Centre for the Study of the Preservation and Restoration of Cultural Property (ICCROM) was launched in 2020 to accelerate, increase, and amplify activities that support sustainable development through the use and conservation of heritage collections. One of its main resources is the OCM toolkit, which aims to provide with a selection of ‘how-to’ guides and methods to help these organisations to support sustainable development (Loddo et al. [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). This toolkit includes the 17 SDGs as well as the ‘5 Ps’ of sustainable development and actions that can be taken to contribute to sustainable development by supporting users in setting goals, planning and delivering actions, and monitoring and evaluating activity in sustainable development terms. Of course, it provides some practical activities aimed at achieving SDG5, but there is still a need for a specific tool that could help to change the gender bias and, more specifically, to provide a practical test that could potentially be applied to any type of collection in order to demonstrate whether heritage can indeed approach gender issues. To address these challenges, a re-reading toolkit was developed that consists of a series of activities designed so that any type of organisation can adapt them according to their needs.

### 3.1. Developing a toolkit aiming to pave gender equality

The re-readings toolkit is designed so that both the staff of each institution and the visitors can make use of the heritage collections, and through them, they can critically re-read femininity, masculinity, stereotypes, assigned roles, and question themselves about their role in fostering such attitudes. Hence, the main objectives of this toolkit are twofold: (1) to change the gender bias (or at least try to) and (2) to demonstrate with actual data that collections can actually be part of this change. Specific objectives were set for both CBOs and visitors. First, for CBOs, the aims of this toolkit are (1) to better understand how each institution represents gender, (2) to increase female representation in their collections, and (3) to encourage active conversations among staff about gender equity. The goals for visitors are (1) to encourage girls and women to (re)gain their power to interpret themselves and not just be objects for the male gaze, and (2) to activate conversations about gender equity.

The development of this toolkit was based on previous research, mainly the re-readings project coordinated by the University of Valencia (Gaitán and Alba [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), an initiative organised around a Valencian (Spanish) museum network that aims to take gender-related perspectives into account in museum collections. Through its web-based platform, it advocates being as open as possible in dealing with gender issues. This project sets the basis for the current toolkit, as it takes the project’s idea of re-reading collections from a gender perspective and translates it into a set of activities for any CBO.

The main idea behind this toolkit is that any institution is independent of its type of collection (paintings, textiles, ethnographic, scientific, etc..) can use the toolkit as anything can be read from a gender perspective, for example, X-ray diffractometry (XRD), an instrument of this type that was finally able to identify the structure of DNA, for which the researchers involved were awarded two Nobel prizes. Years later, it was revealed that the work of chemist Rosalind Franklin was central to the understanding of the structure of DNA, but her contributions to the discovery were only recognised posthumously. This is yet another example of the so-called ‘Matilda effect, which countless female scientists have suffered throughout history.

In the re-reading toolkit, the combination and use of proposed activities depend on several factors: staff availability, time, space, and commitment. These activities depend on the use of collections. Participants had to find the right balance between keeping objects safe and making them accessible.

The toolkit consists of five activities (including the evaluation), three of which are designed to use heritage collections as an instrumental means to change gender bias, one is designed as an icebreaker, and the last is the GRAS test. Of these activities, only one was intended exclusively for use by the CBO staff. It is recommended to start with the first activity, which is the famous riddle developed by Belle et al. (2021) who used it to demonstrate the lingering power of gender stereotypes. In other words, our perceptions of women and men are biased by our nonconscious beliefs about gender differences (Valian [Citation 2005](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). This exercise is meant to act as an ice breaker but also to help people understand that most of us hold these biases and that we do have to change them. How can this be done by CBOs is the answer we hope to ignite from this activity?

The other activities are thought to actively use heritage collections; of these, two can be used by staff members and visitors, while the third is meant only for CBOs staff. First, the toolkit reinterprets the Bechdel test ([Citation 1986](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) which shows three criteria that movies should follow to measure gender bias: (1) having at least two women in it, (2) who talk to each other, and (3) abouts something other than a man. This test has the potential to be used in other contexts, owing to its generality. The activity requires participants to walk around with the displayed collection and think about how women are represented (paintings, sculptures, signs, etc.). For example, are they loving mothers or mistresses or are they represented as women in their own right in active attitudes? Participants had to place a green post if they thought they were active women, a yellow if there was an intention but it would not succeed, and a red if it definitely would not pass the test. This was followed by an active discussion, in which participants had to rethink how they would reinterpret yellow or red objects.

The next activity is the so-called re-reading collection which aims to invite visitors to re-read collections and to analyse how gender is shown in the exhibited objects from a gender perspective, as CBOs have the chance to be part of this change through reinterpretation of the patriarchal bias of collections and attributions, through literacy programs, or by providing positive role models for women (Malt [Citation 2007](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). In this activity, participants were asked to walk around with the displayed collection and select one or more objects from the collection (depending on the time available), and with the selected object, they had to interpret it from a gender perspective. Once this is done, participants should be gathered, and they must discuss their reinterpretation (e.g., narrating a story somehow contemporary or related to their experiences). Other participants may interact with and discuss the objects and their stories. Why did they select the objects? What were the proposed changes? How can this be a part of gender equality? How can we break a glass ceiling?

On the other hand, the third activity that directly used the collection was designed exclusively by the CBOs staff. This last exercise is meant to achieve a long-term impact by questioning their practices and policies using gender as a criterion, with the aim of eliminating gender inequalities. In this activity, participants are tasked with gathering information from all the activities and areas of their institution related to women; for example, how many women are conservators, curators, administrators, etc., are they equally paid as their male colleagues? How many female artists and scientists were mentioned or shown in the collection? These data can be gathered in the same session or fulfilled in one week. Once it is done, participants have to share the data and discuss what positive actions they will take, for example, taking more women out on exhibitions, increasing the number of collaborations with women, etc. These commitments must be evaluated after one year, and the results must be compared to improve or adjust the goals previously set.

The idea behind these three activities is not new; in fact, in 1995, Berg and Lie ([Citation 1995](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) questioned whether objects have gender? They concluded that artefacts indeed have gender and gender politics in the sense that they are designed and used in gendered contexts. Similarly, Smith argues that heritage is gendered in the way heritage is defined, understood and talked about, and, in turn, in the way it reproduces and legitimises gender identities and the social values that underpin them (Smith [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). CBOs have produced a masculine perspective, formed by class-specific values and perceptions which have turned into validating an elite view of male history (Colella [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) and excluding women under the excuse of universality or neutrality (‘the genius’). Through these activities, participants can question the traditional canon embedded in the traditional male context and reframe its ideological assumptions. Collections should be analysed from a contextual standpoint, taking into consideration the specific historical, social, and racial situations that women face in every historical period. Moreover, they can be used to apply an intersectional approach, as each culture inevitably has specific connotations for the determined concepts. Indeed, as already mentioned, the mainstream feminist movement traditionally focuses on white, straight, middle-class, and able-bodied women, excluding other realities in favour of universal womanhood (Hooks [Citation 1981](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

Regarding the evaluation, the re-readings toolkit uses the test developed by the Spanish psychologists García-Cueto et al. ([Citation 2015](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) but it is adapted to CBOs. The aim of this test, which has already been tested in contexts other than Spain (Bringas Molleda et al. [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Pérez Sánchez et al. [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), is to evaluate opinions and egalitarian or sexist beliefs that people have about the role that men and women play in society (García-Díaz et al. [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) in three domains: family attitudes (six items), work attitudes (six items), and social attitudes (eight items). Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale with five response alternatives. The values ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The sum of the above-mentioned factors results in the fourth factor, total attitude. The maximum score on the test is 80. The test does not have a scale; therefore, a lower score indicates a more sexist attitude, whereas a higher score indicates a more egalitarian one.

The selection of the GRAS test was mainly due to its length, its easiness regarding the analysis of responses, as well as for being updated. In this regard, we have to consider the time museum staff have not only when performing didactic activities but also when they can evaluate them, especially in small- and medium-sized museums, which are usually understaffed and underfunded. Therefore, the GRAS test permits credible evidence to clearly demonstrate that CBOs can contribute significantly and measurably to sustainable development across all dimensions and goals. This piece of evidence can serve as a snowball and encourage more organisations to apply this and other toolkits, become an important part of the 2030 Agenda, and strengthen sustainable development. When applying it, CBOs were requested to explain to the participants that the objective of the survey was to evaluate the activities and that there were no incorrect answers. In addition, as the survey is anonymous, they just have to fulfil some demographic data (age and sex) and add a number or other type of indicative to the survey and remember it as they will have to write again in GRASII. Participants were required to complete the survey in silence and without seeing each other’s answers. Afterwards, the surveys should be collected and then the other activities should begin; once they are finished, participants must fulfil the same test again, such that CBOs can compare results and if the gender gaze has indeed changed into more positive actions towards gender equality.

## 4\. Case study: a ceramics museum

### 4.1. Workshop

This toolkit was first applied at the National Museum of Ceramics in González Martí, Spain. Their collections are characterised by their variety and heterogeneity, due to the very history of the formation of the collections, it houses Spanish ceramics, especially those from Valencia (Paterna, Manises, Al-cora), foreign ceramics, collections of decorative arts (furniture, clothing, jewellery, etc.), as well as paintings, prints and sculptures.

Owing to the heterogeneity of the collection, only a part of it was chosen to apply some of the toolkit activities and ceramic collections. The choice was made by museum conservators along with the director and the didactic team, as most of the ceramics were located in the same area and the participants could move freely without interrupting the preestablished flow of the museum rooms. This was important because the participants had to select the pieces calmly after visiting the exhibits and get to know them freely.

The activity was conducted in the morning of July 2023. Of the activities developed in the toolkit, three were chosen: the GRAS test, gender riddle, and re-reading of collections. The activity was moderated by the author of this paper and counted with the participation of 18 participants from 16 countries, with ages ranging from 19 to 40. All participants were female and were also participants of the European H2020 project ‘Equals’ (Equals [Citation 2022](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)).

First, the moderator introduced herself to the participants, handed out the GRAS test, and asked them to fill it silently and anonymously (they only marked the page with a number that had been previously assigned to them).

The next activity consisted of projecting the riddle on a screen and reading it to them, asking those who knew the answer beforehand not to say it for the moment, while the remaining participants were asked to indicate who the surgeon might be. This served as an icebreaker to ask them about gender stereotypes in the workplace and why they thought they were widespread. They were then asked to name female artists, scientists, and other relevant women from their respective fields.

Next, the moderator explained to the participants the re-reading activity, and the participants were grouped (four teams of three and three teams of two participants) and activity sheets () were shared with them in case they wished to use them as support for further brainstorming. Participants were given approximately 30–40 min to select an object from the ceramic collection and re-read it from a gender perspective. After the time passed, the participants returned to the room and shared their re-readings while triggering a discussion of each of the themes that the participants commented on. Finally, they were asked to complete the GRAS test again and their activity was determined.

### 4.2. Methodology

This toolkit follows the museological practice of including the affective turn in curatorial practices. That is, evoking emotional responses fosters engagement with the objects in the exhibition and the public. Scholars have noted that the emotional connection and empathy generated in museum settings can enhance people's comprehension of the past but also to connect it with the present and their future as it can transform the relationships amongst objects, spaces, visitors and curatorial practices (Innes and Sharp [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Savenije and de Bruijn [Citation 2017](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Varutti [Citation 2023](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). For example, affect provides novel analytical insights into the influence of objects, both diachronic and synchronic, as in the case of gender approaches.

On the other hand, as per the evaluation, this toolkit employs two distinct approaches: quantitative assessment using a Likert scale and qualitative evaluation through post-intervention discussions. As previously noted, the quantitative component.

utilised the GRAS test, modified for CBOs (). The qualitative aspect was integrated into the post-intervention review process, wherein participants were asked to present their chosen object and explain how they reinterpreted it from a gender-focused perspective. The intervention facilitator recorded observations and encouraged further dialogue by noting recurring themes, with the objective of gaining thorough insight into how various audiences perceive and experience gender-related interventions.

### 4.3. Data analysis

According to the qualitative assessment, group interaction patterns were monitored in situ to evaluate factors, such as member engagement, interpersonal exchanges, and the range of viewpoints expressed.

Concerning the GRAS assessment, pre-intervention and post-intervention outcomes were examined to determine which specific elements changed due to the intervention. The items were categorised divided into three domains: (1) family (e.g., ‘Mothers should make most of the decisions about how to bring up their children’), including Family Function Transcendent (FFT) and Family Function Sexism (FFS);; (2) social interactions (e.g., ‘I think it is worse for a man to cry than for a woman’), including Social Function Transcendent (SFT) and Social Function Sexism (SFS); and (3) work-related matters (e.g., ‘Only some kinds of job are equally appropriate for men and women’) Employment Function Sexism (EFS).

## 5\. Discussion and conclusions

The first version of the Re-readings Toolkit has proven to be a positive experience that can be replicated in other CBOs. Our findings on the riddle hint that there is still a long way toward gender bias, which aligns with other research(Kollmayer et al. [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Skorinko [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#); Stoeger, Ziegler, and David [Citation 2004](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) which claims that there is still a long way to overcome role schemas, as individuals still often underrate women in professional roles. In fact, in a recent study in 2021 (Belle et al. [Citation 2021](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), only 30% of the participants answered that the surgeon was the mother, whereas 36% offered the same-sex parent as an answer to the riddle. Both in that case and in the present study, these answers show that advancements in LGBTQ+ rights still demonstrate resistance to gender schemas. Despite this, we can argue that this exercise was quite positive in terms of sharing knowledge from other parts of the world, and it was clear that, for the most part, women continue to occupy fewer positions of power, but especially act as ice breakers.

Regarding Re-Readings themselves, the results demonstrate that objects in CBOs prove to be an excellent means to engage with cultural production and reflect on how (1) collections are shown, (2)what they reflect, and (3) how we relate (or not) to them. The results of the experiment found clear support for the fact that CBOs and their collections are not gender-neutral, as they perpetuate through what they decide to expose and how they adopt a traditionally masculine perspective which has dominated the way those collections have been preserved, valued, and interpreted (Colella [Citation 2018](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), marginalising women and their history. Using their collections to rethink our past, interpret our present, and have a better future comes from acknowledging women’s history and their contributions (Botte [Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), as well as their struggles in the past and in the present, paving the way towards gender equality.

As per the GRAS test, it allowed to discover that as per family attitudes, the FFT () did not occurred any change, while the FFS () was improved, especially Q7 ‘My partner thinking that I am responsible for doing the household chores would cause me stress’. Regarding, the SFT (), showed that Q1 had poor results, whereas the rest remained the same or improved by one point (Q2). The transcendent aspects remained unchanged. The SFS () indicated that it did not improve following the intervention, whereas the EFS () attitudes were improved.

Despite recognising the constraints of GRAS, given its limited resources, it provided an efficient and swift means to obtain an initial understanding of gender attitudes within CBOs. It is worth noting that even without considering Cronbach’s alpha interpretation, GRAS remains an accessible and straightforward tool for gathering numerical data to inform future interventions. This is particularly significant in many CBOs, where decision making often prioritises quantifiable data. In the absence of universally specific instruments for assessing how CBOs address gender issues, the GRAS test yields concrete and comparable scores, thus facilitating gender evaluation.

The limitations of the present studies naturally lie in the (1) number of participants who attended the activity; and (2) that they were all women. However, previous exercises related to this activity, such as guided tours carried out by the Rereading project (Alba, Cuesta, and Gaitán [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)), seem to confirm that the use of this toolkit will have a good reaction in any other CBO. At this stage of understanding, we support the use of collections from a gender perspective, as sustainable development cannot be achieved without gender equality (Pujar [Citation 2016](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)). Nonetheless, future investigations are necessary to validate the conclusions that can be drawn from this study, which has already been planned for museums in Latin America, Europe, and Africa. In this regard, the author of this paper has already worked in other contexts and museums which will be used to analyse not only the activities, but especially the GRAS test which is not shown in this paper as it is expected to have several CBOs data to be displayed.

As Azcona and Bhatt ([Citation 2020](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) point out, SDGs are bold in urging all stakeholders to collaborate in tracking progress and shaping policies. However, the process of forming a coalition of gender data producers and users to track progress on economic, social, and environmental targets is complex and time-consuming, and requires significant financial investment and political commitment. In this regard, CBOs can play a significant role by including gender perspectives in their practices providing unique perspectives on thinking, analysing, and presenting knowledge that goes beyond conventional methods, capturing dimensions that other approaches may miss (Oliveira [Citation 2019](https://www-tandfonline-com.sire.ub.edu/doi/full/10.1080/#)) and providing data, as shown in this paper.

In summary, CBOs using this toolkit and addressing SDG5 are vindicating women's work as a means to understand women as visual artists, video artists, cu-rators, historians, dealers, conservators, scientists, etc. as important as men, as well as showing how their spaces are not neutral and might collaborate to reduce gender biases.
