# User-Centered Design and Neuro-Symbolic AI for Digital Historical Archives: Addressing Epistemic Injustice in Francoist Repression Databases

## Abstract

This PhD research proposal presents a comprehensive framework for developing user-centered digital historical archives that address epistemic injustice and representational gaps in databases documenting Francoist repression in Spain. The study employs participatory design methodologies and neuro-symbolic AI technologies to create inclusive information architectures that serve diverse user communities while promoting epistemic justice for historically marginalized groups. By focusing on five digital archives containing personal data related to Francoist repression and censorship, this research aims to contribute to how historical information is curated, accessed, and represented in digital environments, with particular attention to gender perspectives, intersectionalities, and underrepresented voices.

## Introduction: The Historical Context of Repression and Digital Memory

The Spanish Civil War (1936-1939) and subsequent Francoist dictatorship (1939-1975) represent one of the most traumatic periods in modern Spanish history, characterized by systematic repression, censorship, and the suppression of democratic voices. During this era, hundreds of thousands of individuals faced persecution, imprisonment, torture, and execution for their political beliefs, regional identities, or simply for being perceived as threats to the regime. The historical memory of this period has been systematically marginalized, and the voices of victims and their families have been silenced for decades.

In recent years, there has been a growing movement to recover and preserve this historical memory through digital archives and databases. These initiatives aim to document the experiences of those who suffered under the Francoist regime, providing access to historical records, personal testimonies, and archival materials that were previously hidden or destroyed. However, the creation and curation of these digital historical archives present unique challenges related to representation, accessibility, and the perpetuation of historical injustices within digital information systems.

The digital representation of historical repression data is not merely a technical challenge but a deeply political and ethical undertaking. Traditional archival practices have often reproduced the power structures and biases of the societies that created them, leading to the continued marginalization of certain voices and perspectives. In the context of Francoist repression, this means that women, LGBTQ+ individuals, ethnic minorities, and other intersectional identities may continue to be underrepresented or misrepresented in digital historical archives.

## Theoretical Framework: Epistemic Injustice and Participatory Design

This research is grounded in the theoretical framework of epistemic injustice, as developed by Miranda Fricker (2007), which examines how knowledge systems can perpetuate social inequalities through testimonial injustice (when speakers are not given appropriate credibility) and hermeneutical injustice (when there are gaps in shared interpretive resources). In the context of digital historical archives, epistemic injustice manifests through the systematic exclusion or marginalization of certain perspectives and voices in the creation, organization, and presentation of historical information.

The concept of epistemic injustice is particularly relevant when examining databases of Francoist repression, where the historical silencing of victims and their families continues to be reproduced in digital form. Women, for instance, who played crucial roles in resistance movements and suffered specific forms of repression, are often underrepresented in traditional historical narratives and may face similar marginalization in digital archives. Similarly, LGBTQ+ individuals who were persecuted under Francoist laws may find their experiences inadequately represented or categorized in ways that do not reflect their lived realities.

Participatory design (PD) and user-centered design (UCD) methodologies offer a framework for addressing these epistemic injustices by involving diverse communities in the design and development of digital historical archives. These methodologies recognize that meaningful digital solutions emerge only within appropriate social contexts where archives, systems, and media function as integral components of community practice. By engaging directly with the communities most affected by historical repression, participatory design can help ensure that digital archives reflect the diverse perspectives and needs of those whose histories are being preserved.

The research also incorporates the concept of "double hybridization" - the integration of human-machine collaboration through participatory co-creation processes and the combination of symbolic (semantic web, ontologies, linked data) and subsymbolic (machine learning, large language models) systems. This hybrid approach aims to improve the interpretability, explainability, and generative capabilities of information systems while advancing epistemic justice through co-production of knowledge processes.

## Methodology: Participatory Design for Digital Historical Archives

The methodology of this research is structured around four transversal axes that integrate theoretical reflection with practical application:

### Axis 1: Theoretical Foundation for Double Hybridization

The first axis focuses on establishing an interdisciplinary theoretical framework that supports the use of PD/UCD processes in various phases of designing systems for the curation and semi-automatic generation of content based on historical archives. This includes analyzing the application of participatory design methodologies in the context of digital humanities, with particular attention to how these methodologies can address epistemic injustices in historical representation.

The theoretical framework draws from multiple disciplines, including information science, digital humanities, gender studies, and critical theory. It examines how traditional archival practices have perpetuated historical injustices and explores how participatory design can create more inclusive and representative digital historical archives. The framework also considers the ethical implications of using AI technologies in the curation and presentation of historical information, particularly when dealing with sensitive personal data related to repression and trauma.

### Axis 2: Methodological Framework for Participatory Co-Creation

The second axis involves designing and implementing methodologies for co-creation processes tailored to the specific objectives of different project phases and focused on specific knowledge communities related to the case study of five databases concerning Francoist repression in Spain. These communities include:

**Researchers**: Academic historians, archivists, and digital humanities scholars who have compiled the databases to be integrated. These users require sophisticated search and analysis tools, comprehensive metadata, and the ability to trace sources and verify information.

**Activists**: Human rights activists, memory recovery organizations, and social justice advocates who use historical information to support contemporary struggles for justice and recognition. These users need accessible interfaces, clear narratives, and tools for advocacy and education.

**Family Members**: Descendants of victims and survivors who seek information about their relatives and family histories. These users require sensitive, respectful interfaces that acknowledge the emotional and personal nature of their searches, as well as privacy protections and support for processing traumatic information.

**Wikipedia Contributors**: Digital volunteers who create and maintain Wikipedia entries related to historical repression. These users need reliable sources, clear citation formats, and tools for collaborative editing and fact-checking.

**Software Developers**: Technical professionals involved in creating the neuro-symbolic AI systems and information architectures. These users require detailed documentation, testing frameworks, and tools for maintaining and improving the systems.

Each of these user communities has distinct needs, expectations, and use cases that must be carefully considered in the design process. The participatory design methodology involves engaging with these communities through interviews, workshops, co-creation sessions, and iterative testing to ensure that the resulting systems truly serve their needs and reflect their perspectives.

### Axis 3: Evaluation Tools for Participatory Design Processes

The third axis focuses on developing and applying evaluation tools for co-creation processes and the contributions that participatory exercises produce in the development of semi-automatic content generation and curation technologies. This evaluation framework considers criteria of epistemic justice, interpretability (both human and machine learning), and usage contexts with ethical support and social responsibility.

The evaluation process includes both formative and summative assessments. Formative evaluations occur throughout the design and development process, providing continuous feedback that can inform iterative improvements. Summative evaluations assess the final systems against established criteria for usability, accessibility, representational accuracy, and epistemic justice.

Key evaluation criteria include:
- **Representational Accuracy**: Do the digital archives accurately represent the diverse experiences and perspectives of those affected by Francoist repression?
- **Accessibility**: Are the systems usable by individuals with different technical skills, languages, and abilities?
- **Epistemic Justice**: Do the systems promote rather than perpetuate historical injustices?
- **Explainability**: Can users understand how the AI systems make decisions and recommendations?
- **Privacy and Ethics**: Are sensitive personal data adequately protected while still providing meaningful access to historical information?

### Axis 4: Evaluation Framework for Neuro-Symbolic AI Systems

The fourth axis involves participating in the evaluation of the HerStory Neuro-Symbolic AI service, its information architecture, transformation to Linked Open Data (LOD) and semantic web, interface design for information retrieval, and interaction models. This evaluation considers ethical, functional (bias reduction and hallucination mitigation), explainability, and gender perspective criteria.

The neuro-symbolic AI systems combine symbolic knowledge representation (ontologies, knowledge graphs) with subsymbolic machine learning approaches (neural networks, language models) to create more interpretable and trustworthy AI systems. In the context of historical archives, this hybrid approach can help ensure that AI-generated content is accurate, explainable, and respectful of the historical context and emotional significance of the information.

## Case Study: Five Digital Archives of Francoist Repression

The research focuses on five specific digital humanities initiatives in the context of Francoist Spain, each containing personal data related to repression and censorship during the Francoist period. These initiatives, each driven by unique motivations, historical knowledge, and technical expertise, provide a solid foundation for research design and ensure comprehensive exploration of personal data related to historical repression.

### Archive 1: Victims of Francoist Repression Database
This archive contains biographical information, photographs, and testimonies of individuals who were persecuted, imprisoned, or executed during the Francoist period. The database includes detailed records of court proceedings, prison records, and family testimonies, providing a comprehensive view of individual experiences of repression.

### Archive 2: Women's Resistance and Repression Archive
This specialized archive focuses specifically on women's experiences during the Francoist period, including their roles in resistance movements, specific forms of gender-based repression, and the unique challenges faced by women political prisoners. The archive documents how women's experiences were often overlooked in traditional historical narratives and seeks to recover these marginalized voices.

### Archive 3: Regional and Linguistic Repression Archive
This archive documents the systematic suppression of regional identities, languages, and cultural practices under the Francoist regime. It includes information about individuals persecuted for speaking regional languages, practicing regional customs, or advocating for regional autonomy. This archive is particularly important for understanding how the Francoist regime sought to create a homogenized Spanish identity.

### Archive 4: LGBTQ+ Persecution Archive
This archive documents the experiences of LGBTQ+ individuals who were persecuted under Francoist laws that criminalized homosexuality and gender non-conformity. The archive includes records of arrests, trials, and imprisonment, as well as testimonies from survivors and their families. This archive is crucial for understanding a form of repression that has been particularly marginalized in historical narratives.

### Archive 5: Intellectual and Cultural Repression Archive
This archive focuses on the persecution of intellectuals, artists, writers, and cultural figures who were targeted for their ideas, creative work, or political affiliations. It includes information about book burnings, censorship, exile, and the suppression of cultural expression during the Francoist period.

Each of these archives presents unique challenges and opportunities for participatory design and neuro-symbolic AI integration. The diversity of the archives ensures that the research addresses a wide range of representational issues and user needs, providing a robust foundation for developing generalizable principles for digital historical archives.

## Information Architecture and Semantic Technologies

The research involves the design and prototyping of information architecture services (navigation, labeling, visualization) for the curation and access to historical data about Francoist repression with a gender perspective. This includes analyzing models applied in existing experiences, defining technically viable information architecture strategies, generating evaluable prototypes through participatory processes, and integrating results from work with interest groups.

The information architecture must balance several competing demands: providing comprehensive access to historical information while respecting privacy and emotional sensitivity; supporting both expert researchers and general public users; and ensuring that the organization and presentation of information does not perpetuate historical biases or injustices.

### Navigation Design
The navigation systems must accommodate different user types and their varying levels of expertise and emotional investment in the material. For family members searching for information about relatives, the navigation should be intuitive and supportive, with clear pathways and emotional support resources. For researchers, the navigation should provide sophisticated filtering and analysis tools. For activists, the navigation should highlight connections between historical events and contemporary issues.

### Labeling and Categorization
The labeling and categorization systems must be carefully designed to avoid reproducing historical biases. Traditional archival categories may not adequately capture the experiences of marginalized groups or may use language that is offensive or inappropriate. The participatory design process involves working with affected communities to develop more inclusive and accurate categorization systems.

### Visualization
Data visualization tools must be designed to reveal patterns and connections in the historical data while being sensitive to the human cost represented by the data points. Visualizations should help users understand the scale and scope of repression while maintaining the dignity and humanity of the individuals whose experiences are being represented.

## Linked Open Data and Semantic Web Integration

The research involves participating in the transformation of databases into Linked Open Data (LOD) through semantic web technologies (RDF, ontologies), ensuring the inclusion of underrepresented narratives (such as those related to gender, intersectionalities, and underrepresented groups).

Linked Open Data technologies offer significant opportunities for connecting related information across different archives and databases, creating a more comprehensive and interconnected view of historical repression. However, the integration of diverse datasets also presents challenges related to data quality, consistency, and the potential for perpetuating biases across connected datasets.

### Ontology Development
The development of ontologies for historical repression data requires careful consideration of how concepts and relationships are defined and represented. Traditional ontologies may not adequately capture the complexity and nuance of historical experiences, particularly those of marginalized groups. The participatory design process involves working with affected communities to develop ontologies that accurately represent their experiences and perspectives.

### Data Integration
The integration of data from multiple archives requires careful attention to issues of data quality, consistency, and provenance. Different archives may use different standards, formats, and terminology, requiring sophisticated data integration techniques. The integration process must also consider how to handle conflicting or contradictory information while maintaining the integrity of the original sources.

### Cross-Archive Connections
Linked Open Data technologies enable the creation of connections between related information across different archives. For instance, a family member might be able to trace connections between their relative's experience and broader patterns of repression, or researchers might be able to identify previously unknown connections between different resistance movements or forms of persecution.

## Neuro-Symbolic AI for Historical Archives

The research involves collaborating in the development and evaluation of Neuro-Symbolic AI systems for the representation and retrieval of historical information, with criteria of explainability, ethics, and absence of bias.

Neuro-symbolic AI combines the strengths of symbolic AI (which is interpretable and can incorporate explicit knowledge) with the strengths of subsymbolic AI (which can learn from data and handle complex patterns). In the context of historical archives, this hybrid approach can help create AI systems that are both powerful and trustworthy.

### Explainable AI for Historical Context
Historical information often requires careful interpretation and contextualization. AI systems that can explain their reasoning and provide transparent justifications for their recommendations are crucial for maintaining trust and ensuring accuracy in historical research. The neuro-symbolic approach enables the creation of AI systems that can provide clear explanations for their outputs while still leveraging the power of machine learning.

### Bias Detection and Mitigation
Historical archives often contain biases that reflect the power structures and prejudices of the societies that created them. AI systems must be designed to detect and mitigate these biases rather than perpetuate them. The neuro-symbolic approach allows for the explicit encoding of ethical principles and bias detection mechanisms while still benefiting from machine learning capabilities.

### Privacy-Preserving AI
Historical archives often contain sensitive personal information that must be protected while still providing meaningful access to historical knowledge. AI systems must be designed to respect privacy while enabling research and discovery. This may involve techniques such as differential privacy, federated learning, or other privacy-preserving approaches.

## Impact on Participatory Curation and Digital Humanities

The research explores the role of neuro-symbolic AI in the preservation, curation, and dissemination of historical memory with gender perspective, intersectionality, and underrepresented groups. It analyzes the impact of citizen participation on the improvement of quality, visibility, and representation of data in open semantic systems.

### Participatory Curation
Traditional archival curation has often been the domain of professional archivists and historians, who may not fully represent the perspectives and needs of the communities whose histories are being preserved. Participatory curation involves engaging directly with affected communities in the curation process, ensuring that their voices and perspectives are included in decisions about what information to preserve, how to organize it, and how to present it.

### Quality Improvement Through Community Input
Community participation can significantly improve the quality of historical archives by providing additional context, correcting errors, and identifying important connections that might be missed by professional archivists working in isolation. Community members often have unique insights into the historical context and significance of archival materials that can enhance the overall quality and usefulness of the archive.

### Visibility and Representation
Community participation can help ensure that historically marginalized voices and perspectives are adequately represented in digital archives. By involving diverse communities in the design and curation process, archives can become more inclusive and representative of the full complexity of historical experiences.

## Ethical Considerations and Social Responsibility

The development of digital historical archives raises numerous ethical considerations that must be carefully addressed throughout the research process. These considerations are particularly acute when dealing with sensitive historical information related to repression, trauma, and human rights violations.

### Privacy and Data Protection
Historical archives often contain sensitive personal information that must be protected while still providing meaningful access to historical knowledge. This requires careful attention to data protection regulations, privacy by design principles, and the development of appropriate access controls and consent mechanisms.

### Trauma-Informed Design
Many users of historical repression archives are seeking information about traumatic events that affected their families or communities. The design of these systems must be trauma-informed, providing appropriate support, warnings, and resources for users who may be processing difficult emotional content.

### Cultural Sensitivity
Historical archives must be designed with cultural sensitivity, recognizing that different communities may have different perspectives on how historical information should be presented, accessed, and used. This requires ongoing engagement with affected communities and a commitment to respecting their cultural values and practices.

### Reparative Justice
Digital historical archives can play an important role in processes of reparative justice by providing access to information that has been previously hidden or suppressed. However, the creation of these archives must be done in ways that respect the dignity and agency of affected communities and contribute to rather than hinder processes of healing and reconciliation.

## Expected Outcomes and Impact

This research is expected to produce several significant outcomes that will advance both theoretical understanding and practical application in the field of digital historical archives.

### Theoretical Contributions
The research will contribute to theoretical understanding of how participatory design and neuro-symbolic AI can address epistemic injustice in digital information systems. It will develop frameworks for evaluating the ethical and social implications of AI technologies in historical contexts and provide insights into how digital technologies can promote rather than perpetuate historical injustices.

### Methodological Contributions
The research will develop and validate methodologies for participatory design in the context of digital historical archives, providing a model that can be applied to other historical contexts and types of archives. It will also contribute to the development of evaluation frameworks for assessing the effectiveness of participatory design processes and their impact on epistemic justice.

### Technical Contributions
The research will develop and evaluate neuro-symbolic AI systems specifically designed for historical archives, demonstrating how hybrid AI approaches can improve the interpretability, accuracy, and ethical appropriateness of AI systems in sensitive historical contexts.

### Social Impact
The research will contribute to processes of historical memory recovery and reparative justice by creating more inclusive and representative digital historical archives. It will provide tools and resources that can support ongoing efforts to document and preserve historical memory while promoting healing and reconciliation.

## Conclusion: Toward More Just Digital Historical Archives

This research proposal represents a comprehensive approach to addressing epistemic injustice in digital historical archives through the integration of participatory design methodologies and neuro-symbolic AI technologies. By focusing on the specific context of Francoist repression databases and engaging directly with affected communities, the research aims to create a model for more inclusive, representative, and ethically appropriate digital historical archives.

The significance of this research extends beyond the specific context of Francoist repression to broader questions about how digital technologies can be designed and deployed to promote social justice and historical truth. In an era when digital technologies play an increasingly important role in how we access, understand, and remember the past, it is crucial that these technologies be designed with attention to their social and ethical implications.

The participatory design approach ensures that the voices and perspectives of historically marginalized communities are central to the design and development process. The neuro-symbolic AI approach provides technical capabilities while maintaining transparency and explainability. Together, these approaches offer a framework for creating digital historical archives that serve the needs of diverse communities while promoting epistemic justice and historical truth.

The research will contribute to ongoing efforts to recover and preserve historical memory while ensuring that digital technologies serve as tools for justice rather than instruments of continued marginalization. By demonstrating how participatory design and neuro-symbolic AI can be effectively integrated in the context of sensitive historical archives, this research will provide a model that can be applied to other historical contexts and types of archives, contributing to the broader goal of creating more just and inclusive digital information systems.
