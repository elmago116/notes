## (A) Abstract for COMM submission (~130 words; within COMM 100–150; English)

Participatory design, semantic web technologies, and gendered GLAM research overlap in practice yet diverge in vocabulary and database taxonomies, obscuring the field’s structure. Anchored in the University of Barcelona’s HerStory NeSy AI project, this study combines SALSA-informed scoping with bibliometric science mapping of co-occurring author and database keywords in Scopus and Web of Science (2014–2025). After deduplication, 3,250 records fed SciMAT networks for 2014–2019 and 2020–2025, read through Callon’s centrality–density strategic map. Findings highlight persistent fragmentation, declining motor themes, expanding basic and emergent configurations, and denser ties among knowledge graphs, participatory design, cultural heritage, and the keyword *women*. Gender- and intersectionality-related terms surface in more clusters in the later period. The article argues that metadata maps render how scholarly communication organises this intersection, informing more inclusive, human-centred knowledge infrastructures.

**Keywords (illustrative; refine for final submission):** participatory design; human-centred computing; semantic web; GLAM; science mapping; gender; knowledge organization

## (B) Extended abstract (~700 words; senior ICS framing)

**Mapping human-centered knowledge systems in semantic web and neuro-symbolic AI with gendered perspectives in GLAM**

The central issue is infrastructural and communicational: when participatory design, human-centred computing, semantic web technologies, and GLAM practice converge, indexed scholarly documents remains disperse—disciplines use divergent keywords, database taxonomies slice problems differently, and cross-cutting concerns (gender, intersectionality, misrepresentation, epistemic injustice) are hard to trace at scale. That opacity matters for information and communication science because **metadata and indexing are politically consequential**: they stabilise what can be found, cited, and modelled downstream. The study’s **guiding research question** is: *How are participatory design, semantic-web and GLAM-relevant themes, and gender- or identity-related concerns **structurally positioned** in the co-word architecture of Scopus and Web of Science literature between 2014 and 2025, and **how does that architecture change** when comparing 2014–2019 and 2020–2025?* A subsidiary question, tied to a Phd research project, asks where **visibility for women and misrepresented groups** emerges—or fails to consolidate—in that macro-discourse.

**Theoretical and methodological approach.** **Theoretically**, the paper treats author and database keywords as a **surface of scholarly self-description** (how the field “announces” its topics to indexers and peers), linking science-mapping grammar to debates on knowledge organisation, social epistemology, and representational bias in platforms. **Methodologically**, it combines a **SALSA-oriented scoping logic** (search, appraisal, synthesis, analysis) to bound an interdisciplinary corpus with **bibliometric science mapping** in **SciMAT**. Records are drawn with proximity operators, wildcards, and field-tagged queries from **Scopus** (*n* = 2,657 before merge) and **Web of Science** (*n* = 613), 2014–2025; **3,250** unique items remain after deduplication. Normalised keyword **co-occurrence** networks are partitioned into clusters whose strategic roles are read through **Callon’s centrality and density** framework (motor, specialised, basic/transversal, emergent/declining zones), compared across two quinquennia. This design privileges **replicability and transparency** over subjective cluster naming: clusters are reported via their keyword contents.

**Anticipated conclusions and contributions.** The analysis **expects**—and the full paper corroborates—a **persistently fragmented** landscape: many clusters fall in emergent/declining quadrants, indicating weak thematic consolidation at the intersection under study. **Temporal contrast** should show **fewer motor themes**, thinner specialisation, and **more basic and emergent configurations** in 2020–2025 than in 2014–2019, consistent with diversification across contributing fields rather than a single paradigmatic core. **Substantively**, identity-related discourse is anticipated to **shift** from an emphasis on ethnicity, migration, and minority heritage toward **more visible gender, feminism, and marginalisation** vocabularies, spread across **more clusters** in the later window—evidence of gradual repositioning in indexed talk. Patterns around digital humanities, ontologies, linked data, and knowledge graphs are expected to **align partially** with recent domain reviews, lending external plausibility. The headline **empirical contribution** is structural: denser co-occurrence linking knowledge graphs, participatory design, cultural heritage, and the keyword *women*, with that bundle moving toward a more specialised strategic niche—**bibliometric evidence** that gender is gaining traction in the same lexical layer later reused in retrieval and analytics. For **information and communication science**, anticipated **contributions** are: (1) a **workflow** for mapping interdisciplinary contact zones via metadata; (2) an **interpretive bridge** between co-word cartography and critique of knowledge infrastructures; (3) an **empirical baseline** for project-led interventions (HerStory NeSy AI) on fairer description and navigation. **Limitations** remain those of keyword studies (noise, indexing artefacts, Anglophone skew); **future work** should triangulate with full text, citation milieux, and institutional geography.
