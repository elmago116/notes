**Mapping Human-Centered Knowledge:**

**Participatory Design, Semantic Web and gendered perspectives in GLAM**

Elena Gómez*<sup>1</sup>*, Nuria Ferran-Ferrer<sup>*2*</sup> and Miquel Centelles*<sup>2</sup>*

<sup>1</sup> Barcelona University, Spain

<sup>2</sup> Barcelona University, Melcior de Palau, 140, Barcelona-Spain

Abstract

This work is framed within a doctoral research project at the University of Barcelona, HerStory NeSy Ai, which seeks to identify pivotal literature on human-centered computation projects and information architectures within a given interdisciplinary framework: semantic and neurosymbolic technologies, participatory design and GLAM (galleries, libraries, archives, and museums). Furthermore, it will examine the visibility of specific topics related to epistemic injustice, gender and intersectional perspectives, and misrepresented groups in them. This study uses a metadata and bibliometric analysis method based on scientific maps and keywords to construct the conceptual structure of a section of a knowledge domain situated at the intersection of various topics over the last decade. The mapping reveals significant, albeit weak, co-occurrence patterns, given the terminological diversity within each topic. The clusters' evolution shows a decrease in density and a subtle increase in centrality, except for one cluster that grows in density by gaining a new keyword, 'women'.

Keywords

> Participatory Design, Human Centered Computing, Human Centered Design, User-centered design, Human-machine interaction, Semantic Web, Open Linked Data, RDF, Ontologies, GLAM (Galleries, Libraries, Archives, Museums). [^1]

# Introduction 

Research areas have emerged, driven by the social impact of technology-centered design while simultaneously seeking to integrate human needs and perceptions into the design process. However, substantial ambiguity persists in a comprehensive understanding of these fields, especially in the conceptual and methodological frameworks.

This study, as part of PhD research in the context of HerStory project (PID2023-147673OB-I00), explores how Neuro‑Symbolic Artificial Intelligence (NeSyAI) and Information Architecture can contribute to mitigating gender bias in information systems (Smith, A., Johnson, B., 2023). The project seeks to enhance the ways data about women and misrepresented groups is searched, navigated, organized, labeled, visualized, and narrated. For knowledge-based technologies to address biases and informational gaps, advances are required not only in semantic technologies and IA theory, including their ontological designs and metadata, but also in human participation frameworks (Capel, T., Brereton, M 2023). Participatory approaches such as user-centered design (UCD) contribute to system coherence from social science and humanistic perspectives. By improving the design processes in the context of richer data and metadata in the context of co-production of knowledge (Jasanoff, S. 2004), these strategies may help to identify and counteract representational gaps caused by information appropriation and access, making them essential for addressing issues of gender, intersectionality and other marginalized groups. As Fricker, M (2007) observes, epistemic advantages often follow from material advantages, underscoring the need for critical engagement with knowledge management infrastructures.

Participatory design (PD) and related approaches (human-centered, user-centered, and co-creation) are therefore essential (García, M., López, P., 2024). They suggest that technology development with active participation of the stakeholders—particularly those most affected by it — can improve their feasibility, viability and desirability (García, M., López, P., 2024; Chen, L., Wang, H., 2023). When such technology is knowledge-based the need for participation goes beyond usability. It becomes a matter of social and ethical responsibility with “its social context because archives, systems and media only have meaning as part of a community practice” (Escobar, A. 2019).

This development context calls for ways to explore relevant academic literature acknowledging its multidisciplinarity. This can be challenging due to the use of different terminologies and epistemological understandings (Fidel, R. 2012) and incompatible disciplinary taxonomies (Wacnik, P., et al. 2025). Traditional inquiries in databases like Scopus and Web of Science (WoS) yield partial or broad results even with sound conceptual questions. Using academic metadata and bibliometric techniques allows the understanding of the relationship between the multidisciplinary queries such as these and reveals its latent thematic structures (Cobo, M. et al. 2011a; Ermel, A. et al. 2021).

Recognizing and clarifying the conceptual structure of these intersecting domains is thus crucial for establishing solid methodological foundations. Only then can the development of technological processes genuinely reflect a user-centered design philosophy (Cobo, M. 2011b).

# Methodology

To explore the interdisciplinary field at the intersection of participatory design, semantic web technologies, and gender perspectives in GLAM institutions, a combined methodological approach was adopted. This approach integrates a SALSA scoping review framework (Lopezosa, C. et al. 2023) with metadata analysis and bibliometric mapping techniques - scientific maps (Cobo, M. et al. 2011a) - to capture both the breadth of the literature and the conceptual relationships between key themes. The scoping review provides a systematic framework for the bibliometric mapping to show the thematic clusters positioned strategically. Together, these methods offer a comprehensive view of the field’s conceptual structure and support the identification of research gaps and emerging directions through four sequential phases.

In the **search** phase, search equations were built (Cobo, M. 2011b) and its results refined (Lopezosa, C., Codina, L. 2023) ensuring a significant and representative sample of literature[^2] (primary documents and their metadata) to apply bibliometric techniques effectively. The search strategy made use of focused, proximity, and broad operators (e.g., W/n, NEAR/n, wild cards \*, and field-specific labels) for metadata extraction from Scopus (2,657 records) and Web of Science (613 records) from 2014–2025, yielding 3,250 unique records after deduplication. The **appraisal** phase involved the systematic evaluation and refinement of the metadata corpus. The Synthesis phase consisted of the application of the bibliometric mapping techniques (Cobo, M. et al. 2011a; Ermel, A. et al. 2021) using SciMAT software (Snyder, H. 2019). This generated a thematic network with 14,151 nodes and 231,319 edges divided into two five-year periods (2014–2019 and 2020–2025) to identify what are termed “strategic zones”. These networks are based on bibliometrics of 45,650 retrieved and normalized keywords co-occurrence (Callon, M. et al 1991) and clustering algorithms (Cobo, M. 2011b; Cobo, M. et. al. 2012) (such as simple centers). This approach ensured both thematic coverage and the minimization of bias by avoiding manual keyword inclusion. The resulting clusters were then positioned into four quadrants—motor themes, specialized themes, emerging or declining themes, and basic/transversal themes (based on Callon’s centrality and density measures) (Callon, M. et al 1991) —allowing the description of a conceptual structure based on metadata across the last decade. Finally, the **analysis** phase consisted of the examination of both the general conceptual structure of the field and the thematic positioning of the project-specific topics. This methodological design enables both quantitative mapping of the field’s conceptual structure and qualitative identification of relevant projects, user-centered or participatory design methodologies, and the presence of semantic web technologies within GLAM contexts.

Seven reviews are considered relevant within the search themes although none integrating all of them. The work made by Wacnik, P. et al. (2025) integrates the literature basis for participative design from its emergence context since 1990. From a complementary perspective, Bhandari, A. (2023) builds a bibliometric map about design thinking research trends. Another study (Delgado, F. et al., 2023) focuses on AI design processes, examining the theoretical frameworks in which participatory approaches mark a significant shift in the design of these processes. Baghini, M., et al. (2024) compiles usability testing literature, its trends and methodologies. The work of Hamilton, K. et al. (2024) structured a review analyzing reported advances on neurosymbolic AI in terms of natural language processing. There is also the state of the art made by Desul, S. et al. (2025) focused on the use of semantic technology for cultural heritage, its benefits and discussions. This review looks at two of the four topics that are of interest. It lacks both the gender perspective and the participatory design methodologies to complete the picture. Addressing the gender perspective, Ferran-Ferrer, N. et al. (2023) study the gender gap in Wikipedia as a knowledge architecture and platform with great visibility and social impact.

Although these reviews contribute to the context in which this bibliometric process is analyzed, they do not allow the results to be contrasted or discussed directly. They provide disciplinary insight into this interdisciplinary endeavor.

# Results: bibliometric analysis of the fields’ conceptual structures (2014-25) 

The general analysis of the conceptual structure of this intersection of fields, as revealed through the bibliometric mapping, is shown in two strategic diagrams (Cobo, M., M. et al. 2018) for each period (P1:2014–19 -Figure 1- and P2:2020–25 - Figure 2). The bibliometric analysis exposes distinct conceptual structures built from the co-occurrence patterns of the authors' keywords plus those of the analyzed databases at a given time. Such structure is shown by crossing two normalized and measured dimensions (Cobo, M. et al. 2018): centrality, as a cluster interaction measure; and density, as the strength of the inner patterns of co-occurrence (Ermel, A. et al., 2021, p. 181) for each cluster. This is how a two-dimensional field is created with four strategic zones:

1.  **Motor zone** (high density and high centrality) shows both, clusters with strong co-occurrence patterns and active interaction between them, “they are well developed and they are important for the structure of the research field” (Cobo, M. et al. 2018. p. 265).

2.  **Specialized** and peripheral themes (high density and low centrality) indicate highly developed themes that are isolated and have less interaction with others.

3.  **Basic** and transversal themes (low density and high centrality) point to structural and disciplinary themes highly interacting with other clusters but lacking the level of development necessary to be a motor theme.

4.  **Emergent/declining** themes (low density and low centrality) indicate clusters of keywords that are on the verge of disappearing or becoming central, motor or specialized themes.

This map enables future researchers to conduct systematic literature searches by expanding the disciplinary logics using the keywords materialized in existing articles and indexed systems instead of relying solely on those within each disciplinary paradigm, which is essential for interdisciplinary reading and interpretation. This is the subsequent course of action that will be pursued to elucidate the conceptual architecture of the field that has been revealed by the co-occurrence of keywords, a phenomenon which presents elements for comparison across different periods.

This creates a difficulty in reading, which will be better illustrated below by reviewing some clusters of interest.

In the same period the **specialized** and niche themes reveal keywords around music education, art in general, dancing with geographical references. There is also a maritime-related theme, focusing on issues such as ecological preservation, cultural heritage and social wellbeing. Other, less dense clusters, which are still specialized, include keywords such as *social innovation, sustainability* and *co-creation dynamics*, as well as *value co-creation*. This suggests that these activities are neither central nor interacting with the other keywords. In addition, identity-related clusters are, on the one hand, related to either ethnicity or race and education, and, on the other hand, to social image perceptions and standards.

**Emergent/declining** clusters are common in an interdisciplinary search as initial condition of this analysis. For this reason, the clusters in this zone must be interpreted as novel interests in the research communities or new technological developments, but also to observe keywords intrinsically related with the fields of knowledge interacting. These are not expected to evidence high interdisciplinary co-occurrences patterns. The topics here for period 1 are related to cultural heritage in critical terms with keywords such as *gentrification* (for exacerbated thematic tourism around patrimony), critical studies around race and information (as *misrepresented groups*), *experiential pedagogies, minority’s cultural heritage, participatory methodologies and Storytelling, slow data and ethnobotany* through a patrimonial approach.

**Basic themes** in period 1 present near to cero density suggesting that there are no clear central themes in the intersection of these specific fields. This is expected too as a general result although the themes positioned in this zone can elucidate potential subjects for impactful interactions in the field with keywords such as *ethnic minorities* in *national cultural heritage*, *empowerment, media literacy* (cluster 18-P1)*, multilingualism, human rights declaration* (cluster 3-P1) and *social capital, co-creation* and *hyper-connected users* (cluster 5-P1).

The 0-P1 (Figure 11) cluster’s keywords seem to have gained density from period 1 to the second as there is an almost identical cluster. It transitioned into a specialized theme (Figure 1), even though it was previously categorized in the emerging/declining zone (Figure 2). This cluster contains the most central search terms of the metadata sample, but its structure shows a new keyword: “women” besides stronger internal relationships. This keyword is also present in the P1 but lacking strong connections to be visible in the map. Its consolidation in P2 represents an important development, which will be further examined in relation to the positioning of misrepresented social groups, gender perspective and intersectionalities theme.

The general exercise shows the thematic areas that have been addressed by the studies included in the sample designed to find the direct topics of the searches, but also those that are connected by keywords to other areas of the field in order to situate themselves, with a scope, in the context of knowledge production and distribution (Bourdieu, P. 1997; Chartier, R. 2006).

Heritage issues arise from its immateriality, both in P1 and P2. The clusters refer to keywords such as *intangible cultural heritage, intangible cultural heritage protection, minority intangible cultural heritage, intangible culture*, among others. This trend could be reviewed in depth in future research intended to contribute to the digital archives dedicated to such subject.

In general, the conceptual structure of the field can be described with some trends that connect P1 and P2. Identity issues related to migration and ethnicity in P1 and specific gender issues in P2 were reviewed. Issues related to gender and intersectionality are clearer in the last five years, according to the sample material, and are present in seven clusters (P2) and, previously (P1), in only two. The maritime theme and the use of intangible cultural heritage remain possibilities for future study, as does the relationship with the theme of science communication, in the movement of keyword co-occurrence between the periods described by the sample collected.

# Positioning of transversal themes and strategic interpretation 

To identify the positioning of topics related to the HerStory NeSy Ai project, the initial search terms are reviewed, along with keywords that reveal the structures of the strategic maps. Keywords directly related to topics of interest are present in 13/20 clusters in P1 (in emerging and specialized topics) and in 16/20 clusters in P2, aligned with the reading already carried out as an overview.

From the search terms related to **(i) historical heritage and GLAM** context, it can be seen that clusters containing *intangible cultural heritage, cultural heritage*, and both *libraries* and *virtual museums* are located, for P1, in all areas, and for P2, they remain in emerging/marginal topics. This shows a widespread presence of these keywords, demonstrating the dispersion of the topic among interdisciplinary thematic groups which, according to the inclusion index measurements, do not have such strong homogenized relationships (as a single field, although internally, as already explained).

To conclude the position analysis, greater visibility was expected for keywords that are conceptually broader than those found. Although the number of co-occurrences is greater, the inclusion algorithm and the simple center algorithm applied allow those with stronger relationships to be displayed, even if they are not the exact taxonomical conceptual terms. From this, the bridge concepts mentioned as such could be analyzed.

In sum, Keywords related to (i) historical heritage and GLAM context, are located across all zones in P1, while in P2 they remain in emerging/declining themes, with presence across all clusters. As for (ii) digital humanities, data retrieval or specific type-semantic technologies, it was identified they appear in very specific clusters in both review moments, moving from the specialized to the emerging/declining, and basic zone suggesting more interaction with the subjects. For topics related to (iii) gender, minorities, and intersectionality, keywords such as ethnic minorities, minority cultural heritage, social justice, gender, women's issues, women's rights, lack of diversity, and identity are transversal themes across clusters. Same observation for (iv) UCD, human-computer interaction, co-creation, social innovation, design activism, participatory approach, usability testing, community-based participation.

Greater visibility was expected for keywords that are conceptually more encompassing than many found (as *link data, metadata, metadata aggregation, metadata model*). Although the number of co-occurrences is high, its relationships aren’t. These work as cluster bridges, which can be explored in future research.

# Conclusions 

This study demonstrates the usefulness of scientific mapping techniques for examining interdisciplinary fields that combine participatory design, semantic web technologies, GLAM contexts, and gender perspectives. By applying bibliometric analysis, it was possible to generate an overview of the conceptual structure of the field and to observe how its thematic composition has evolved over the last decade (Cobo, M. 2011b; Callon, M. et al 1991).

The analysis shows a fragmented but evolving landscape. From the first to the second period, motor themes disappear, specialized themes decrease (from three to one), and basic themes expand (from three to six). Emerging and declining themes remain relatively stable. These results indicate low levels of conceptual consolidation and a trend toward disciplinary dispersion, but they also reveal areas where important connections are strengthening.

A particularly relevant finding is the consolidation of a cluster that brings together knowledge graphs, participatory design methodologies, cultural heritage, and women. This cluster, which was previously located in the emerging/declining zone, gains density and is set as a specialized theme, confirming the increasing relevance of gender perspectives in relation to semantic technologies and participatory approaches, besides proposing more interaction with other GLAM subjects in the future. This result directly supports the objectives of the HerStory NeSyAI (2025, June 18) project and highlights its contribution to the field.

In sum, the findings provide a conceptual framework that encourages cross-disciplinary research, as needed for subjects such as participation, misrepresentation, and gender perspectives (Fricker, M. 2023; Escobar, A. 2019). This allows the articulation of different areas such as interactive information retrieval, digital humanities, and human-computer interaction (Fidel, R. 2012) through metadata representing the academic knowledge production and ultimately fostering more inclusive and gender-aware knowledge infrastructures.

## Extended abstract (structured; proposal / repository version)

*See also `Hipertext-2025_ElenaG_abstract_COMM.md` for journal-length abstract variants.*

**Central issue and research question.** The central issue is infrastructural and communicational: when participatory design, human-centred computing, semantic web technologies, and GLAM practice converge, **indexed scholarly discourse remains poorly integrated**—disciplines use divergent keywords, database taxonomies slice problems differently, and cross-cutting concerns (gender, intersectionality, misrecognition, epistemic injustice) are hard to trace at scale. That opacity matters for information and communication science because **metadata and indexing are politically consequential**: they stabilise what can be found, cited, and modelled downstream. The study’s **guiding research question** is: *How are participatory design, semantic-web and GLAM-relevant themes, and gender- or identity-related concerns **structurally positioned** in the co-word architecture of Scopus and Web of Science literature between 2014 and 2025, and **how does that architecture change** when comparing 2014–2019 and 2020–2025?* A subsidiary question, tied to the HerStory NeSy AI project (University of Barcelona), asks where **visibility for women and misrepresented groups** emerges—or fails to consolidate—in that macro-discourse.

**Theoretical and methodological approach.** **Theoretically**, the paper treats author and database keywords as a **surface of scholarly self-description** (how the field “announces” its topics to indexers and peers), linking science-mapping grammar to debates on knowledge organisation, social epistemology, and representational bias in platforms. **Methodologically**, it combines a **SALSA-oriented scoping logic** (search, appraisal, synthesis, analysis) to bound an interdisciplinary corpus with **bibliometric science mapping** in **SciMAT**. Records are drawn with proximity operators, wildcards, and field-tagged queries from **Scopus** (*n* = 2,657 before merge) and **Web of Science** (*n* = 613), 2014–2025; **3,250** unique items remain after deduplication. Normalised keyword **co-occurrence** networks are partitioned into clusters whose strategic roles are read through **Callon’s centrality and density** framework (motor, specialised, basic/transversal, emergent/declining zones), compared across two quinquennia. This design privileges **replicability and transparency** over subjective cluster naming: clusters are reported via their keyword contents.

**Anticipated conclusions and contributions.** The analysis **expects**—and the full paper corroborates—a **persistently fragmented** landscape: many clusters fall in emergent/declining quadrants, indicating weak thematic consolidation at the intersection under study. **Temporal contrast** should show **fewer motor themes**, thinner specialisation, and **more basic and emergent configurations** in 2020–2025 than in 2014–2019, consistent with diversification across contributing fields rather than a single paradigmatic core. **Substantively**, identity-related discourse is anticipated to **shift** from an emphasis on ethnicity, migration, and minority heritage toward **more visible gender, feminism, and marginalisation** vocabularies, spread across **more clusters** in the later window—evidence of gradual repositioning in indexed talk. Patterns around digital humanities, ontologies, linked data, and knowledge graphs are expected to **align partially** with recent domain reviews, lending external plausibility. The headline **empirical contribution** is structural: denser co-occurrence linking knowledge graphs, participatory design, cultural heritage, and the keyword *women*, with that bundle moving toward a more specialised strategic niche—**bibliometric evidence** that gender is gaining traction in the same lexical layer later reused in retrieval and analytics. For **information and communication science**, anticipated **contributions** are: (1) a **workflow** for mapping interdisciplinary contact zones via metadata; (2) an **interpretive bridge** between co-word cartography and critique of knowledge infrastructures; (3) an **empirical baseline** for project-led interventions (HerStory NeSy AI) on fairer description and navigation. **Limitations** remain those of keyword studies (noise, indexing artefacts, Anglophone skew); **future work** should triangulate with full text, citation milieux, and institutional geography.
