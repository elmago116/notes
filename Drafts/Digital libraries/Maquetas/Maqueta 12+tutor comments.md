## 1. Introduction

Digital libraries have evolved from technical repositories to human-centered ecosystems requiring integration of semantic web technologies with participatory methodologies in the design of its knowledge architectures. However, the methodological landscape of how users are integrated into semantic web projects for digital libraries remains underexplored, and even more, the misrepresented voices in terms of gender perspectives, intersectionalities and other types. While individual projects demonstrate various approaches—from participatory design workshops to crowdsourcing platforms—there is a lack of systematic analysis of methodological patterns across the field.

This study addresses this gap through a bibliometric mapping analysis that identifies projects at the intersection of semantic web technologies, participatory design, citizen science, and human-computer interaction in digital library and GLAM contexts. The bibliometric study provides the foundation for identifying and analyzing projects methodologically, examining how users are integrated, when in the process, and through what methods.

## 2. Research Methodology

This study presents a methodological analysis of use-cases of co-researching/designing user-centered semantic or NeSy AI systems in digital libraries identified through a SALSA scoping framework integrated by qualitative and quantitative methods such as use-case analysis and a science mapping - bibliometric technique applied to a systematically designed sample of academic production from an interdisciplinary point of view.

### 2.1 The Four-Dimensional Analytical Framework

The first (i) is a ==research/design process-driven dimension==. This is composed of two perspectives: one sits on the technological development point of view including the iterative process of systems design, development, evaluation, technology deployment and its ongoing monitoring besides an evaluation framework of the participative effectiveness. The second perspective seeks elements from a research and knowledge production framework and citizen science. This includes the research phases in which citizens contribute, such as research design, information gathering, field work, analysis, science communication and knowledge transference and its impact evaluation; and the research form being "top-down" or "bottom up".

The second dimension (ii) is concerned with the ==methodological tools== employed in the research/design process, such as participatory design workshops, co-design sessions, user testing and interface evaluation, crowdsourcing, annotation and curation, among others. Other tools that can be found include the models of citizen engagement on science (collective intelligence, pooling of resources, data collection, analysis tasks, serious games, participatory experiments, grassroots activities).

The third dimension (iii) brings together the ==levels of user participation and engagement== in the research process (scientific, inspirational, educational, social, economic, environmental, political community agency). On the other side, the participation levels within a design process can be understood by consulting, including, elaborating and owning the object of development.

Finally the fourth methodologically comprehensive dimension (iv) is related with the presence or absence of ==components related to representational gaps and epistemic justice== in terms of gender, intersectionalities, censorships, bias recognition and mitigation, knowledge diversity or power dynamics awareness of any kind.

Finally, the **synthesis phase** this study offers a panoramic view of designing effective, inclusive, and sustainable systems with four elements: a project inventory as a comprehensive catalog of projects with semantic-participatory integrations; a classification framework with a systematic approach to understanding their user integration patterns and epistemic justice perspectives; a compilation of methodological foundations to analyze and apply participatory design, citizen science, HCI, and epistemic justice approaches; and a research agenda with identified gaps and future directions for methodological development, with particular attention to gender perspectives, intersectionality, and representation of marginalized voices analyzed in their own semantic technological context (knowledge graphs, ontologies, semantic portals, neurosymbolic AI).

**4. Epistemic Justice Perspective:**
- **Gender Perspectives:** Explicit attention to gender representation, gender-inclusive design, mitigation of gender bias
- Sandra Harding- objetividad fuerte
- **Intersectionality:** Consideration of multiple intersecting identities (gender, race, class, ability, etc.)
- **Bias Effects Mitigation:** Processes for identifying and addressing bias in knowledge organization, classification, or representation
- **Community Agency:** Extent to which communities control their own representation, narratives, and knowledge systems
- **Recognition of Diverse Knowledge Systems:** Acknowledgment and integration of non-Western, Indigenous, or marginalized knowledge traditions
- **Power Dynamics:** Attention to power relations in knowledge production, who gets to speak, and whose knowledge is valued
- **Not Explicitly Addressed:** Projects that do not explicitly engage with epistemic justice concerns

### List of use-cases: description

1. **Yanyuwa Comic Project:** [[Data Storytelling on Multi-modal Knowledge graph via data comics]] - Co-construction workshops for language teachers; iterative, session-based collaboration that supports future teacher involvement (collaborate/empower).
2. **World Literature Knowledge Graph:** [[The World Literature Knowledge Graph]] - Integration of three reader communities into a shared semantic model; ongoing use and reception data (involve/collaborate).
3. **PhD Students' Information-Sharing Knowledge Graph:** [[It answers questions that I didn't know I had" PhD students' evaluation of an information sharing knowledge graph]] [[Community design of a knowledge graph to support interdisciplinary PhD Students.pdf]] - Participatory design workshop with PhD students plus mixed-methods evaluation (interviews and sentiment analysis); strong participatory design + HCI linkage (collaborate).
4. **Research Knowledge Graphs for Participatory Archives:** [[Metadata and Semantic Research - Conference.pdf]] - Archivists and researchers customize and enrich school archives; long-term collaborative curation (collaborate/ongoing community curation).
5. **KnowEdu:** [[KnowEdu_ A System to Construct Knowledge Graph for Education – DOAJ.pdf]] - Explicit user-centred design involving experts and users; focus on iterative refinement of educational knowledge representations (consult/involve).
6. **Polariscope Collaborative Storytelling Platform:** [[Remixing and repurposing cultural heritage archives through a collaborative and AI-generated storytelling digital platform]]- Collaborative storytelling platform that gathers citizens and institutions to co-create narrative visualizations over cultural heritage archives, combining AI-generated stories with user participation and user testing (involve/collaborate).
7. **GTDOnto:** [[GTDOnto- An Ontology for Organizing and Modeling Knowledge about Global Terrorism.pdf]] - NeOn methodology with UX aspects in a sensitive terrorism domain; expert-led design with attention to user tasks and potential bias issues (consult, bias-aware modelling).
8. **DigiNUMA:** #op/acc/download - Explicit participatory heritage component; collaboration between heritage professionals and communities around numismatic and archaeological data (involve/collaborate).
9. **SAMPO Model Projects:** [[Digital humanities on the Semantic Web - Sampo model and portal series.pdf]] - User-oriented semantic portals emphasizing exploration and reuse; interaction via configurable views and faceted exploration, without formal participatory design (inform/involve through rich exploration tools).
10. **Wikibase for Cultural Heritage:** #op/acc/download - Collaborative editing environment where non-expert users can contribute; continuous crowdsourced enrichment and correction (community curation, ongoing collaboration).
11. **Cultural Leaf Semantic Portal:** [[Cultural Leaf - a LOD portal for exploring the cultural heritage.pdf]] - Usability-focused portal design; interaction framed through exploratory use and (implicitly) usability considerations, though evaluation processes are under-documented (inform/involve).
12. **European Cultural Gems:** [[Cultural gems linked open data- Mapping culture and intangible heritage in European cities.pdf]] - Crowdsourced platform where citizens contribute information about cultural and creative places; ongoing citizen-contribution model with linked open data backend (crowdsourcing / citizen participation).
13. **ARCA:** [[Design, realization, and user evaluation of the ARCA system for exploring a digital library.pdf]] - User-centred, iterative semantic search system; two releases with comparative usability testing position users as evaluators and co-shapers of functionality (HCI evaluation; involve/collaborate).
14. **InTaVia:** [[The_Multiple_Faces_of_Cultural_Heritage_Towards_an_Integrated_Visualization_Platform_for_Tangible_and_Intangible_Cultural_Assets.pdf]] [[The InTaVia Knowledge Graph–European National Biographical and Cultural Heritage Object Data.pdf]] - Requirement workshops and user testing on the interface side, plus empirical validation; strong HCI orientation with structured user-centred design across design and evaluation phases (collaborate).
15. **ATLAS:** [[ATLAS- Towards a Knowledge Graph of International Scholarly Research on the Italian Digital Cultural Heritage.pdf]] - Supports participatory research on Italian digital cultural heritage; researchers and possibly practitioners co-construct and interrogate the knowledge graph (collaborate within expert communities).
16. **Neurosymbolic Narrative Generation for Cultural Heritage:** [[Neurosymbolic Narrative Generation for Cultural Heritage.pdf]] - Emphasizes user empowerment in narrative generation; users move from consumers to co-authors by controlling narrative parameters (empower, HCI and participatory design blend).
17. **ArsEmotica:** #op/acc/download - Uses social media comments as user-generated emotional metadata; large-scale, implicit crowdsourcing of affective responses to art (crowdsourcing / community contribution).
18. **Participatory Indexing for Academic Digital Libraries:** [[Participatory Indexing in the Eyes of Its Potential Users- An Example of a Co-design of Participatory Services in an Academic Digital Library.pdf]] - Co-design workshops with potential users to prototype participatory indexing services; strong participatory design in shaping indexing workflows (collaborate/empower around metadata).
19. **ProvKOS:** [[A conceptual model for tracking the provenance of activities in knowledge organization systems.pdf]] - Five-step participatory process engaging editorial managers, information professionals, and researchers to define provenance tracking; interaction focuses on negotiating controversial classifications and bias mitigation (collaborate).
20. **Inuvialuit Digital Library:** #op/acc/download - Community-led co-design, digital storytelling workshops, and contributor-controlled narratives; strong community agency and culturally grounded access rules (collaborate/empower; epistemic justice-centred).
21. **HyperReal:** #op/acc/download - Explicit participatory design of multivocal exhibitions; users act as co-curators, shaping narrative and interpretive layers over time (collaborate/empower).
22. **Rhizomer:** [[Rhizomer- Interactive semantic knowledge graphs exploration]] - Interactive tool for exploring knowledge graphs available as Semantic Web data; supports overview, zoom and filter, and details-on-demand tasks without requiring prior knowledge of dataset structure or SPARQL; user-centered interface design for researchers, practitioners, and lay users (inform/involve through exploration tools).

## 4. Methodological Analysis

Synthesized from the project table and justifications across all batches.

### 4.1 Process-Driven Dimension

The process-driven dimension examines when in the development lifecycle users are integrated into semantic and NeSy AI systems for digital libraries, distinguishing between early-stage co-design, late-stage evaluation, constant engagement, and cases where user integration is not explicitly documented. Analysis of the 22 use-cases is non-exclusive across phases: seven projects integrate users early in development, five engage users primarily during late evaluation, and six report constant engagement (e.g., ongoing crowdsourcing or continuous co-curation), while four do not explicitly document the participation phase. Constant participation doesn't mean necessarily that users take part in the whole project's phases, this can be due to a process that accepts contributions continuously but in a very specific way; such as Polariscope, that is presented as an online community for everyone to contribute although the users participated mainly by testing the tool in the final design stages [[Clippings/Remixing and repurposing cultural heritage archives through a collaborative and AI-generated storytelling digital platform |(Pedro Almeida et al., 2024)]]. Another example is *PhD Students' Information-Sharing KG* (design + evaluation). This process integrates strategically interviews for understanding the system requirements, and sentiment analysis for evaluation [[Clippings/It answers questions that I didn't know I had" PhD students' evaluation of an information sharing knowledge graph| (Gardasevic & Lamba, 2024)]]. 

The distribution reflects a predominance of design-led approaches (15 projects) over research-driven or research process-driven methodologies (7 projects), with design-led projects more frequently incorporating early user integration compared to research-oriented initiatives that tend toward constant monitoring or late-stage evaluation. Early integration is one sign of treating users as partners rather than objects of study enabling shared ideation and early co-creation; although is not sufficient to the case. Leaving users as passive subjects risks expert-only assumptions, missing contextual needs and sustainable engagement for diverse communities [[Co-creation and the new landscapes of design.pdf#page=2|(Stappers & Sanders, 2008)]]. This partnership stance is also necessary to confront HCAI challenges—inscrutable models, embedded bias against minorities, privacy risks, and “illusions of meaning”—that arise when AI systems act on personal and trace data without accountable co-design [[What is Human-Centered about Human-Centered AI_ A Map of the Research Landscape _ Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems.pdf#page=1|(Capel & Brereton, 2023)]]. 

### 4.2 Methodological Tools and Models

The methodological tools and models dimension identifies the strategic use of techniques, frameworks, and approaches employed to integrate users into semantic and NeSy AI systems for digital libraries, revealing a diverse toolkit ranging from structured participatory workshops to automated knowledge extraction pipelines. 

Analysis of the 22 use-cases demonstrates six primary methodological clusters: participatory and co-design approaches, which utilize co-construction workshops, participatory design processes, and community-led co-design sessions; user testing and user-centered design methods, which incorporate usability testing, requirement workshops, iterative test phases, and user-centered interface design; crowdsourcing and user-generated content strategies, which leverage collaborative editing environments, crowdsourced platforms, and implicit crowdsourcing through social media data collection. 

Additionally, ontology and knowledge graph engineering methodologies represent structured technical development processes, integrating data description, annotation-based customization, characterization via crowdsourcing tools, and hackathon-based prototyping. The neuro-symbolic and AI-assisted methods integrate derivative narratives, creativity, and knowledge appropriation into participatory workflows, there is a study suggesting that this could diminish the cognitive load and promoting creativity [[Human_AI_Co_Creation_A_Framework_for_Collaborative_Design_in_Intelligent_Systems|(Liu, Z., 2025)]].

Yet most approaches still lack robust participation evaluation (use-cases report efficiency/impact evaluations beyond usability/IR metrics—impact on participation remains under-documented). Design research also cautions that without explicit evaluation, users slip back into “subjects” observed by experts instead of co-creators steering goals and workflows, undermining the adaptive, user-driven exploration envisioned for future digital libraries [[How-Should-One-Explore-the-Digital-Library-of-_2021_Data-and-Information-Man.pdf#page=3&rect=34,489,365,735|(Fox & Chandrasekar, 2021)]].

### 4.3 Levels of User Participation

The levels of user participation dimension maps the spectrum of user consultation and decision-making power and ownership [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice|(Fernando Delgado et al., 2023)]] within semantic and NeSy AI systems for digital libraries, ranging from just knowledge building and scientific sphere to full community empowerment and political agency [[White Paper on Citizen Science for Europe|(Stephanie MATT, 2015)]] [[The Science of Citizen Science|(Katrin Vohland et al., 2021)]]. 

Analysis of the 22 use-cases reveals a concentration of projects at consultative and collaborative levels, with fewer instances of true empowerment or community agency models. Projects classified at the consult level position users primarily as informants whose feedback shapes system design but who do not participate in core decision-making processes, while projects at the involve/collaborate level (Polariscope Collaborative Storytelling Platform) engage users as active partners in co-creation activities. Citizen science literature argues that genuine co-production aims for transformative and democratic renewal rather than consultative feedback alone [[The science of citizen science.pdf#page=126&annotation=27918R|(Vohland et al., 2021)]] but this is only possible in a situated knowledge context [[Situated learning _ legitimate peripheral participation _ Lave, Jean _ Free Download, Borrow, and Streaming _ Internet Archive| (Leave, J. Wenger, E. 1991)]]

The most participatory projects operate at the collaborate/empower level (Yanyuwa Comic Project, PhD Students' Information-Sharing Knowledge Graph, ProvKOS, HyperReal, and Neurosymbolic Narrative Generation), where users co-design system components, control narrative parameters, or participate in governance decisions. A distinct category emerges with projects that frame participation through scientific, educational, or social engagement models (Research Knowledge Graphs for Participatory Archives, DigiNUMA, Participatory Indexing, ATLAS, Wikibase for Cultural Heritage, and European Cultural Gems), which emphasize knowledge production, learning outcomes, or community contribution rather than explicit empowerment frameworks. Notably, two projects (Inuvialuit Digital Library and ArsEmotica) explicitly adopt political community agency perspectives, positioning users as co-owners of knowledge representation and cultural memory, with Inuvialuit Digital Library demonstrating the strongest community-led governance model through culturally appropriate access rules and community-determined metadata frameworks.

### 4.4 Representational Gaps and Epistemic Justice
The representational gaps and epistemic justice dimension assesses how projects surface or mitigate power, bias, and inclusion issues in semantic/NeSy AI for digital libraries [[A Knowledge Graph of Contentious Terminology for Inclusive Representation of Cultural Heritage|(Nesterov, A. et. al.2023)]]. Evidence from this study shows only a small subset makes it explicit in some way. Yanyuwa-comics case treats with indigenous language revitalization; althugh focused on students none of them were involved. Another case, Inuvialuit Digital Library (community-controlled access and narratives) did work with its communities, and HyperReal experiments with platforms for multivocal curation of contents. Without the formal multi-actor workflow integration advocated by Fox and Chandrasekar—explicitly linking UX researchers, subject matter experts, and developers into extensible knowledge-graph-driven workflows—digital libraries fail to enable adaptive self-organization and tailored exploration for diverse stakeholders [[How-Should-One-Explore-the-Digital-Library-of-_2021_Data-and-Information-Man.pdf#page=3&rect=34,489,365,735|(Fox & Chandrasekar, 2021)]] but still, the processes made contribute with a perspective and learnings from its own limitations for the strenghtening of the relationship between knowledge and societal relevance [[Science Communication Culture, Identity and Citizenship|(Davis, S. & Horst, M. 2016)]].

Bias-aware modelling appears in GTDOnto (sensitive terrorism domain) and ProvKOS (tracking controversial classifications), while a few projects signal representation aims (e.g., World Literature KG foregrounding non-Western authors). This can be understood ad Haraway’s situated knowledges; arguing that there is no “view from nowhere,” demanding accountable, positioned description practices governed with those represented [[Donna Haraway_ Story Telling for Earthly Survival|(Haraway, 2016)]] 

Most use-cases provide no explicit representational gaps framing (KnowEdu, ARCA, InTaVia, SAMPO, Wikibase, DigiNUMA, European Cultural Gems, ArsEmotica, Rhizomer, ATLAS, Polariscope, Neurosymbolic Narrative Generation), highlighting a gap between participatory rhetoric and concrete safeguards such as gender/intersectionality audits, provenance of contested labels, or community governance over description practices—elements emphasized in the methodological framework’s call for bias mitigation, culturally grounded metadata, and participatory control over meaning-making.

These practices need to be strengthened as Fricker’s (2011) account of epistemic injustice: testimonial credibility deficits and hermeneutical gaps persist when marginalized groups cannot contest or define classifications, leaving their knowledge underrepresented and misinterpreted—precisely the risk when epistemic justice safeguards are absent [[Epistemic Injustice  Power and the Ethics of Knowing.pdf#page=1|(Fricker, 2011)]]; while Stengers & Despret call for epistemic disobedience so communities are not merely “spoken for” but co-author classifications and meaning [[Women Who Make a Fuss  The Unfaithful Daughters of Virginia Woolf on JSTOR|(Stengers & Despret, 2014)]]. No specific methodological processes, tools or engagement strategies were reported to operationalize these epistemic justice safeguards through participation.

## 8. Conclusion

This use-case study covered 25 projects integrating semantic/AI technologies with participatory approaches in digital library and similars in GLAM contexts. The classification by technology, integration moment, method, and epistemic justice enabled a four-dimensional analysis (process, tools, participation level, representational gaps and epistemic justice).

Findings: design-led efforts dominate; early co-design is limited to a few cases (Yanyuwa, PhD KG, Inuvialuit, InTaVia, ARCA). Evaluation is sparse beyond usability/IR metrics, leaving participation impact unclear. Epistemic justice is explicit mainly in community-led projects (Yanyuwa, Inuvialuit, HyperReal); most lack gender/intersectionality/bias treatment. Crowdsourcing cases rarely document quality control or motivation.

For practitioners and researchers, this study offers:
1. **Project Inventory:** Consolidated catalog of 25 semantic-participatory initiatives.
2. **Classification Framework:** Systematic view of user integration patterns, methods, and EJ attention.
3. **Methodological Insights:** What is present (workshops, testing, UCD, crowdsourcing) and what is missing (early co-design, impact evaluation, EJ checks).
4. **Action Agenda:** Add participatory phases to KG/ontology builds, pair them with lightweight evaluation, and embed EJ reviews before release.

As digital libraries evolve toward human-centered ecosystems, strengthening participatory design, measurable evaluation, and explicit epistemic justice will be key to inclusive and sustainable systems across CS, LIS, DH, and user communities.


## 9. Lessons Learned, Good Practices, and Methodological Principles

This section synthesizes lessons learned, good practices, and methodological principles extracted from the documentation and analysis of the use-cases examined in this study. These insights are drawn from the linked sources and project documentation referenced throughout this document.

### 9.1 Participatory Design and Co-Construction Principles

**Iterative, Session-Based Collaboration:** The Yanyuwa Comic Project [[Data Storytelling on Multi-modal Knowledge graph via data comics]] demonstrates the effectiveness of structured co-construction workshops with iterative, session-based collaboration. The project employed six design workshop sessions that progressively built participants' capacity: from introductory sessions on data comics fundamentals, through design objective clarification, sketching practice, story selection, prototyping, to final presentation and discussion. This structured progression enabled knowledge graph engineers to translate technical knowledge into accessible visual narratives for non-technical stakeholders.

**Design Guidelines for Knowledge Representation:** The Yanyuwa project established five design guidelines (G1-G5) that proved essential: (G1) Visual perception—visually explain and engage audiences about multi-modal knowledge stories; (G2) Gestalt Principles—support recognition via coherent designs based on proximity and continuity; (G3) Affordances—inform use cases and guide decision-making; (G4) Cultural context—produce accurate representation with traceability; (G5) Cultural context—explore potential for engaging material design. These guidelines balanced technical requirements with cultural authenticity and user engagement.

**Five-Step Participatory Process:** ProvKOS [[A conceptual model for tracking the provenance of activities in knowledge organization systems.pdf]] demonstrates a systematic five-step approach: (1) content analysis of editorial data; (2) environmental scanning of existing provenance models; (3) developing general provenance questions; (4) participatory design with stakeholders; and (5) developing persona-specific provenance queries. This dual bottom-up and top-down strategy ensures both theoretical grounding and practical applicability, engaging editorial managers, information professionals, and researchers throughout the process.

**Co-Design Workshops with Prototyping:** Participatory Indexing for Academic Digital Libraries [[Participatory Indexing in the Eyes of Its Potential Users- An Example of a Co-design of Participatory Services in an Academic Digital Library.pdf]] emphasizes the value of co-design workshops where potential users prototype participatory indexing services. The approach enables users to shape indexing workflows collaboratively, moving beyond consultation to active collaboration and empowerment around metadata creation.

### 9.2 User-Centered Design and Evaluation Practices

**Iterative Development with Comparative Testing:** ARCA [[Design, realization, and user evaluation of the ARCA system for exploring a digital library.pdf]] exemplifies iterative user-centered design through two releases with incremental evaluation. The first release evaluated extracted data quality through expert analysis, while the second release underwent comparative usability testing with other systems. This two-phase approach allowed refinement based on initial feedback while maintaining objectivity through comparative evaluation. The system's modular architecture, separating semantic enrichment pipeline from user interface, facilitated maintenance and adaptation.

**Mixed-Methods Evaluation:** The PhD Students' Information-Sharing Knowledge Graph [[It answers questions that I didn't know I had" PhD students' evaluation of an information sharing knowledge graph]] combined participatory design workshops with mixed-methods evaluation including semi-structured interviews and sentiment analysis. This approach captured both quantitative patterns and qualitative insights, providing comprehensive understanding of user experience and system effectiveness.

**Requirement Workshops and Empirical Validation:** InTaVia [[The_Multiple_Faces_of_Cultural_Heritage_Towards_an_Integrated_Visualization_Platform_for_Tangible_and_Intangible_Cultural_Assets.pdf]] demonstrates the value of requirement workshops combined with user testing and empirical validation. The project engaged 41 participants (historians, cultural scientists, GLAM practitioners, technicians) across design and evaluation phases, ensuring that both functional requirements and usability concerns were addressed systematically.

**Contextual Help and Tutorial Systems:** ARCA's evaluation revealed that initial exploration can be difficult without viewing tutorials, suggesting the importance of contextual help systems. Users took longer to navigate ARCA than other tools, indicating both greater complexity and greater exploratory interest. The recommendation is to implement lighter alternatives to video tutorials, such as help components that accompany users in first searches, making them independent in exploiting system possibilities.

### 9.3 Community Engagement and Cultural Appropriateness

**Community-Driven Co-Design:** The Inuvialuit Digital Library [[Community-Driven Knowledge Organization for Cultural Heritage Digital Libraries The Case of the Inuvialuit Settlement Region]] demonstrates deep, long-term community partnerships. The project employed diverse engagement methods: 15 interviews, 68 surveys, 3 demonstrations at high school, 6 information tables at community events, 6 open houses, and participant observation across five communities. This multi-method approach ensured broad community input while respecting cultural communication preferences.

**Culturally Appropriate Metadata Structures:** The Inuvialuit project established several critical metadata principles: (1) **Language and dialect representation**—capturing Inuvialuktun and its three dialects (Kangiryuarmiutun, Siglitun, Uummarmiutun) as essential knowledge organization elements; (2) **Geographic names**—using both traditional/Indigenous names and colonial names, recognizing both as part of history (e.g., Akłarvik/Aklavik, Inuuvik/Inuvik); (3) **Contributor roles**—recognizing diverse roles beyond standard creator distinctions (storytellers, translators, performers, drummers, dancers); (4) **Browse-first interfaces**—prioritizing visual browse interfaces over search-only systems, incorporating icons and visual elements.

**Community-Determined Knowledge Organization:** The Inuvialuit project learned that teachers use seasons as organizational schemes in classrooms, suggesting alternative browsing and filtering approaches. The community expressed strong interest in geographic organization, wanting to select places on maps and see all associated items, not just locate single items. This demonstrates the importance of flexible, community-responsive organizational structures rather than imposing standard library classification schemes.

**Culturally Grounded Access Rules:** The project implemented contributor-controlled narratives and culturally appropriate access rules, ensuring community agency over representation. This approach recognizes that knowledge organization systems must respect cultural protocols and community ownership of knowledge, particularly for Indigenous and marginalized communities.

### 9.4 Knowledge Graph and Semantic Technology Principles

**Traceability and Authenticity:** The Yanyuwa project emphasized traceability as a core advantage of knowledge graphs. The hierarchical document-section-atomic structure enables encoded knowledge to be efficiently traceable to original sources. Any extensive use of knowledge can be easily referred back to KG entities via retrieval, ensuring authenticity and enhancing accountability. The project explicitly avoided inference-based approaches (e.g., GPT) that might produce novel relations not traceable to original data, compromising authenticity.

**Multi-Modal Knowledge Alignment:** Knowledge graphs enable semantic alignment between different modalities (audio, video, images, text). For example, atomic entities from video animations can be extracted from corresponding song texts, establishing one-to-one connections. Images from encyclopedias can be associated with stories and vocabulary terms, creating rich contextual relationships that support language learning and cultural preservation.

**Modular Semantic Enrichment Pipelines:** ARCA demonstrates the value of configurable semantic enrichment pipelines with pluggable components. The system uses RDF throughout, employing existing vocabularies (SKOS, FOAF, Schema.org, MADS/RDF, Dublin Core, DBO) while creating domain-specific extensions when needed. This approach balances standardization with domain-specific requirements, facilitating interoperability while maintaining flexibility.

**Visual Exploration and Serendipity:** ARCA's graph navigation supports serendipitous discovery, enabling users to start from any entity and explore connections. The system distinguishes between main topics (top_concept) and regular topics (concept), allowing users to filter by relevance. The trace path feature identifies books connected to multiple selected entities, supporting complex research queries. Users appreciated the ability to observe connections between books and find arguments in common, facilitating interdisciplinary research.

### 9.5 Epistemic Justice and Bias Mitigation

**Provenance Tracking for Controversial Classifications:** ProvKOS demonstrates how provenance tracking can address bias in knowledge organization systems. The model's "warrant" class documents the evidence and rationale behind changes, enabling tracking of controversial classifications (e.g., "gender dysphoria" in DDC). This approach makes editorial decisions transparent and allows for critical examination of how classifications reflect or challenge social norms over time.

**Community Control Over Representation:** Both the Yanyuwa and Inuvialuit projects emphasize contributor-controlled narratives and community agency. The Yanyuwa project addressed concerns about authenticity by establishing design guidelines that prioritize accurate representation with traceability (G4), while recognizing that narrative interpretation involves subjective elements. The Inuvialuit project implemented culturally grounded access rules and community-determined metadata structures, ensuring that communities control how their knowledge is represented and accessed.

**Bias-Aware Modelling in Sensitive Domains:** GTDOnto [[GTDOnto- An Ontology for Organizing and Modeling Knowledge about Global Terrorism.pdf]] demonstrates attention to bias issues in sensitive domains. The project employed NeOn methodology with UX aspects, incorporating expert review and competency questions to evaluate the ontology. While user integration was limited, the project recognized the need for bias-aware modelling in terrorism-related knowledge organization.

**Multivocal Approaches:** HyperReal and the Yanyuwa project both emphasize multivocal approaches that recognize multiple perspectives and community agency. HyperReal enables user-curated exhibitions where users act as co-curators, shaping narrative and interpretive layers over time. This approach challenges single-authority knowledge systems and supports epistemic justice by recognizing diverse knowledge traditions and perspectives.

### 9.6 Interface Design and Interaction Patterns

**Balance Exploration with Accessibility:** ARCA's graph navigation enables serendipitous discovery but requires contextual help systems. The Inuvialuit Digital Library prioritizes browse interfaces with visual elements over search-only systems. Both approaches recognize that different interaction patterns serve different user needs: exploration for discovery, browse for familiar content, search for specific queries.

**Supporting Multiple Search Behaviors:** ARCA was designed to support five general search behaviors: (1) finding documents relevant to a specific topic; (2) expanding or specializing searches by moving through related topics; (3) having visibility of available related resources; (4) visually organizing found resources by relationships; (5) finding topics and documents at the crossing of multiple topics. This comprehensive approach recognizes that library searching involves complex cognitive processes beyond single topic-based queries.

**Faceted Exploration Without Technical Knowledge:** Rhizomer [[Rhizomer- Interactive semantic knowledge graphs exploration]] demonstrates that knowledge graph exploration can be made accessible to non-technical users. The system supports overview, zoom and filter, and details-on-demand tasks without requiring prior knowledge of dataset structure or SPARQL. This principle of hiding technical complexity while enabling powerful exploration is essential for inclusive semantic web applications.

### 9.7 Methodological Synthesis

**Early and Continuous User Integration:** Projects that integrated users early and continuously (Yanyuwa, PhD KG, InTaVia, ARCA, Inuvialuit) demonstrated stronger alignment with user needs and higher satisfaction. Early integration enables requirements to be shaped by actual user contexts rather than assumptions, while continuous integration allows iterative refinement based on real usage patterns.

**Diverse Stakeholder Engagement:** ProvKOS engaged three distinct stakeholder groups (editorial managers, information professionals, researchers) with persona-specific queries, recognizing that different user roles have different information needs. This approach ensures that system design addresses the full spectrum of potential users rather than optimizing for a single user type.

**Evaluation Beyond Usability Metrics:** While most projects report usability or IR-style metrics, few document participation impact or long-term effectiveness. Projects like ARCA that employed comparative testing and mixed-methods evaluation provide more comprehensive understanding of system effectiveness. The field would benefit from more systematic evaluation of participation impact, community agency outcomes, and epistemic justice effects.

**Flexibility and Adaptability:** Successful projects demonstrate modular architectures that facilitate adaptation across contexts. ARCA's separation of semantic enrichment pipeline and user interface, the Inuvialuit project's flexible metadata framework accommodating community-determined structures, and ProvKOS's generalizable five-step process all emphasize building systems that can evolve and adapt rather than rigid, fixed solutions.

These lessons learned, drawn from the actual implementation and evaluation of the use-cases examined in this study, provide practical guidance for future semantic-participatory digital library projects. They emphasize the importance of early community engagement, iterative design with evaluation, cultural appropriateness, epistemic justice, and flexible technical architectures that support rather than constrain user agency and community control.

### 9.8 Synthesis of Lessons Learned (300 words)

Across the use-cases, a coherent methodological pattern emerges that links participatory design, user-centered evaluation, epistemic justice, and semantic technologies into a mutually reinforcing ecosystem. Iterative, session-based co-design processes such as the Yanyuwa workshops (Liang et al., 2024) show how carefully scaffolded collaboration can translate highly technical knowledge graph structures into accessible, culturally grounded narratives, while ProvKOS (Choi & Cheng, 2024) demonstrates that similarly structured, multi-step participatory processes are effective for abstract modelling tasks like provenance tracking. Community-led projects such as the Inuvialuit Digital Library (Farnel et al., 2019) extend this logic by combining diverse engagement formats with community-determined metadata, browsing structures, and access rules, foregrounding local epistemologies over inherited library schemes.

On the evaluation side, ARCA (Bernasconi et al., 2022), the PhD Students' Knowledge Graph (Gardasevic & Lamba, 2024), and InTaVia (Mayr et al., 2022) illustrate that mixed methods—comparative usability testing, interviews, and sentiment analysis—are necessary to understand both effectiveness and experience. These projects also show that modular architectures (e.g., ARCA’s separation of semantic pipeline and UI) are key to sustaining iterative design.

Technically, the cases converge on principles of traceability, multi-modal alignment, and modularity: Yanyuwa’s insistence on source-level traceability, ARCA’s RDF-based enrichment pipelines, and Rhizomer’s task-oriented faceted exploration (García et al., 2022) collectively argue for semantic infrastructures that expose complexity without overwhelming users. Finally, across sensitive and marginalized domains, GTDOnto’s bias-aware ontology construction and the multivocal exhibition model of HyperReal (Sartini, 2025) underline that epistemic justice is best operationalized when community agency, provenance transparency, and plural perspectives are designed in from the outset rather than retrofitted as afterthoughts.
