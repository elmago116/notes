# Title: Cases for cultural heritage and epistemic justice in the design of semantic and neurosymbolic intelligence systems from a user-centred perspective.

# Abstract:

**Purpose**
The general purpose is to analyze the ways in which users and communities are integrated into the development of semantic and neurosymbolic Ai projects in digital libraries to identify methodological trends, gaps and opportunities for future transdisciplinary collaboration with particular attention to gender perspectives, intersectionality, and representation of marginalized voices.

**Design/methodology/approach**
This study presents a methodological analysis of use-cases of co-researching/designing user-centered semantic/neurosymbolic systems in digital libraries identified through a SALSA scoping framework integrated by qualitative and quantitative methods such as use-case and bibliometric keyword analysis applied to a systematically designed sample of academic production from an interdisciplinary point of view.

**Findings**
Design-led projects dominate; only a few cases embed early co-design. Evaluation beyond usability/IR metrics is scarce. Epistemic justice is explicit mainly in community-led cases; most lack gender/intersectionality and bias checks; crowdsourcing seldom documents quality control or motivation.

**Originality**
By mapping who is involved, when, how, and with which attention to epistemic justice dimension, the analysis provides a structured lens for assessing the inclusiveness and accountability of current approaches. The resulting framework is intended both as an analytic tool for researchers and as a pragmatic guide for practitioners who design, evaluate, or govern semantic infrastructures in libraries, supporting future projects that embed co-design, citizen science, and bias-aware modelling from the outset.

**Keywords:** User-centered design, Participatory design, citizen science, human-computer interaction, epistemic justice, digital libraries, bibliometric analysis, SALSA, semantic web technologies, knowledge graphs, methodological analysis, gender-inclusive design.

# Introduction:

Digital libraries have shifted from technical repositories to socio-technical ecosystems where semantic and neurosymbolic AI must serve diverse, situated communities. “User-centered” is therefore a complex, context-dependent task rather than a fixed recipe: roles range from co-designers to annotators, evaluators, or data subjects, and responsibilities span usability, provenance, bias mitigation, and epistemic justice. Use-case research offers a pragmatic lens to see how methods, roles, and power interplay across concrete projects. Participatory indexing experiments show co-design can surface needs while exposing tensions between expert vocabularies and lived practices [[Clippings/Participatory Indexing in the Eyes of Its Potential Users- An Example of a Co-design of Participatory Services in an Academic Digital Library|(Leblanc, 2020)]]. Future-oriented Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) work argues that exploration support requires tightly coupling UX researchers, domain experts, and developers within knowledge-graph-driven workflows to avoid brittle, designer-only models [[How Should One Explore the Digital Library of the Future?|(Fox & Chandrasekar, 2021)]]. Symbolic-AI evaluation reminds that human control must be preserved alongside system autonomy to prevent opaque or unsafe behaviors, making human oversight a design requirement rather than an afterthought ([[Design and evaluation of high-quality simbolic AI systems.pdf#page=1&annotation=340R|p.1]]; [[Design and evaluation of high-quality simbolic AI systems.pdf#page=4&selection=152,0,183,3|p.4]]).

At the same time, much co-production literature remains aspirational or facilitative, pointing to gaps between methodological guidance and real power-sharing [[The politics of co-production participation, power, and transformation.pdf#page=1&annotation=540R|p.15]]. In cultural heritage, calls for decentralized curation, radical user orientation, and contextualized records suggest that standards and iterative development can broaden participation across archival processes [[Metadata and Semantic Research - Conference.pdf#page=402&annotation=11793R|p.384]]. Human-centered AI surveys underline the stakes: AI systems act on personal and trace data, with risks of inscrutable models, embedded bias (especially against minorities), privacy harms, and “illusions of meaning” when models lack accountability [[What is Human-Centered about Human-Centered AI_ A Map of the Research Landscape _ Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems.pdf#page=1&selection=931,0,1057,2&color=yellow|Capel & Brereton, 2023]]. These threads converge on a core premise: user roles and methods must be matched to domain sensitivities, community agency, and social responsibility.

This article therefore frames user-centered semantic/NeSy AI for digital libraries as a situated, negotiated practice. Through a use-case analysis across 22 projects, we map when and how users are integrated (early vs. late, continuous vs. episodic), what methodological tools are deployed (co-design workshops, usability testing, crowdsourcing, semantic curation, AI-assisted creation), and how participation levels and epistemic justice are addressed—or left implicit. By comparing concrete implementations—from co-designed indexing services to exploratory semantic search, crowdsourced enrichment, and community-led storytelling—we trace where users appear as co-creators, evaluators, annotators, or subjects, and how methodological choices shape inclusion, bias mitigation, and accountability. The goal is to surface patterns and gaps so future digital library projects can better align methodological strategies with the situated needs and rights of the communities they intend to serve, strengthening both social responsibility and technical robustness. No prior work was found that synthesizes methodological practices across these disciplines in a comparable, multi-dimensional way, limiting direct benchmarks for contrast.

Strengthening participatory design, measurable evaluation, and explicit epistemic justice will be essential for inclusive, accountable semantic/NeSy systems.

# 2. Methodological analysis (process, tools, engagement and representational gaps)

The methodological findings will be presented by each one of the analised dimensions (i) development phases where the user being integrated - process-driven dimension, then (ii)the methodological Tools and Models observed in the use-cases, (iii) levels of user participation and engagement and (iv) representational gaps and epistemic justice dimensions observable in the use-cases related literature. 

The first dimension describes the (i) process-driven dimension. It examines when in the development lifecycle users are integrated into semantic and NeSy AI systems for digital libraries, distinguishing between early-stage co-design, late-stage evaluation, constant engagement, and cases where user integration is not explicitly documented. Analysis of the 22 use-cases is non-exclusive across phases: seven projects integrate users early in development, five engage users primarily during late evaluation, and six report constant engagement (e.g., ongoing crowdsourcing or continuous co-curation), while four do not explicitly document the participation phase. Constant participation doesn't mean necessarily that users take part in the whole project's phases, this can be due to a process that accepts contributions continuously but in a very specific way; such as Polariscope, that is presented as an online community for everyone to contribute although the users participated mainly by testing the tool in the final design stages [[Clippings/Remixing and repurposing cultural heritage archives through a collaborative and AI-generated storytelling digital platform |(Pedro Almeida et al., 2024)]]. Another example is *PhD Students' Information-Sharing KG* (design + evaluation). This process integrates strategically interviews for understanding the system requirements, and sentiment analysis for evaluation [[Clippings/It answers questions that I didn't know I had" PhD students' evaluation of an information sharing knowledge graph| (Gardasevic & Lamba, 2024)]]. 

The distribution reflects a predominance of design-led approaches (15 projects) over research-driven or research process-driven methodologies (7 projects), with design-led projects more frequently incorporating early user integration compared to research-oriented initiatives that tend toward constant monitoring or late-stage evaluation. Early integration is one sign of treating users as partners rather than objects of study enabling shared ideation and early co-creation; although is not sufficient to the case. Leaving users as passive subjects risks expert-only assumptions, missing contextual needs and sustainable engagement for diverse communities [[Co-creation and the new landscapes of design.pdf#page=2|(Stappers & Sanders, 2008)]] and this has been a working principle since participative early days. This partnership stance is also necessary to confront HCAI challenges—inscrutable models, embedded bias against minorities, privacy risks, and “illusions of meaning”—that arise when AI systems act on personal and trace data without accountable co-design [[What is Human-Centered about Human-Centered AI_ A Map of the Research Landscape _ Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems.pdf#page=1|(Capel & Brereton, 2023)]]. 

The next analysis is related with (ii) methodological tools observed in the use-cases. This dimension identifies the strategic use of techniques, frameworks [[Ciència Ciutadana o Ciutadanies Científiques? Quatre Models de Participació en Ciència i Tecnologia|(Javier Gómez-Ferri, 2014)]], and approaches employed to integrate users into semantic and NeSy AI systems for digital libraries, revealing a diverse toolkit ranging from structured participatory workshops to automated knowledge extraction pipelines. Analysis of the 22 use-cases demonstrates six primary methodological clusters: participatory and co-design approaches, which utilize co-construction workshops, participatory design processes, and community-led co-design sessions; user testing and user-centered design methods, which incorporate usability testing, requirement workshops, iterative test phases, and user-centered interface design; crowdsourcing and user-generated content strategies, which leverage collaborative editing environments, crowdsourced platforms, and implicit crowdsourcing through social media data collection. 

Additionally, ontology and knowledge graph engineering methodologies represent structured technical development processes, integrating data description, annotation-based customization, characterization via crowdsourcing tools, and hackathon-based prototyping. The neuro-symbolic and AI-assisted methods integrate derivative narratives, creativity, and knowledge appropriation into participatory workflows, even there is a suggestion of lowering the cognitive load and promoting creativity [[Human_AI_Co_Creation_A_Framework_for_Collaborative_Design_in_Intelligent_Systems|(Liu, Z., 2025)]]. Yet most approaches still lack robust participation evaluation (use-cases report efficiency/impact evaluations beyond usability/IR metrics—impact on participation remains under-documented). Design research also cautions that without explicit evaluation, users slip back into “subjects” observed by experts instead of co-creators steering goals and workflows, undermining the adaptive, user-driven exploration envisioned for future digital libraries [[How Should One Explore the Digital Library of the Future?|Fox & Chandrasekar, 2021]].

After this analysis its important to observe too the (iii) levels of user Participation and engagement. These levels maps the spectrum of user consultation and decision-making power and ownership [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice|(Fernando Delgado et al., 2023)]] within semantic and NeSy AI systems for digital libraries, ranging from just knowledge building and scientific sphere to full community empowerment and political agency [[White Paper on Citizen Science for Europe|(Stephanie MATT, 2015)]] [[The Science of Citizen Science|(Katrin Vohland et al., 2021)]]. Analysis of the 22 use-cases reveals a concentration of projects at consultative and collaborative levels, with fewer instances of true empowerment or community agency models. Projects classified at the consult level position users primarily as informants whose feedback shapes system design but who do not participate in core decision-making processes, while projects at the involve/collaborate level (Polariscope Collaborative Storytelling Platform) engage users as active partners in co-creation activities. Citizen science literature argues that genuine co-production aims for transformative and democratic renewal rather than consultative feedback alone [[The science of citizen science.pdf#page=126&annotation=27918R|(Vohland et al., 2021)]] but this is only possible in a situated knowledge context [[Situated learning _ legitimate peripheral participation _ Lave, Jean _ Free Download, Borrow, and Streaming _ Internet Archive| (Leave, J. Wenger, E. 1991)]].

The most participatory projects operate at the collaborate/empower level (Yanyuwa Comic Project, PhD Students' Information-Sharing Knowledge Graph, ProvKOS, HyperReal, and Neurosymbolic Narrative Generation), where users co-design system components, control narrative parameters, or participate in governance decisions. A distinct category emerges with projects that frame participation through scientific, educational, or social engagement models, which emphasize knowledge production, learning outcomes, or community contribution rather than explicit empowerment frameworks. Notably, two projects (Inuvialuit Digital Library and ArsEmotica) explicitly adopt political community agency perspectives, positioning users as co-owners of knowledge representation and cultural memory, with Inuvialuit Digital Library demonstrating the strongest community-led governance model through culturally appropriate access rules and community-determined metadata frameworks.

The last analyzed dimension is (iv) the inclusive aspect in terms of representational gaps and epistemic justice one, which assesses how projects surface or mitigate power, bias, and inclusion issues in semantic/NeSy AI for digital libraries [[A Knowledge Graph of Contentious Terminology for Inclusive Representation of Cultural Heritage|(Nesterov, A. et. al.2023)]]. Evidence from this study shows only a small subset makes it explicit in some way. Yanyuwa-comics case treats with indigenous language revitalization; althugh focused on students none of them were involved. Another case, Inuvialuit Digital Library (community-controlled access and narratives) did work with its communities, and HyperReal experiments with platforms for multivocal curation of contents. Without the formal multi-actor workflow integration advocated by Fox and Chandrasekar—explicitly linking UX researchers, subject matter experts, and developers into extensible knowledge-graph-driven workflows—digital libraries fail to enable adaptive self-organization and tailored exploration for diverse stakeholders [[How Should One Explore the Digital Library of the Future?|Fox & Chandrasekar, 2021]] but still, the processes made contribute with a perspective and learnings from its own limitations for the strenghtening of the relationship between knowledge and societal relevance [[Science Communication Culture, Identity and Citizenship|(Davis, S. & Horst, M. 2016)]]. 

Bias-aware modelling appears in GTDOnto (sensitive terrorism domain) and ProvKOS (tracking controversial classifications), while a few projects signal representation aims (e.g., World Literature KG foregrounding non-Western authors). This can be understood as Haraway’s situated knowledges—there is no “view from nowhere,” demanding accountable, positioned description practices governed with those represented [[Ciencia, cyborgs y mujeres - la reinvención de la naturaleza|(Donna Haraway, 1991)]] 

Most use-cases provide no explicit representational gaps framing (KnowEdu, ARCA, InTaVia, SAMPO, Wikibase, DigiNUMA, European Cultural Gems, ArsEmotica, Rhizomer, ATLAS, Polariscope, Neurosymbolic Narrative Generation), highlighting a gap between participatory rhetoric and concrete safeguards such as gender/intersectionality audits, provenance of contested labels, or community governance over description practices—elements emphasized in the methodological framework’s call for bias mitigation, culturally grounded metadata, and participatory control over meaning-making.

These practices need to be strengthened as Fricker’s (2011) account of epistemic injustice: testimonial credibility deficits and hermeneutical gaps persist when marginalized groups cannot contest or define classifications, leaving their knowledge underrepresented and misinterpreted—precisely the risk when epistemic justice safeguards are absent [[Epistemic Injustice  Power and the Ethics of Knowing.pdf#page=1|(Fricker, 2011)]]; while Stengers & Despret call for epistemic disobedience so communities are not merely “spoken for” but co-author classifications and meaning [[Women Who Make a Fuss  The Unfaithful Daughters of Virginia Woolf on JSTOR|(Stengers & Despret, 2014)]]. No specific methodological processes, tools or engagement strategies were reported to operationalize these epistemic justice safeguards through participation.

# 3. Methodological Insights and good practices

This section synthesizes recommendations and lessons learned from the analysis of the 25 use-cases, drawing on insights from project documentation, evaluation reports, and methodological reflections.

The analysis of 25 use-cases reveals six interconnected recommendation domains essential for effective semantic-participatory digital library projects. First, **process and methodology** must prioritize early and continuous user integration, as demonstrated by [[Clippings/Design, realization, and user evaluation of the ARCA system for exploring a digital library|(Bernasconi et al., 2022)]], [[The Multiple Faces of Cultural Heritage Towards an Integrated Visualization Platform for Tangible and Intangible Cultural Assets|(Mayr et al., 2022)]], and [[Clippings/It answers questions that I didn't know I had" PhD students' evaluation of an information sharing knowledge graph|(Gardasevic & Lamba, 2024)]], which employed iterative user-centered design with multiple evaluation phases. Modular system architectures, exemplified by ARCA's separation of semantic enrichment pipeline and user interface, facilitate maintenance and adaptation across contexts. Second, **community engagement and cultural appropriateness** requires deep, long-term partnerships, as [[Clippings/Community-Driven Knowledge Organization for Cultural Heritage Digital Libraries The Case of the Inuvialuit Settlement Region|(Farnel & Shiri, 2019)]] demonstrates through community-determined metadata structures, multilingual support for dialects, and flexible contributor role vocabularies that recognize storytellers, translators, and performers beyond standard creator distinctions. Third, **interface design** must balance exploration capabilities with accessibility: ARCA's graph navigation enables serendipitous discovery but requires contextual help systems, while Inuvialuit Digital Library prioritizes browse interfaces with visual elements over search-only systems. Fourth, **epistemic justice and bias mitigation** demands provenance tracking mechanisms, as [[Clippings/A conceptual model for tracking the provenance of activities in knowledge organization systems|(Choi & Cheng, 2024)]] shows for controversial classifications, alongside community control over representation—evident in Inuvialuit Digital Library's culturally grounded access rules and [[Clippings/Data Storytelling on Multi-modal Knowledge graph via data comics|(Liang et al., 2024)]]'s contributor-controlled narratives. Fifth, **technical and data management** should employ configurable semantic enrichment pipelines with pluggable components (ARCA), plan for data growth and quality maintenance, and balance standard vocabularies (Dublin Core, SKOS, CIDOC CRM) with domain-specific extensions. Sixth, **participatory design and co-creation** benefits from structured co-design workshops, as [[Clippings/Participatory Indexing in the Eyes of Its Potential Users- An Example of a Co-design of Participatory Services in an Academic Digital Library|(Leblanc, 2020)]] and PhD KG demonstrate, engaging diverse stakeholders (ProvKOS) and employing iterative refinement cycles with comparative testing (ARCA, InTaVia). These recommendations, when integrated, support the development of inclusive, sustainable semantic-participatory systems that respect community agency while enabling effective knowledge discovery.
# 4. Future directions for methodological development for neurosymbolic library systems

Across the use-cases, a coherent methodological pattern emerges that links participatory design, user-centered evaluation, epistemic justice, and semantic technologies into a mutually reinforcing ecosystem. Iterative, session-based co-design processes such as the Yanyuwa workshops (Liang et al., 2024) show how carefully scaffolded collaboration can translate highly technical knowledge graph structures into accessible, culturally grounded narratives, while ProvKOS (Choi & Cheng, 2024) demonstrates that similarly structured, multi-step participatory processes are effective for abstract modelling tasks like provenance tracking. Community-led projects such as the Inuvialuit Digital Library (Farnel et al., 2019) extend this logic by combining diverse engagement formats with community-determined metadata, browsing structures, and access rules, foregrounding local epistemologies over inherited library schemes.

On the evaluation side, ARCA (Bernasconi et al., 2022), the PhD Students' Knowledge Graph (Gardasevic & Lamba, 2024), and InTaVia (Mayr et al., 2022) illustrate that mixed methods—comparative usability testing, interviews, and sentiment analysis—are necessary to understand both effectiveness and experience. These projects also show that modular architectures (e.g., ARCA’s separation of semantic pipeline and UI) are key to sustaining iterative design.

Technically, the cases converge on principles of traceability, multi-modal alignment, and modularity: Yanyuwa’s insistence on source-level traceability, ARCA’s RDF-based enrichment pipelines, and Rhizomer’s task-oriented faceted exploration (García et al., 2022) collectively argue for semantic infrastructures that expose complexity without overwhelming users. Finally, across sensitive and marginalized domains, GTDOnto’s bias-aware ontology construction and the multivocal exhibition model of HyperReal (Sartini, 2025) underline that epistemic justice is best operationalized when community agency, provenance transparency, and plural perspectives are designed in from the outset rather than retrofitted as afterthoughts.

# Conclusions

Across 25 GLAM-oriented semantic/AI projects, design-led approaches prevail, with early co-design documented in only a handful of cases (e.g., Yanyuwa, PhD KG, Inuvialuit, InTaVia, ARCA). Evaluation is largely confined to usability/IR metrics, leaving the effects of participation on outcomes and equity under-examined. Epistemic justice appears mainly in community-led initiatives (Yanyuwa, Inuvialuit, HyperReal); most projects omit gender/intersectionality and bias checks, and crowdsourcing cases seldom report quality control or contributor motivation. This study contributes: (1) an inventory of 22 semantic-participatory initiatives; (2) a classification framework spanning process phase, methodological tools, participation level, and representational gaps; (3) synthesized methodological insights that contrast common practices (workshops, UCD/testing, crowdsourcing) with missing elements (early co-design, participation impact evaluation, epistemic justice safeguards); and (4) future directions that call for embedding participatory phases into ontology design workflows, pairing them with lightweight but systematic evaluation, and instituting knowledge representation reviews and community governance before release. Strengthening participatory design, measurable evaluation, and explicit social relevance attention is essential for inclusive, accountable, and sustainable semantic/NeSy infrastructures across computer science, LIS, digital humanities, and the communities these systems aim to serve.

# Discussion

The analysis highlights persistent gaps in how participatory methods are documented, compared, and sustained. Many projects describe user involvement only briefly, limiting reproducibility and transfer across contexts; longitudinal evidence on what works over time is scarce. Cross-case comparison is needed to disentangle when early co-design, late evaluation, or continuous crowdsourcing best fit different semantic/NeSy AI goals. Geographic coverage remains skewed toward English-language projects from North America and Europe, leaving Global South perspectives under-represented and constraining generalizability. Explicit integration of epistemic justice—gender, intersectionality, bias mitigation, and community ownership of meaning—remains limited, even where participation is claimed. Community agency over vocabularies, provenance, and access rules appears only in a few cases, underscoring a need for participatory governance of knowledge representation. Methodological limitations include uneven documentation quality, a 2014–2025 temporal window that may omit earlier precedents, language barriers that filter out non-English work, and classification challenges where hybrid projects resist neat categorization. Addressing these gaps requires richer process reporting, longitudinal and comparative designs, broader geographic sampling, and intentional epistemic justice safeguards embedded in both design and governance of semantic infrastructures (Fox & Chandrasekar, 2021; Vohland et al., 2021).
