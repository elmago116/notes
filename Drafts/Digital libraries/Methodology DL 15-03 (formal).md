# Methodology

This study adopts a qualitative-dominant mixed-methods design that combines (i) a SALSA-guided scoping review with bibliometric keyword mapping and (ii) a systematic content analysis of semantic and neurosymbolic AI (NeSyAI) practices for digital libraries and cultural heritage. The overall objective is to answer RQ1 by mapping where and how participatory and citizen-science practices appear in semantic/NeSyAI systems, and RQ2 by examining how these practices are methodologically described in the scholarly literature.

## Operational definitions and analytical constructs

To ensure conceptual clarity and coding consistency, several constructs are operationalized for the analytical codebook.

### Use-case / practice

In this study, a *use-case* refers to a documented instance of a semantic/NeSyAI digital-library or cultural-heritage system in scholarly literature. A *practice* denotes such an instance when the publication provides sufficient methodological information to enable content coding across the analytical dimensions (Table 1).

### User-centred design (UCD) / participatory design (PD)

UCD and PD are treated as complementary lenses on user integration rather than as fixed procedures. User-centred design is therefore conceptualized as a situated, negotiated practice in which roles may range from co-designers and annotators to evaluators or data subjects, with responsibilities spanning usability, provenance, bias mitigation, and epistemic justice [[Wacnik *et al.*, 2025]].

### Semantic and neurosymbolic AI systems

Neurosymbolic AI is understood as the integration of artificial neural networks (connectionism) with symbolic representations and reasoning (symbolism), enabling the joint handling of perceptual/learned signals and logic-based knowledge structures [[Huang, 2022]].

### Epistemic justice

Epistemic justice is operationalized as an analytical dimension that tracks how published practices attend to representational gaps, gender, intersectionality, bias recognition and mitigation, and related power-awareness considerations [[Fricker, 2007]] [[Haraway, 1991]] [[Stengers and Despret, 2014]] [[Escobar, 2016]].

### Cultural heritage (scope)

The scope of *cultural heritage* is limited to contexts relevant to digital libraries and cultural-heritage infrastructures (e.g., archives, museums, and GLAM settings), where semantic mediation shapes discovery, narration, and meaning-making across communities [[Ranjgar *et al.*, 2024]].

## SALSA-based scoping review procedure

The review follows the SALSA framework (Search, Appraisal, Synthesis, Analysis) [[Lopezosa *et al.*, 2024]] [[Cardoso *et al.*, 2021]]. Across phases, transparency is ensured by reporting the selection chain from the initial bibliometric corpus to the final set of practices.

### Search phase

In the *Search* phase, a structured literature search was conducted across academic databases spanning computer science, library and information science, digital humanities, human–computer interaction, human–computer AI, and design studies. Search equations combined terms for semantic and NeSyAI technologies with UCD/PD and citizen-science approaches, and with terms referring to digital-library and cultural-heritage infrastructures.

To refine search strings and reduce disciplinary blind spots, Perplexity AI was used iteratively as a search/agent assistant during query development [[Codina, 2023]].

After deduplication, the bibliometric corpus comprised 3,270 records. This corpus was used as the input to the Appraisal phase.

### Appraisal phase

The *Appraisal* phase included two sequential filters.

1. **Bibliometric keyword co-occurrence filter.** Keyword co-occurrence mapping was used to cluster the 3,270 deduplicated records into thematic axes. This filter retained 497 records whose keywords involved at least two thematic axes, and further narrowed the set to 80 records based on co-occurrence patterns anchored in canonical keyword presence (at least 8 out of 16 canonical keywords).
2. **Content-based screening filter.** Titles, abstracts, and reported results of the 80 records were screened to identify publications that document concrete participatory practices relevant to the study objectives. This screening produced 40 records identified as objects of study. Among them, 22 practices contained sufficient methodological detail to support the subsequent content analysis answering RQ2.

### Synthesis phase

In the *Synthesis* phase, the four dimensions of the analytical codebook (Table 1) were used to aggregate codes across the 22 practice documents. At this stage, codes were organized to identify patterns and gaps in (i) user-integration phases and process-driven patterns, (ii) participatory approaches and methodological tools, (iii) levels of user participation and engagement, and (iv) inclusion, representational gaps, and epistemic-justice considerations.

### Analysis phase

In the *Analysis* phase, the aggregated coded patterns were interpreted in light of the five thematic lenses developed in the theoretical framework. This interpretive step enables a structured link between methodological choices and analytical outcomes, including user integration, agency, and epistemic-justice-related implications in semantic/NeSyAI systems for digital libraries.

## Analytical codebook and content analysis (coding process)

The content analysis is implemented as a practice-level coding procedure using the analytical codebook reported in Table 1. Coding is informed by methodological repertoires identified in the theoretical framework (UCD/PD, HCI/HCAI, citizen science/crowdsourcing, semantic/NeSyAI for GLAM contexts, and epistemic-justice concerns).

### Codebook dimensions

The codebook operationalizes four complementary dimensions.

1. **User integration phases and process-driven patterns.** This dimension captures whether user integration occurs early, late, through constant engagement, or remains undocumented, and how this aligns with iterative development and research-design phases [[Waidelich *et al.*, 2018]] [[Calvano, 2025]] [[Turnhout *et al.*, 2020]] [[Haklay *et al.*, 2020]].
2. **Participatory approaches and methodological tools.** This dimension inventories tool clusters spanning participatory design workshops, co-design/co-creative sessions, usability and interface evaluations, and crowdsourcing-based contribution mechanisms [[Delgado *et al.*, 2023]] [[Stappers and Sanders, 2008]].
3. **Levels of user participation and engagement.** This dimension codes the spectrum from consultative roles to collaborative and empowerment-oriented forms of agency [[Delgado *et al.*, 2023]] [[Matt, 2015]] [[Vohland *et al.*, 2021]].
4. **Inclusion, representational gaps, and epistemic justice considerations.** This dimension tracks whether publications explicitly address representational gaps, bias recognition and mitigation, and epistemic-justice-related considerations [[Fricker, 2007]] [[Haraway, 1991]] [[Stengers and Despret, 2014]] [[Escobar, 2016]] [[Ferran-Ferrer *et al.*, 2023]].

### Practice-level data extraction and evidence trail

Coding is performed at the level of each practice. For each practice, the analysis is based on the full paper report, extracting relevant textual fragments that justify each code assignment. Extracted fragments are then systematized into a comparison grid that supports cross-practice aggregation.

To support replicability and traceability, the research process follows a two-step logic: (i) *code assignment* is grounded in extracted evidence fragments, and (ii) *code aggregation* is performed by consolidating codes across practices within each codebook dimension. This procedure links reported findings to specific analytical categories in the codebook, enabling readers to audit how methodological information in the literature is translated into comparative results.

## Transparency, rigor, and limitations of inference

Methodological rigor is operationalized through explicit reporting of the SALSA-driven selection chain (from 3,270 records after deduplication to 497 and 80 after bibliometric and content-based appraisal, resulting in 40 objects of study and 22 practices for coding). Analytical rigor is further supported by the use of a codebook structure that separates integration phases, tool repertoires, participation levels, and inclusion/epistemic-justice considerations, allowing patterns to be interpreted within a constrained analytical space.

However, limitations arise when practice documentation is incomplete. Because inclusion and epistemic-justice-related mechanisms are sometimes only implicitly reported, the analysis may require interpretive caution in mapping published statements to codebook categories. This constraint is treated as an empirically grounded limitation of the available corpus rather than a weakness of the coding structure.
