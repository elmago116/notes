# Methodologies for participative practices of semantic or neurosymbolic AI systems for cultural heritage and epistemic justice

**Purpose**

This article examines how users and communities are integrated into the
design and evaluation of semantic and neurosymbolic (NeSyAI) information
systems in digital libraries and cultural heritage. Through a structured
scoping review and content analysis of academic databases, it identifies
and describes multidisciplinary participatory practices (PP) in the
development of these systems and maps methodological trends, gaps, and
opportunities for transdisciplinary collaboration, with particular
attention to trust, accountability, and epistemic justice.

**Approach**

The study combines a SALSA-based scoping review with bibliometric
mapping and a qualitative content analysis. Useful to identify
multidisciplinary and documented PP and observe their methodologies in
predefines analytical dimensions: D1:user integration phases and
process-driven patterns, D2: participatory approaches and methodological
tools, D3: levels of user participation and engagement, and D4:
inclusion, representational gaps, and epistemic-justice considerations.

**Findings**  
Analysis reveals several methodological trends: early user integration
occurs in seven practices, with positioning users at late evaluation
phase, or continuous in narrow contribution; participatory tools cluster
around co-design workshops, usability testing, and crowd science;
participation levels concentrate at consultative and collaborative
stages, some empowerment models. Epistemic justice considerations under
documented. Bias-aware modeling and provenance tracking are found in
specific cases. Evaluation practices predominantly rely on usability and
information-retrieval metrics, leaving participation impacts,
trust-building, and inclusion outcomes under-documented.

**Originality**  
By mapping who is involved, when, how, and with what degree of agency
and epistemic responsibility, the study offers a structured lens on the
inclusiveness, trustworthiness, and accountability of human-centered
semantic and AI practices in digital libraries. To our knowledge, no
published synthesis offers comparable scope for NeSyAI and semantic
technologies in digital libraries. The framework serves both as an
analytical tool for researchers and as a pragmatic guide for
practitioners seeking to embed bias-aware PP in the sustainable
governance of digital library infrastructures.

**Limitations**  
The analysis is constrained by uneven documentation across practices, a
temporal focus on the past decade, and a corpus dominated by
English-language and Global North sources, which limits
generalizability. Hybrid initiatives often resist neat categorization,
which underscores the need for more transparent reporting of
participatory processes and evaluation methods. Future research should
combine longitudinal and comparative designs, broaden geographic and
linguistic coverage, and refine tools for assessing participation,
trust, and epistemic justice in human-centered AI systems for digital
libraries.

**Keywords:** User-centered design, participatory design, citizen
science, human–computer interaction, epistemic justice, gender-inclusive
design, digital libraries, bibliometric analysis, SALSA, content
analysis, semantic web technologies, knowledge graphs.

# Introduction

Digital libraries have shifted from technical repositories to
socio-technical systems mediating access, representation, and
meaning-making across cultural and knowledge domains (Davies et al.,
2025). A socio-technical system implies that infrastructures shape how
cultural heritage is discovered, narrated, and contested (Fox and
Chandrasekar, 2021), raising questions about trust, accountability, and
epistemic justice for diverse communities (Shneiderman, 2021; Haraway,
1991; Wang et al., 2024). Cultural heritage is a multidimensional
resource (Ranjgar et al., 2024) supporting cultural memory, diversity,
local development, and well-being. These infrastructures are
increasingly being designed using semantic and NeSyAI systems (Desul et
al., 2023; Ranjgar et al., 2024) albeit often only to a limited extent
and through PP. The semantic technologies comprise ontological models
and semantic-based data repositories, portals, or search services; also,
knowledge graph-based systems, linked open data platforms and other
specialized semantic tools for knowledge organization needs
(Garoufallou, 2021;Krishna et al., 2020). NeSyAI systems extend these
semantic foundations with artificial intelligence (AI) or artificial
neural network components (Huang, 2022).

In addition, within digital-library projects, PP is a complex,
context-dependent task drawing on multiple disciplines. A participatory
turn in AI design (Delgado et al., 2023) and in cultural-heritage
collections (Lähdesmäki et al., 2025) positions users as co-designers,
annotators, evaluators, or data subjects, with responsibilities spanning
usability, provenance, and bias mitigation. Epistemic-justice frameworks
foreground how bias, representation, gender, and intersectionality
(Fricker, 2007) shape whose knowledge is recognized; (Haraway, 1991),
while human-centered AI scholarship highlights risk of inscrutable
models and embedded bias when systems lack accountability (Capel and
Brereton, 2023). Where such safeguards are absent, participatory claims
can mask persistent power asymmetries.

Prior work has examined participatory practices in disciplinary
pockets—including Participatory Design (PD) (Reymen, I. 2001) showing
how methods, roles, and power are configured in specific projects.
Human-computer interaction (HCI) and Human-computer artificial
intelligence (HCAI) work stresses coupling UX (user experience) and
User-centered Design (UCD) researchers, domain experts, and developers
in knowledge-graph workflows (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021). An example is
when participatory indexing surfaces user needs and tensions between
them and expert vocabularies (Leblanc, 2020). Although they are
different fields, they are often merged into practice.

Symbolic and NeSyAI evaluations have also been reviewed where human
control is a design requirement (Calvano, 2024). Despite this, the
literature remains fragmented in how it documents and compares user
integration across semantic and NeSyAI systems for cultural heritage.
Approaches such as citizen science (Lähdesmäki et al., 2025; Ridge et
al., 2021), PD, UCD, HCI, HCAI (Delgado et al., 2023; Stappers and
Sanders, 2008), co-design, and design thinking (Bhandari, 2022) are
often invoked with limited, fragmented, and uneven documentation of the
PP. In consequence, impact, replicability, and operationalization can be
minimized. This study intends to contribute to this understanding
through two questions:

- RQ1. To what extent, and in which contexts, are PP practices
  integrated into the design and evaluation of semantic and NeSyAI
  systems for digital libraries and cultural heritage documented in
  academic databases?

- RQ2. How does academic literature and curated preprints describe these
  PP in terms of development phases, methodological tools, levels of
  user participation, and approaches to inclusion and epistemic justice?

Together, RQ1 and RQ2 aim to map where PP are documented in the
literature and how they are characterized. This is a basis for more
inclusive, accountable, and epistemically just infrastructures. It also
supports researchers and practitioners in aligning technical and
institutional demands with the situated needs and rights of the
communities concerned.

# Theoretical Framework

The literature review is structured around five thematic lenses. The
first (L1) synthesizes the reviews of multidisciplinary PP with a design
driven focus (not necessarily coming from Design Studies themselves). It
is found in the built corpus around fields like UCD, PD, co‑creation,
design thinking, HCI, and HCAI. The literature informing this lens talks
about the participatory turn in AI design (Delgado et al., 2023)
clarifying how users can act as partners in configuring AI
socio-technical systems. One systematic mapping of the evolution of PD
(Wacnik, 2025), another compiling Design thinking (Bhandari, 2022)
perspectives, two more reviewing usability-testing (Baghini et al.,
2024; Mahdie et al., 2024); situate methodological repertoires in UCD,
HCI/HCAI, and co‑creative design (Stappers and Sanders, 2008; Liu,
2025).

L2 draws on the research driven PP (citizen‑science, crowdsourcing, and
cultural‑heritage participation literature). It informs about
co‑production and citizen‑science frameworks with methodological
terminology of research-design, fieldwork, communication, and impact
[(Turnhout et al.,
2020;](The%20politics%20of%20co-production%20participation,%20power,%20and%20transformation)
[Vohland et al., 2021). It’s complemented by a policy/white paper
(European Union, 2015), a guiding framework built through practitioners
consensus in a recognized academic event for European citizen
science](The%20Science%20of%20Citizen%20Science) [(Haklay et al., 2020)
and terminology for citizen science models and levels of participation
proposed by Gómez-Ferri
(2014).](ECSA's%20Characteristics%20of%20Citizen%20Science) The L1 and
L2 are built to include the diversity of fields found in the databases
while allowing aggregation the different forms of participation
described. Although they share the same object of study (the form of
participation), specific terminology requires distinction.

L3 addresses the technological nature of the systems being designed
(semantic web and NeSyAI). It was built from a structured review of
NeSyAI [(Hamilton et al.,
2022)](Is%20Neuro-Symbolic%20AI%20Meeting%20its%20Promise%20in%20Natural%20Language%20Processing?%20A%20Structured%20Review)
and another evaluating it ([Calvano,
2025),](Techniques%20and%20Methods%20to%20Evaluate%20Human-Centered%20Symbiotic%20AI%20Systems)
complemented with a bibliometric synthesis on semantic technologies for
cultural heritage [(Desul et al.,
2023).](Semantic%20technology%20for%20cultural%20heritage%20a%20bibliometric-based%20review.pdf#page=11&annotation=496R)
L4 implies cultural heritage, digital libraries and other GLAM
(galleries, libraries, archives, and museums) contexts. It specifies
process‑driven patterns, continuous monitoring, and engagement
strategies [(Fox and Chandrasekar,
2021](How%20Should%20One%20Explore%20the%20Digital%20Library%20of%20the%20Future?);
[Ranjgar et al., 2024) in digital
libraries.](Cultural%20Heritage%20Information%20Retrieval%20Past%20Present%20and%20Future%20Trends)

L5 constitutes the conceptual work needed to observe epistemic‑justice
(Fricker, 2007) perspectives on representation and gender (Stengers and
Despret, 2014). Informs about inclusion variables and frame digital
libraries as contested sites where participation, and representational
identities (Ferran-Ferrer et al., 2023) must be analyzed alongside
technical architectures (Haraway, 1991). ..

# Methodology

This scoping review study adopts a qualitative-dominant mixed-methods.
It combines bibliometric mapping techniques (Cobo et al., 2011) with
content analysis (Cardoso et al., 2021) within the SALSA framework
(Search, Appraisal, Synthesis, Analysis) (see Figure 1) (Lopezosa et
al., 2024). The objective is to answer RQ1 by mapping, in the scholarly
databases literature, where and how PP are inscribed in the design
process of semantic or NeSyAI systems for digital libraries and cultural
heritage. The second research question (RQ2) is addressed by examining
how these practices are documented in academic databases.

In Search, the first phase, a structured literature search was conducted
between 2014 and 2025 across Scopus and Web of Science (including books,
articles, and preprints). A set of search equations were designed
combining multidisciplinary terms for semantic and NeSyAI technologies
(what is designed); UCD, PD, design thinking, co-creation and
citizen‑science approaches (how was PP made); all within the fields of
computer science, library and information science, digital humanities,
HCI, HCAI, and Design Studies.. Preprints are incorporated as a
recognized form of academic dissemination within mentioned domains, its
exclusion would amount to disregarding important PP. A search
engine/agent called Perplexity AI allowed for direct injection of Scopus
and WoS syntax rules by official URLs (Codina, 2023)., was used
iteratively as a syntax corrector and fixer of the boolean strategies to
get quality results in terms of quantity and quality (Codina, 2023).
This makes it an effective syntax corrector and fixer of the Boolean
strategies. It was used iteratively to get appropriate sample quality
results in terms of quantity and quality In the Appraisal phase, two
filters were applied: a bibliometric keyword‑co‑occurrence filter that
took the 3,270 deduplicated records from the search phase, built 40
keyword co-occurrence clusters and found a set of their canonical
keywords. These allowed the identification of 479 metadata records with
the intended information according to their keywords co-occurrence
strength. Having this measure, a finer threshold was applied, and 80
records containing 8 out 16 canonical keywords to start the content
analysis process. The second filter started the content analysis
allowing us to capture the presence of PP in the documents by assessing
the title, abstract, results, and keywords and manually selecting the
ones describing them. 40 records showed signs of such information but,
after reading the full text only 22 had the sufficient methodological
description to cover the content analysis phase. Several of the 40
records were gathered to build the codebook and the theoretical
framework. Other references were added to complete the conceptual
support needed for the analysis, i.e., for citizen science or the
epistemic justice variables.

In the Synthesis phase, the four dimensions of the analytical codebook
(Table 1) are performed at the level of each practice to aggregate codes
across the selected documents. At this stage, codes were organized to
identify patterns and gaps in: (D1) user-integration phases and
process-driven patterns; (D2) participatory approaches and
methodological tools; (D3) levels of user participation and engagement;
and (D4) inclusion, representational gaps, and epistemic-justice
considerations. To support traceability, the research process
systematically read full papers and extracted relevant fragments
following a two steps protocol. first code assignment, and second, code
aggregation. This connected the reported findings to specific analytical
categories from the codebook (Table 1) into a comparison grid. This is
how methodological information in the literature is translated into
comparative results.

In the Analysis phase, the aggregated coded patterns were interpreted
considering the five thematic lenses developed in the theoretical
framework. This enables a structured link between methodological choices
and analytical outcomes of this study, including user integration,
agency, and epistemic-justice-related implications in semantic/NeSyAI
systems for digital libraries. The sample comprises 22 PPgrouped by
primary technology. Knowledge graph-based systems enable diverse users
to co-create structured knowledge representations. NeSyAI systems
include explicit neurosymbolic narrative generation and hybrid
applications. Semantic portals and Linked Open Data platforms facilitate
community-driven content creation, and emphasize interoperable
publication, enrichment, and reuse of linked heritage data.
ontology-based systems support domain-specific knowledge organization.
semantic search and exploration systems prioritize interactive retrieval
and sense-making over complex graph corpora. specialized semantic tools
address specific knowledge organization needs.

# [Findings about methodological user integration](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

capture the moments of users entering system’s design life cycles—early
co-design, late evaluation, continuous engagement, or
undocumented—drawing on iterative design frameworks (Waidelich et al.,
2018; Calvano, 2025; Xu, R. et al., 2024), co-production in knowledge
work (Turnhout et al., 2020), and citizen-science contribution phases

addressed. Lastly, if there is a report of any technical safeguards
(provenance mechanisms, bias checks, and configurable governance models)

# [Discussion](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

[The findings provide a critical perspective on the current state of
human-centered semantic and NeSyAI practices in digital libraries as
part of a broader cultural and socio-technical system. When compared
with PD reviews, like the studies summarized in Wacnik’s (2025), many
projects describe stakeholder participation at multiple stages (D1),
while others confine it to specific moments in the lifecycle (Wacnik et
al., 2025). Aligned with Delgado’s team analysis of participatory AI
design, PP examined implement participation through consultative modes,
in which preferences and values are elicited but scope and purpose
remain primarily in expert hands (Delgado et al., 2023). In accordance,
PP for digital libraries replicate the literature pattern whereby
participation is distributed unevenly across phases that can reduce the
influence on co‑governance of systems, which limits their capacity to
transform how cultural‑heritage infrastructures are conceived and
steered.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

[Furthermore, the repertoire of tools (D2) identified shows a similar
combination of ambition and constraint when compared with the reviews.
Methodologically, the widespread use of co‑design workshops (L1),
collaborative activities, and community‑oriented processes “echoes the
twin desire diagnosed in participatory‑design literature to remedy
inequitable design by engaging directly with stakeholders and users”
(Wacnik et al., 2025, p. 2). However, the strong emphasis on user
testing and interface evaluation within this corpus mirrors the
bibliometric finding that usability testing “has become a central
methodological focus across multiple fields, including information
science and library science” (Mahdie et al., 2024, p. 11). As Delgado’s
team study argues, many participatory AI projects “focus exclusively on
informing selected aspects of the user interface, thereby restricting
what is on the table in design negotiations” (Delgado et al., 2023, p.
6). Read through this lens, the semantic and NeSyAI practices in digital
libraries show the use of a diverse toolkit, although their evaluation
wasn’t documented; they mainly assess system performance and usability,
which suggests that participation functions more as a technique embedded
in expert‑driven workflows than as a fully theorized and measured
methodological
object.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

[Finally, the implications for trust and resilience derived from this
study align with human‑centered AI critiques (Davies et al., 2025) and
with work on community‑driven knowledge organization and science
communication. Semantic and NeSyAI systems that integrate users
primarily at late evaluation stages could risk reproducing
expert-centric assumptions and opaque knowledge representations; trust
is thus framed not as a purely technical property but as a
socio‑technical outcome dependent on meaningful participation,
transparency of design choices, and accountability for representational
decisions (Stappers and Sanders, 2008; Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021).
Human‑centered AI surveys underscore that problems such as inscrutable
models, embedded bias, privacy risks, and illusions of meaning cannot be
resolved solely through technical fixes when AI systems act on personal
and trace data without accountable co‑design (Capel and Brereton, 2023).
Community‑driven heritage and science‑communication perspectives
emphasize that resilient cultural‑heritage infrastructures depend on
sustained social relationships, shared authority, and culturally
grounded practices (Escobar, 2016; Farnel and Shiri, 2019; Davis and
Horst, 2016) and that embedding community governance and long‑term
engagement helps address cultural sensitivities, contested knowledge,
and evolving community needs (Farnel and Shiri, 2019; Stengers and
Despret, 2014). Resilience in digital libraries is not only a matter of
infrastructural robustness but of sustained social relationships and
distributed responsibility. Against this backdrop, the semantic and
NeSyAI practices documented in this article point to a posible agenda
for digital libraries: aligning participatory strategies with domain
sensitivity, pairing participatory methods with explicit evaluations of
engagement and governance outcomes, and embedding provenance mechanisms,
bias checks, and community‑centered governance from the outset are
advisable steps to move from consultative to genuinely co‑produced
semantic and NeSyAI infrastructures in cultural‑heritage
contexts.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)
[These patterns point to a broader challenge for transdisciplinary
collaboration. Although the analyzed PP draw from diverse
fields—computer science, library and information science, digital
humanities, HCI, and citizen science—the integration of these
perspectives often remains partial. Technical development and
participatory design are frequently decoupled, rather than co-evolving
through shared conceptual frameworks and evaluation criteria (Delgado et
al., 2023; Capel and Brereton, 2023), and good practices can miss the
opportunity of being transferred, undermining the adaptive, user-driven
exploration envisioned for future digital libraries. Transdisciplinary
collaboration can be expanded to multi-actor workflow integration
advocated by Fox and Chandrasekar—explicitly linking UX researchers,
subject matter experts, and developers into extensible
knowledge-graph-driven workflows (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021), while
processes contribute perspectives and learnings from their own
limitations for strengthening the relationship between knowledge and
societal relevance (Davis and Horst,
2016).](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

[From a practical perspective these results highlight the need for
participatory steps to be systematically paired with evaluation methods
that capture engagement quality, equity, and governance outcomes, not
only usability or system performance (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021;
Calvano, 2025). Semantic and NeSyAI infrastructures could embed
provenance mechanisms, bias checks, and configurable governance models
from the outset to support long-term trust and adaptability. If PP do
not document concrete safeguards such as gender or intersectionality
audits, provenance of contested labels, or community governance over
description practices, representational gaps remain rhetorical rather
than operational—extending the epistemic-justice concerns raised
above.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)
[Conclusions](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

[By combining a scoping review of the scholarly literature with a
content analysis of 22 practices, this study examined how users and
communities are integrated into the development of human-centered
semantic and NeSyAI systems in digital libraries, identifying
methodological patterns, gaps, and opportunities for future
transdisciplinary
collaboration.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

[Across the analyzed cases, the findings suggest a trend of design-led
approaches, with early co-design documented in only a limited number of
initiatives. User involvement is more commonly confined to late-stage
evaluation or continuous but narrowly scoped contribution models,
particularly in crowdsourcing-based PP (L2) Evaluation practices tend to
appeal to usability and information retrieval metrics, leaving the
broader effects of participation—such as trust-building, equity,
community empowerment, and long-term sustainability—under-examined or
under-documented.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)
[The contribution of this study could lie in its systematic mapping of
participatory practices against epistemic justice dimensions. Explicit
attention to gender, intersectionality, bias mitigation, provenance of
contested classifications, and community authority over meaning-making
is documented primarily in community-led initiatives, while PP rely on
implicit inclusivity (Stengers and Despret, 2014). Where epistemic
justice safeguards are absent, participatory claims could risk masking
persistent asymmetries of power (Haraway, 1991), possibly mishandling
epistemic injustice (Fricker, 2007) . Community agency over vocabulary,
metadata schemas, and access rules are documented in particular cases
underscoring the need for participatory governance of knowledge
representation in digital
libraries.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

[Methodologically, the analysis highlights persistent gaps in how
participatory processes are documented, compared, and sustained. Many PP
provide only limited descriptions of user involvement, constraining
reproducibility, and cross-context transfer. Longitudinal evidence on
what participatory configurations work overtime is scarce, and
cross-case comparison remains underdeveloped, making it difficult to
assess when early co-design, late evaluation, or continuous
crowdsourcing are most appropriate for different semantic or
neurosymbolic AI goals. Geographic and linguistic coverage is skewed
toward English-language use-cases from North America and Europe, leaving
Global South perspectives under-represented and limiting
generalizability.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)

[In response to these gaps, this study contributes (RQ1 and RQ2): (i) an
empirically grounded inventory of 22 semantic-participatory digital
library initiatives; (ii) a replicable analytical framework spanning
process phases, methodological tools, participation levels, and
representational gaps; (iii) a synthesis of methodological insights that
explores dominant practices and discusses strengthening of user-centered
ones still lacking comprehensible and measurable evaluation and explicit
safeguards to epistemic justice and representational gaps for developing
inclusive, accountable, and sustainable semantic and neurosymbolic AI
infrastructures for digital
libraries.](https://euc-word-edit.officeapps.live.com/we/Design%20Thinking%20Process%20Model%20Review)
