# Methodologies for user-centered design of semantic or neurosymbolic AI systems for cultural heritage and epistemic justice

**Purpose**

This article examines how users and communities are integrated into the design and evaluation of semantic and neurosymbolic (NeSyAI) information systems in digital libraries and cultural heritage. Through a structured scoping review and content analysis of academic databases, it identifies and describes multidisciplinary participatory practices (PP) in the development of these systems and maps methodological trends, gaps, and opportunities for transdisciplinary collaboration, with particular attention to trust, accountability, and epistemic justice.

**Approach**

The study combines a SALSA-based scoping review with bibliometric mapping and a qualitative content analysis. Useful to identify multidisciplinary and documented PP and observe their methodologies in predefines analytical dimensions. D1:user integration phases and process-driven patterns, D2: participatory approaches and methodological tools, D3: levels of user participation and engagement, and D4: inclusion, representational gaps, and epistemic-justice considerations.

**Findings**
By mapping who is involved, when, how, and with what degree of agency and epistemic responsibility, the study offers a structured lens on the inclusiveness, trustworthiness, and accountability of human-centered semantic and AI practices in digital libraries. A synthesis of comparable scope for NeSyAI and semantic technologies in this domain was not identified in the literature reviewed. The framework serves both as an analytical tool for researchers and as a pragmatic guide for practitioners seeking to embed bias-aware PP in the sustainable governance of digital library infrastructures.

**Originality**
By mapping who is involved, when, how, and with what degree of agency and epistemic responsibility, the study offers a structured lens on the inclusiveness, trustworthiness, and accountability of human-centered semantic and AI practices in digital libraries. To our knowledge, no published synthesis offers comparable scope for NeSyAI and semantic technologies in digital libraries. The framework serves both as an analytical tool for researchers and as a pragmatic guide for practitioners seeking to embed bias-aware PP in the sustainable governance of digital library infrastructures.

**Limitations**
The analysis is constrained by uneven documentation across practices, a temporal focus on the past decade, and a corpus dominated by English-language and Global North sources, which limits generalizability. Hybrid initiatives often resist neat categorization, which underscores the need for more transparent reporting of participatory processes and evaluation methods. Future research should combine longitudinal and comparative designs, broaden geographic and linguistic coverage, and refine tools for assessing participation, trust, and epistemic justice in human-centered AI systems for digital libraries.

**Keywords:** User-centered design, participatory design, citizen science, human--computer interaction, epistemic justice, gender-inclusive design, digital libraries, bibliometric analysis, SALSA, content analysis, semantic web technologies, knowledge graphs.

# Introduction

Digital libraries have shifted from technical repositories to socio-technical systems mediating access, representation, and meaning-making across cultural and knowledge domains (Davies et al., 2025). A socio-technical system implies that infrastructures shape how cultural heritage is discovered, narrated, and contested (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021), raising questions about trust, accountability, and epistemic justice for diverse communities (Shneiderman, 2021; Haraway, 1991; Wang et al., 2024). Cultural heritage is a multidimensional resource (Ranjgar et al., 2024) supporting cultural memory, diversity, local development, and well-being. These infrastructures are increasingly being designed using semantic and NeSyAI systems (Desul et al., 2023; Ranjgar et al., 2024) albeit often only to a limited extent and through PP. The semantic technologies comprise ontological models and semantic-based data repositories, portals, or search services; also, knowledge graph-based systems, linked open data platforms and other specialized semantic tools for knowledge organization needs (Garoufallou, 2021;Krishna et al., 2020). NeSyAI systems extend these semantic foundations with artificial intelligence (AI) or artificial neural network components (Huang, 2022).

In addition, within digital-library projects, PP is a complex, context-dependent task drawing on multiple disciplines. A participatory turn in AI design (Delgado et al., 2023) and in cultural-heritage collections (Lähdesmäki et al., 2025) positions users as co-designers, annotators, evaluators, or data subjects, with responsibilities spanning usability, provenance, and bias mitigation. Epistemic-justice frameworks foreground how bias, representation, gender, and intersectionality (Fricker, 2007) shape whose knowledge is recognized; (Haraway, 1991), while human-centered AI scholarship highlights risk of inscrutable models and embedded bias when systems lack accountability (Capel and Brereton, 2023). Where such safeguards are absent, participatory claims can mask persistent power asymmetries.

Prior work has examined participatory practices in disciplinary pockets---including Participatory Design (PD) (Reymen, I. 2001) showing how methods, roles, and power are configured in specific projects. Human-computer interaction (HCI) and Human-computer artificial intelligence (HCAI) work stresses coupling UX (user experience) and User-centered Design (UCD) researchers, domain experts, and developers in knowledge-graph workflows (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021); for example, participatory indexing surfaces user needs and tensions between them and expert vocabularies (Leblanc, 2020). Other studies review symbolic and NeSyAI evaluations in which human control is a design requirement (Calvano, 2024). Despite this, the literature remains fragmented in how it documents and compares user integration across semantic and NeSyAI systems for cultural heritage. Approaches such as citizen science (Lähdesmäki et al., 2025; Ridge et al., 2021), PD, UCD, HCI, HCAI (Delgado et al., 2023; Stappers and Sanders, 2008), co-design, and design thinking (Bhandari, 2022) are often invoked with limited, fragmented, and uneven documentation of the PP, minimizing impact, replicability, and operationalization. Therefore, this study intends to contribute to this understanding through two questions:

- RQ1. To what extent, and in which contexts, are PP practices integrated into the design and evaluation of semantic and NeSyAI systems for digital libraries and cultural heritage documented in academic databases?

- RQ2. How does academic literature and curated preprints describe these PP in terms of development phases, methodological tools, levels of user participation, and approaches to inclusion and epistemic justice?

Together, RQ1 and RQ2 aim to map where PP are documented in the literature and how they are characterized, as a basis for more inclusive, accountable, and epistemically just infrastructures---supporting researchers and practitioners in aligning technical and institutional demands with the situated needs and rights of the communities concerned..

# Theoretical Framework

The literature review is structured around five thematic lenses. The first (L1) synthesizes the reviews of multidisciplinary PP with a design driven focus (not necessarily coming from Design Studies themselves) found in the built corpus (UCD, PD, co‑creation, design thinking, HCI, and HCAI). The literature informing this lens talks about the participatory turn in AI design (Delgado et al., 2023) clarifying how users can act as partners in configuring AI socio-technical systems. One systematic mapping of the evolution of PD (Wacnik, 2025), another compiling Design thinking (Bhandari, 2022) perspectives, two more reviewing usability-testing (Baghini et al., 2024; Mahdie et al., 2024); situate methodological repertoires in UCD, HCI/HCAI, and co‑creative design (Stappers and Sanders, 2008; Liu, 2025).

L2 draws on the research driven PP (citizen‑science, crowdsourcing, and cultural‑heritage participation literature). It informs about co‑production and citizen‑science frameworks with methodological terminology of research-design, fieldwork, communication, and impact (Turnhout et al., 2020; Vohland et al., 2021), complemented by a policy/white paper (European Union, 2015) and a guiding framework built through practitioners consensus in a recognized academic event for European citizen science (Haklay et al., 2020); and Gómez-Ferri (2014) formalizes citizen science models and levels of participation. The L1 and L2 are built to include the diversity of fields found in the databases while allowing aggregation the different forms of participation described. Although they share the same object of study (the form of participation), specific terminology requires distinction.

L3 addresses the technological nature of the systems being designed (semantic web and NeSyAI). It was built from a structured review of NeSyAI (Hamilton et al., 2022) and another evaluating it (Calvano, 2025), complemented with a bibliometric synthesis on semantic technologies for cultural heritage (Desul et al., 2023). L4 implies cultural heritage, digital libraries and other GLAM (galleries, libraries, archives, and museums) contexts. It specifies process‑driven patterns, continuous monitoring, and engagement strategies (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021; Ranjgar et al., 2024) in digital libraries.

L5 constitutes the conceptual work needed to observe epistemic‑justice (Fricker, 2007) perspectives on representation and gender (Stengers and Despret, 2014), inform about inclusion variables and frame digital libraries as contested sites where participation , and representational identities (Ferran-Ferrer et al., 2023) must be analyzed alongside technical architectures (Haraway, 1991). .. These are the lenses used to build the analytical tools (see Table 1) and the theoretical discussions of this study.

# Methodology

This scoping review study adopts a qualitative-dominant mixed-methods combining bibliometric mapping techniques (Cobo et al., 2011) - quantitative - with content analysis (Cardoso et al., 2021) - qualitative (see Figure 1) within the SALSA framework (Search, Appraisal, Synthesis, Analysis) (Lopezosa et al., 2024). The objective is to answer RQ1 and RQ2 by mapping, in the scholarly databases literature, where and how PP are inscribed in the design process of semantic or NeSyAI systems for digital libraries and cultural heritage; and examining how those practices are documented in academic literature.

In Search, the first phase, a structured literature search was conducted between 2014 and 2025 across Scopus and Web of Science (including books, articles, and preprints). Search equations combined terms for semantic and NeSyAI technologies (what is designed); UCD, PD, design thinking, co-creation, and citizen-science approaches (how PP was enacted); and domains including computer science, library and information science, digital humanities, HCI, HCAI, and Design Studies. Preprints were retained as a recognized dissemination channel in these fields; omitting them would risk missing relevant PP. Perplexity AI was used iteratively to align Boolean queries with Scopus and Web of Science syntax (Codina, 2023).

In the Appraisal phase, two filters were applied. First, a bibliometric keyword co-occurrence analysis of 3,270 deduplicated records from the search produced 40 keyword co-occurrence clusters and canonical keywords; 479 metadata records matched the intended topic by keyword co-occurrence strength. A finer threshold—at least 8 of 16 canonical keywords—yielded 80 records for content analysis. Second, titles, abstracts, results, and keywords were screened for participatory practice; 40 records met this criterion, and full-text reading retained 22 with sufficient methodological description. Several of the 40 records informed the codebook and theoretical framework; additional references were added for conceptual support (e.g., citizen science, epistemic justice).

In the Synthesis phase, the four dimensions of the analytical codebook (Table 1) are performed at the level of each practice to aggregate codes across the selected documents. At this stage, codes were organized to identify patterns and gaps in (i) user-integration phases and process-driven patterns, (ii) participatory approaches and methodological tools, (iii) levels of user participation and engagement, and (iv) inclusion, representational gaps, and epistemic-justice considerations. To support traceability, the research process systematically read full papers and extracted relevant fragments following a protocol: (i) code assignment, and (ii) code aggregation, linking the reported findings to specific analytical categories from the codebook (Table 1) into a comparison grid, showing how methodological information in the literature is translated into comparative results.

In the Analysis phase, the aggregated coded patterns were interpreted considering the five thematic lenses developed in the theoretical framework. This enables a structured link between methodological choices and analytical outcomes of this study, including user integration, agency, and epistemic-justice-related implications in semantic/NeSyAI systems for digital libraries. # Findings about methodological user integration

The findings are organized along the codebook dimensions D1–D4. They synthesize what the reviewed literature *documents* about participatory methods in semantic and NeSyAI systems that support digital library and GLAM work (collections, discovery, metadata, and public engagement). **For *Digital Library Perspectives* readers,** the practical stakes are: when users and communities enter system lifecycles, which methodological tools are used, how participation levels are described, and whether inclusion and epistemic justice are made explicit—issues that bear on how libraries justify trust, representation, and accountability in AI-augmented infrastructures.

The user integration phases and process-driven patterns (**D1**) capture the moments of users entering system's design life cycles---early co-design, late evaluation, continuous engagement, or undocumented---drawing on iterative design frameworks (Waidelich et al., 2018); (Calvano, 2025), co-production in knowledge work (Turnhout et al., 2020), and citizen-science contribution phases (Haklay et al., 2020). The participatory approaches and methodological tools (**D2**) inventories workshops, co-design and co-creative sessions (Stappers and Sanders, 2008), usability testing (Delgado et al., 2023), crowdsourcing (also named crowd-science)(Lähdesmäki et al., 2025), citizen-science models (European Commission, 2015), annotation practices, (Gómez-Ferri, 2014). **D3** identifies the levels of user participation and engagement in L1 (consult, include, elaborate, own) (Delgado et al., 2023) and L2 with citizen-science roles (scientific, educational, social, economic, environmental, political agency) (Vohland et al., 2021). **D4** codes whether and how the following epistemic justice (Fricker, 2007) aspects influence the process of designing this type of information infrastructures: identity and gender inclusion or relations with the women problem, the mirror effect, or systemic problem (Ferran-Ferrer et al., 2023). Also, if there is any mention of related representational gaps (Stengers and Despret, 2014), knowledge diversity (Haraway, 1991), or power dynamics (Escobar, 2016) addressed. Lastly, if there is a report of any technical safeguards (provenance mechanisms, bias checks, and configurable governance models) (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021; Calvano, 2025). # Discussion

The findings provide a critical perspective on the current state of human-centered semantic and NeSyAI practices in digital libraries as part of a broader cultural and socio-technical system. When compared with PD reviews, like the studies summarized in Wacnik's (2025), many projects describe stakeholder participation at multiple stages (D1), while others confine it to specific moments in the lifecycle (Wacnik et al., 2025). Aligned with Delgado's team analysis of participatory AI design, most of the PP examined implement participation through consultative modes, in which preferences and values are elicited but scope and purpose remain primarily in expert hands (Delgado et al., 2023). In accordance, PP for digital libraries replicate the literature pattern whereby participation is distributed unevenly across phases and rarely extends to co‑governance of systems, which limits their capacity to transform how cultural‑heritage infrastructures are conceived and steered.

Furthermore, the repertoire of tools (D2) identified shows a similar combination of ambition and constraint when compared with the reviews. Methodologically, the widespread use of co‑design workshops (L1), collaborative activities, and community‑oriented processes echoes the "twin desire" diagnosed in participatory‑design literature to remedy inequitable design by engaging directly with stakeholders and users (Wacnik et al., 2025, p. 2). However, the strong emphasis on user testing and interface evaluation within this corpus mirrors the bibliometric finding that usability testing has become a central methodological focus across multiple fields, including information science and library science (Mahdie et al., 2024, p. 11). As Delgado's team study argues, many participatory AI projects "focus exclusively on informing selected aspects of the user interface, thereby restricting what is on the table in design negotiations" (Delgado et al., 2023, p. 6). Read through this lens, the semantic and NeSyAI practices in digital libraries appear to use a rich toolkit of participatory methods, but they seldom evaluate participation itself; instead, they mainly assess system performance and usability, which suggests that participation functions more as a technique embedded in expert‑driven workflows than as a fully theorized and measured methodological object.

Finally, the implications for trust and resilience derived from this study align closely with human‑centered AI critiques (Davies et al., 2025) and with work on community‑driven knowledge organization and science communication. Semantic and NeSyAI systems that integrate users primarily at late evaluation stages risk reproducing expert-centric assumptions and opaque knowledge representations; trust is thus framed not as a purely technical property but as a socio‑technical outcome dependent on meaningful participation, transparency of design choices, and accountability for representational decisions (Stappers and Sanders, 2008; Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021). Human‑centered AI surveys underscore that problems such as inscrutable models, embedded bias, privacy risks, and "illusions of meaning" cannot be resolved solely through technical fixes when AI systems act on personal and trace data without accountable co‑design (Capel and Brereton, 2023). Community‑driven heritage and science‑communication perspectives emphasize that resilient cultural‑heritage infrastructures depend on sustained social relationships, shared authority, and culturally grounded practices (Escobar, 2016; Farnel and Shiri, 2019; Davis and Horst, 2016) and that embedding community governance and long‑term engagement helps address cultural sensitivities, contested knowledge, and evolving community needs (Farnel and Shiri, 2019; Stengers and Despret, 2014). Resilience in digital libraries is not only a matter of infrastructural robustness but of sustained social relationships and distributed responsibility. Against this backdrop, the semantic and NeSyAI practices documented in this article point to a clear agenda for digital libraries: aligning participatory strategies with domain sensitivity, pairing participatory methods with explicit evaluations of engagement and governance outcomes, and embedding provenance mechanisms, bias checks, and community‑centered governance from the outset are necessary steps to move from consultative to genuinely co‑produced semantic and NeSyAI infrastructures in cultural‑heritage contexts.

These patterns point to a broader challenge for transdisciplinary collaboration. Although the analyzed use-cases draw from diverse fields---computer science, library and information science, digital humanities, HCI, and citizen science---the integration of these perspectives often remains partial. Technical development and participatory design are frequently decoupled, rather than co-evolving through shared conceptual frameworks and evaluation criteria (Delgado et al., 2023; Capel and Brereton, 2023), and good practices miss the opportunity of being transferred, undermining the adaptive, user-driven exploration envisioned for future digital libraries. Transdisciplinary collaboration needs to be expanded to multi-actor workflow integration advocated by Fox and Chandrasekar---explicitly linking UX researchers, subject matter experts, and developers into extensible knowledge-graph-driven workflows (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021), while processes contribute perspectives and learnings from their own limitations for strengthening the relationship between knowledge and societal relevance (Davis and Horst, 2016).

From a practical perspective, these results highlight the need for participatory steps to be systematically paired with evaluation methods that capture engagement quality, equity, and governance outcomes, not only usability or system performance (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021; Calvano, 2025). Semantic and NeSyAI infrastructures should embed provenance mechanisms, bias checks, and configurable governance models from the outset to support long-term trust and adaptability. If use-cases do not document concrete safeguards such as gender or intersectionality audits, provenance of contested labels, or community governance over description practices, representational gaps remain rhetorical rather than operational—extending the epistemic-justice concerns raised above.

## Conclusions

By combining a scoping review of the scholarly literature with a content analysis of 22 practices, this study examined how users and communities are integrated into the development of human-centered semantic and NeSyAI systems in digital libraries, identifying methodological patterns, gaps, and opportunities for future transdisciplinary collaboration.

Across the analyzed cases, findings reveal a trend of design-led approaches, with early co-design documented in only a limited number of initiatives. User involvement is more commonly confined to late-stage evaluation or continuous but narrowly scoped contribution models, particularly in crowdsourcing-based and research-based use-cases. Evaluation practices tend to appeal to usability and information retrieval metrics, leaving the broader effects of participation---such as trust-building, equity, community empowerment, and long-term sustainability---under-examined or under-documented.

A central contribution of this study lies in its systematic mapping of participatory practices against epistemic justice dimensions. Explicit attention to gender, intersectionality, bias mitigation, provenance of contested classifications, and community authority over meaning-making emerges primarily in community-led initiatives, while most use-cases rely on implicit or assumed inclusivity (Stengers and Despret, 2014). Where epistemic justice safeguards are absent, participatory claims risk masking persistent asymmetries of power (Haraway, 1991), reinforcing epistemic injustice rather than addressing it (Fricker, 2007). Community agency over vocabulary, metadata schemas, and access rules remain the exception rather than the norm, underscoring the need for participatory governance of knowledge representation in digital libraries.

Methodologically, the analysis highlights persistent gaps in how participatory processes are documented, compared, and sustained. Many practices provide only limited descriptions of user involvement, constraining reproducibility, and cross-context transfer. Longitudinal evidence on what participatory configurations work overtime is scarce, and cross-case comparison remains underdeveloped, making it difficult to assess when early co-design, late evaluation, or continuous crowdsourcing are most appropriate for different semantic or neurosymbolic AI goals. Geographic and linguistic coverage is skewed toward English-language use-cases from North America and Europe, leaving Global South perspectives under-represented and limiting generalizability.

In response to these gaps, this study contributes (RQ1 and RQ2): (i) an empirically grounded inventory of 22 semantic-participatory digital library initiatives; (ii) a replicable analytical framework spanning process phases, methodological tools, participation levels, and representational gaps; (iii) a synthesis of methodological insights that evidences dominant practices and discusses strengthening of user-centered ones still lacking comprehensible and measurable evaluation and explicit safeguards to epistemic justice and representational gaps for developing inclusive, accountable, and sustainable semantic and neurosymbolic AI infrastructures for digital libraries.
