Digital libraries have shifted from technical repositories to socio-technical systems mediating access, representation, and meaning-making across cultural and knowledge domains (Davies et al., 2025). A socio-technical system implies that infrastructures shape how cultural heritage is discovered, narrated, and contested (Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021), raising questions about trust, accountability, and epistemic justice for diverse communities (Shneiderman, 2021; Haraway, 1991; Wang et al., 2024). Cultural heritage is a multidimensional resource (Ranjgar et al., 2024) supporting cultural memory, diversity, local development, and well-being. These infrastructures are increasingly designed using semantic and NeSyAI systems (Desul et al., 2023; Ranjgar et al., 2024), albeit often only to a limited extent and through participatory practices. Semantic technologies include ontological models, semantic data repositories, portals, and search services; knowledge graph-based systems; linked open data platforms; and other specialized tools for knowledge organization (Garoufallou, 2021; Krishna et al., 2020). NeSyAI systems extend these semantic foundations with artificial intelligence (AI) or artificial neural network components (Huang, 2022).

  

Within digital-library projects, PP is a complex, context-dependent task drawing on multiple disciplines. A participatory turn in AI design (Delgado et al., 2023) and in cultural-heritage collections (Lähdesmäki et al., 2025) positions users as co-designers, annotators, evaluators, or data subjects, with responsibilities spanning usability, provenance, and bias mitigation. Epistemic-justice frameworks foreground how bias, representation, gender, and intersectionality shape whose knowledge is recognized (Fricker, 2007; Haraway, 1991). Human-centered AI scholarship highlights risks of inscrutable models and embedded bias when systems lack accountability (Capel and Brereton, 2023). Where such safeguards are absent, participatory claims can mask persistent power asymmetries.

# Resumen de las prácticas:

The final sample comprises 22 practices grouped by primary technology. **Knowledge graph-based systems** (9 practices) enable diverse users to co-create structured knowledge representations: language teachers construct knowledge graph structures and data comics narratives in Yanyuwa; reader communities, teachers, and researchers integrate semantic models and reception data in World Literature Knowledge Graph; PhD students develop knowledge graph data models and visualization designs; archivists and researchers customize knowledge graph structures and annotation enrichments for participatory archives; experts and learners refine educational knowledge graphs with concept extraction and relation identification in KnowEdu; school archivists and researchers collaborate on numismatic and archaeological data knowledge graphs in DigiNUMA; researchers and practitioners co-construct knowledge graphs for Italian cultural heritage in ATLAS; users as co-curators shape multivocal exhibitions and narrative layers in HyperReal; researchers, practitioners, and lay users explore knowledge graphs interactively in Rhizomer. **Semantic portals and Linked Open Data platforms** (5 practices) facilitate community-driven content creation: citizens contribute cultural information through crowdsourced platforms in European Cultural Gems; non-expert users collaboratively edit cultural heritage data using Wikibase; researchers access configurable semantic portals in SAMPO projects; users explore cultural heritage through LOD portals in Cultural Leaf; citizens and institutions co-create collective memories over cultural heritage archives using AI-generated stories in Polariscope. **Ontology-based systems** (2 practices) support domain-specific knowledge organization: end users and curators develop ontologies for sensitive terrorism domains in GTDOnto; editorial managers, information professionals, and researchers define provenance tracking models extending PROV and SKOS in ProvKOS. **Semantic search and exploration systems** (2 practices) position users as evaluators and co-shapers: publishers, researchers, and interested individuals test semantic search systems and knowledge graph exploration in ARCA; historians, cultural scientists, and GLAM practitioners contribute to multimodal historical knowledge graphs and visualization platforms in InTaVia. **Neurosymbolic and multimodal narrative systems** (2 practices) empower users as co-authors: users control narrative parameters in neurosymbolic narrative generation systems for cultural heritage; social media users generate emotional metadata and affective responses in ArsEmotica. **Specialized semantic tools** (2 practices) address specific knowledge organization needs: potential library users prototype participatory indexing services and workflows; community members, students, and language instructors co-design metadata frameworks and culturally appropriate digital library structures in Inuvialuit Digital Library. Across technologies, users range from domain experts (archivists, researchers, curators) to non-expert contributors (citizens, students, social media users), co-creating objects spanning structured knowledge representations, narrative layers, and culturally grounded access rules.

# Discussion discarded draft 

Taken together, the findings offer a critical perspective on the current state of human‑centred semantic and NeSy AI practices in digital libraries as part of a broader cultural cyber‑critical ecosystem. On the one hand, the reviews on participatory design and participatory AI highlight a widespread recognition that inequities are often produced by design practices and a “twin desire” to remedy them by engaging directly with stakeholders and users [[Participatory_design_A_systematic_review_and_insights_for_future_practice.pdf#page=2&selection=10,53,16,36|(Wacnik et al., 2025, p. 2)]]; [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice.pdf#page=6&selection=170,0,176,23|(Delgado et al., 2023, p. 6)]]. On the other hand, both the reviewed literature and the empirical corpus analysed here show that participation is most often implemented through consultative modes, in which preferences and values are elicited and then translated by expert teams into system requirements, interface features, or evaluation criteria [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice.pdf#page=6&selection=193,9,198,9|(Delgado et al., 2023, p. 6)]]. Only a minority of projects, in the wider reviews and in this sample, involve stakeholders in shaping the scope and purpose of systems or in co‑governing semantic and neurosymbolic infrastructures [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice.pdf#page=6&selection=199,40,204,1|(Delgado et al., 2023, p. 6)]].

  

Moreover, although participatory design reviews report that a large share of projects describe stakeholder participation across multiple stages of the design process [[Participatory_design_A_systematic_review_and_insights_for_future_practice.pdf#page=9&selection=18,0,48,10|(Wacnik et al., 2025, p. 9)]], the analysis of semantic and NeSy AI practices for digital libraries confirms that user involvement remains unevenly distributed across lifecycles. Many initiatives mirror the broader pattern in which participation is strongest in early ideation and late evaluation, but weaker or undocumented during intermediate design and development stages. As the participatory AI review notes, this often results in interventions that “focus exclusively on informing selected aspects of the user interface” [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice.pdf#page=6&selection=199,40,204,1|(Delgado et al., 2023, p. 6)]] narrowing decisive decisions. In the cultural‑heritage context, this means that methodological ambitions toward co‑design and community agency are frequently constrained by institutional mandates and resources, which prioritise expert‑driven development pipelines and limit the space for shared authority [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice.pdf#page=8&selection=28,51,31,35|(Delgado et al., 2023, p. 8)]].

  

In addition, the second and third dimensions of the analysis suggest that participatory tools are often deployed without corresponding evaluation of participation itself. Reviews of usability testing show a strong and growing academic interest in usability as a methodological and bibliometric object [[Usability Testing A Bibliometric Analysis Based on WoS Data – Journal of Scientometric Research.pdf#page=3&selection=31,0,103,9|(Mahdie et al., 2024, p. 11)]], and this is reflected in the digital‑library practices, where user testing and interface evaluation are systematically reported. However, participation tends to be assessed indirectly, through efficiency or usability indicators, rather than through explicit measures of engagement quality, power‑sharing, or community outcomes. This aligns with observations in the participatory AI literature that researchers and practitioners have difficulty mapping their participatory ambitions to the practical constraints they face on the ground, and that most projects remain consultative even when they adopt participatory language [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice.pdf#page=10&selection=106,0,115,34|(Delgado et al., 2023, p. 10)]]. In this sense, semantic and NeSy AI practices for cultural heritage exemplify a broader shift from participation as a normative aspiration to participation as a set of techniques that can be incorporated into otherwise expert‑centred workflows.

  

From an inclusion and epistemic‑justice standpoint, the results also resonate with the critiques of tokenistic participation in cultural‑heritage management and of systemic gender and representation gaps in large‑scale knowledge infrastructures. Work on participatory heritage has shown how policy pressure to adopt participatory discourses can expand access to official processes while still failing to grant communities real power or agency, resulting in “authorised” but shallow forms of engagement [[Engaging Communities in Cultural Heritage.pdf#page=38&annotation=40211R|(Lähdesmäki et al., 2025, p. 19)]]. Similarly, research on the Wikipedia gender gap demonstrates how the “women problem,” the “mirror effect,” and the “systemic problem” interact to reproduce structural inequalities in representation [[Wikipedia gender gap a scoping review Profesional de la información.pdf#page=9&annotation=266R|(Ferran-Ferrer et al., 2022, p. 9)]]. The semantic and NeSy AI practices examined here largely confirm that, in the absence of explicit epistemic‑justice safeguards, participatory claims can coexist with persistent asymmetries of power: representational aims are articulated, but concrete procedures for addressing bias, negotiating contested classifications, or ensuring community governance over vocabularies and access rules are comparatively rare [[Epistemic Injustice Power and the Ethics of Knowing|(Fricker, 2007)]]; [[Ciencia, cyborgs y mujeres - la reinvención de la naturaleza|(Haraway, 1991)]]; [[Women Who Make a Fuss The Unfaithful Daughters of Virginia Woolf on JSTOR|(Stengers and Despret, 2014)]].

  

Consequently, and in line with human‑centred AI critiques, these patterns have implications for how trust and resilience are conceptualised in digital‑library infrastructures. As human‑centred AI surveys emphasise, issues such as inscrutable models, embedded bias against minorities, privacy risks, and “illusions of meaning” cannot be solved by technical fixes alone when AI systems act on personal and trace data without accountable co‑design [[What is Human-Centered about Human-Centered AI_ A Map of the Research Landscape _ Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems.pdf#page=1|(Capel and Brereton, 2023)]]. Trust emerges instead as a socio‑technical outcome that depends on meaningful participation, transparency of design choices, and accountability for representational decisions [[Co-creation and the new landscapes of design.pdf#page=2|(Stappers and Sanders, 2008)]]; [[How Should One Explore the Digital Library of the Future?|(Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021)]]. Reviews of community‑driven knowledge organisation and science communication further suggest that resilience in cultural‑heritage information systems is as much about sustained social relationships and distributed responsibility as it is about infrastructural robustness [[Community-Driven Knowledge Organization for Cultural Heritage Digital Libraries The Case of the Inuvialuit Settlement Region|(Farnel and Shiri, 2019)]]; [[Science Communication Culture, Identity and Citizenship|(Davis and Horst, 2016)]].

  

In practical terms, therefore, the combined insights from the reviews and the empirical corpus indicate several priorities for future semantic and NeSy AI work in digital libraries. First, participatory strategies need to be aligned with domain sensitivity and social responsibility, ensuring that stakeholder involvement is not confined to late‑stage evaluation or narrow consultation but extends to earlier phases where scope, purpose, and governance arrangements are negotiated [[Participatory_design_A_systematic_review_and_insights_for_future_practice.pdf#page=9&selection=18,0,48,10|(Wacnik et al., 2025, p. 9)]]; [[The Participatory Turn in AI Design Theoretical Foundations and the Current State of Practice.pdf#page=10&selection=106,0,115,34|(Delgado et al., 2023, p. 10)]]. Second, participatory steps should be systematically paired with evaluation methods that capture engagement quality, equity, and governance outcomes, not only usability or system performance [[Usability Testing A Bibliometric Analysis Based on WoS Data – Journal of Scientometric Research.pdf#page=3&selection=31,0,103,9|(Mahdie et al., 2024, p. 11)]]; [[How Should One Explore the Digital Library of the Future?|(Fox and Chandrasekar, 2021)]]; [[Techniques and Methods to Evaluate Human-Centered Symbiotic AI Systems\|(Calvano, 2025)]]. Finally, semantic and NeSy AI infrastructures should embed provenance mechanisms, bias checks, and configurable governance models from the outset to support long‑term trust and adaptability. Without such safeguards—such as gender and intersectionality audits, documentation of contested labels, and community governance over knowledge‑organisation practices—digital libraries risk perpetuating testimonial and hermeneutical injustices, whereby marginalised communities are included as data sources or contributors but excluded from interpretive control [[Epistemic Injustice Power and the Ethics of Knowing|(Fricker, 2007)]]; [[Ciencia, cyborgs y mujeres - la reinvención de la naturaleza|(Haraway, 1991)]]; [[Women Who Make a Fuss The Unfaithful Daughters of Virginia Woolf on JSTOR|(Stengers and Despret, 2014)]]. In this light, community‑driven, multivocal approaches to knowledge organisation appear less as optional enhancements and more as necessary conditions for aligning human‑centred semantic and NeSy AI in digital libraries with epistemic‑justice and inclusion goals [[Engaging Communities in Cultural Heritage.pdf#page=38&annotation=40211R|(Lähdesmäki et al., 2025, p. 19)]]; [[Autonomía y diseño - La realización de lo comunal|(Escobar, 2016)]].
