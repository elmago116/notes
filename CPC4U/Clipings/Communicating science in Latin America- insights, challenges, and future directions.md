Communicating science
in Latin America: insights,
challenges, and future
directions
Cecilia Rosen 1,*
This article reviews public science
communication 	(SC) 	in 	Latin
America, 	highlighting 	advances
and challenges. It emphasizes the
need to foster inclusive policies,
interdisciplinary approaches, and
effective evaluation to enhance
public engagement, address social
inequalities, and foster informed
decision-making. Improving this
field would strengthen science–
society relationships, benefiting
both professional communicators
and scientific communities.
Navigating public SC in Latin
America
Over the past five decades, Latin America
has strengthened its national science and
technology (S&T) systems, and along with
this growth, countries have increasingly in-
stitutionalized activities aimed at engaging
diverse audiences in scientific research.
Here, public SC refers to the practice and
research field focused on engaging the
public beyond academic circles, often
described as ‘science popularization’ or
‘public communication of science’ [1].
Along with S&T development, SC in Latin
America has expanded significantly in re-
cent decades, gaining importance for its
role in fostering public understanding,
combating misinformation, and enhancing
civic engagement in scientific decision-
making. Despite its growth, the field
faces persistent challenges, including
scarce resources, limited institutional
recognition, and an incomplete under-
standing 	of 	public 	attitudes 	toward
science, all of which are exacerbated by
the region’s unique social and economic
inequalities.

Addressing these challenges requires pri-
oritizing critical thinking and civic engage-
ment across diverse communities. Given
Latin America’s pressing issues, including
inequality, environmental conflicts, and
public health crises, SC could be an
essential tool in empowering citizens and
enabling informed decision-making.
It would also greatly benefit scientists to
share their work more broadly and engage
in meaningful dialog with diverse audiences.
However, to fulfill this potential the field must
cultivate policies that reflect local needs –
such as funding for community-based SC,
the inclusion of communication training in
graduate programs, and the creation of per-
manent positions for SC professionals.
These efforts should also promote interdisci-
plinary approaches and recognize SC as a
core element of scientific research.
In this work, I review some of the advances
and persistent challenges facing SC in
Latin America, emphasizing strategies
that could strengthen the field’s contribu-
tion to tackling the region’s most pressing
concerns.
Regional expansion of SC
A central goal of SC is to enhance the re-
gion’s scientific culture, a multidimensional
concept encompassing scientific literacy,
public understanding, and active engage-
ment with S&T. As Cortassa and Polino
[2] describe it, this refers to ‘all the modes
through which individuals and society ap-
propriate S&T’. However, achieving this
goal requires more than disseminating
information; it calls for the fostering of a
scientific culture that reflects collective atti-
tudes, knowledge, and practices while
also addressing cultural values and beliefs.
Studies 	have 	shown 	that 	citizens’
decisions are influenced by these factors,
especially during crises such as the coro-
navirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pan-
demic [3].
Scientific culture has gained prominence
within National Science and Technology
Organizations (ONCYTs) in countries like
Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, and
Mexico [4]. These nations have led the
way in developing SC institutions and initia-
tives, although other countries in the region
continue to face challenges in establishing
foundational SC practices. SC activities in
Latin America encompass diverse formats,
including science journalism, fairs, exhibi-
tions, TV shows, podcasts, and over 450
science centers. However, these re-
sources remain unevenly distributed, with
Brazil alone housing more than half of the
science centers in the region [5].
The drive for visibility, legitimacy, and
funding – alongside the need for stake-
holder engagement – has spurred in-
creased SC activities, with some countries,
like Mexico, now including communication
efforts in academic assessments. In 2023,
Mexico’s new S&T federal law affirmed that
‘universal access to knowledge is part of
the human right to science’ [6], thereby posi-
tioning SC as an essential part of scientific
work. Additionally, regional associations like
the Latin American Network for Science
Communication (RedPop) have supported
SC development, connecting over 80 mem-
ber institutions since its founding in 1990 [7].
Despite the progress represented by poli-
cies, associations, and networks, there
are still gaps in achieving consistent,
high-quality SC practices across the re-
gion. A survey of 123 organizations from
14 countries found that only 14.3% con-
duct SC activities regularly [7]. Further-
more, only 49.6% of organizations have
dedicated SC budgets, and a mere
10.1% employ trained SC professionals.
Strengthening SC’s institutional founda-
tion and securing resources for consistent,
professional SC work is essential to sus-
tain and scale its impact.

An United Nations Educational, Scientific
and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) -
funded study confirmed the need to culti-
vate ‘knowledge citizens’ – individuals
equipped to engage critically with S&T
[4]. However, Cortassa and Polino [2] cau-
tion that SC policies often fail to translate
into meaningful action, as rhetorical inter-
est does not always match the resources
allocated.
Diversity and inclusion: a situated
SC
Latin America is one of the most unequal
regions globally, where social issues like
environmental conflicts and public health
risks are deeply intertwined with scientific
and technological development. The
COVID-19 pandemic underscored the
critical role of SC in combating misinfor-
mation, yet access to SC activities and re-
sources remains uneven. Socioeconomic
status, education level, and geographic lo-
cation determine public engagement with
science; those with greater resources
benefit disproportionately, while many are
left out [8,9]. As Polino and Castelfranchi
[10] observe, this exclusion limits demo-
cratic participation, as informed decision-
making requires equitable access to sci-
entific knowledge.
Furthermore, the pandemic also exposed
how SC can be manipulated for political
purposes. In some cases, government of-
ficials and politically aligned scientists
downplayed health risks or disseminated
misleading messages about vaccines
and preventive measures. These episodes
highlight the urgent need to protect SC
from ideological distortion and ensure its
integrity as a tool for democratic participa-
tion and public trust [11].
However, merely providing information is
insufficient. The ‘deficit model’ of SC,
which assumes that knowledge alone will
drive public understanding and support,
fails to engage audiences meaningfully,
treating them as passive recipients rather
than active participants. By contrast, fos-
tering ‘scientific citizenship’ empowers
the public not only to understand science
but to participate in shaping S&T initia-
tives, which can strengthen trust in scien-
tific institutions and promote democratic
governance of S&T.
Incorporating local knowledge, cultural
values, and ethical considerations into
S&T policies enables SC to better reflect
societal needs and respect diverse com-
munities, a necessity in Latin America’s
culturally rich landscape [5]. As Cortassa
[12] notes, SC must involve ‘tailored com-
munication’ that resonates with people
across varied social, cultural, and educa-
tional backgrounds, presenting scientific
content in a way that is accessible, rele-
vant, and inclusive.
SC research potential in Latin
America
Integrating research into SC practice is
challenging but essential for a robust field.
Science communicators still lack compre-
hensive data on how effectively their work
impacts different audiences. While this
knowledge gap is a global issue, it is partic-
ularly acute in Latin America, where SC is a
relatively young profession [12]. Connecting
practice and research is crucial to enhanc-
ing SC’s quality and innovation, with
research-informed 	practices 	fostering
meaningful engagement and generating
new approaches [11,13].
Routine evaluation of SC activities remains
rare due to limited resources and institu-
tional support. Many SC efforts are driven
by individuals or small groups operating
without financial backing. In a 2019 sur-
vey, only 34.1% of institutions in the region
had evaluation systems and only 10.1%
employed professional science communi-
cators [7]. Developing evaluation stan-
dards should be viewed as ‘an essential
tool for effective and responsible commu-
nication, not a luxury’ [14].
In Brazil, however, government support for
SC has been sustained despite recent po-
litical changes, with the establishment of a
National Institute of Science Communica-
tion. Brazil also conducted the region’s
first study on public perceptions of science
in 1987, a model now emulated by other
Latin American countries [14]. Increased
collaboration and SC training programs
are vital to strengthen the field; currently,
master’s and PhD programs in SC are
scarce and concentrated in select cities,
which limits accessibility and expertise de-
velopment. Expanding training and foster-
ing 	interdisciplinary 	approaches 	can
bridge the gap between SC research and
practice, advancing SC’s impact in the re-
gion [13].
Concluding remarks
In the past several decades, Latin
American countries have promoted and
institutionalized activities to engage the
public with scientific research, connecting
SC with regional development needs.
However, Latin American SC requires
strategies tailored to its unique social, eco-
nomic, and cultural landscape rather than
relying on Western models. The region’s
specific challenges, from environmental
degradation to public health crises, un-
derscore the need for interdisciplinary ap-
proaches that resonate with diverse
communities [15].
To address social inequality, SC training
programs should prioritize empowering
citizens – especially those in marginalized
contexts – by providing equitable access
to scientific information that supports
quality of life and informed decision-
making. The region’s cultural and biologi-
cal diversity demands decentralized ap-
proaches to reach rural, Indigenous, and
underserved urban communities. More-
over, SC plays an essential role in combat-
