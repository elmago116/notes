Scientists, the Media, and the Public Communication of
Science
Anthony Dudo*
The University of Texas at Austin


Abstract
The relationship between scientists and journalists has evolved in recent years with the advent of numer-
ous sociocultural changes and drastic shifts within the media ecosystem. Media professionals have tradi-
tionally been the gatekeepers of scientific information, but new media technologies grant scientists
more power than ever before to be proactive about their public communication. In this article, I provide
an overview of the science–media relationship and scientists as public communicators. Specifically, I
recount the relationship that has traditionally existed between scientists and media professionals, explain
how this relationship has evolved over recent years, and highlight what I believe are some of the most
salient and exciting areas for future research examining scientists’ efforts to engage with the public.

Introduction
On November 13, 2014, the lander Philae touched down on the surface of a comet 300 million
miles away from Earth. This achievement was the result of the European Space Agency’s (ESA)
Rosetta mission, a 10-year-long effort to do something unthinkable: land a spacecraft on a
comet for the first time in the history of humankind. This miraculous feat, which is yielding data
to help us understand the origins of our planet (Feltman and McCoy 2014), was recently named
by the editors of the journal Science to be the top scientific breakthrough of 2014 (Hand 2014).
And yet, it was one of the ESA scientists, Matt Taylor, who generated the most media coverage
related to the mission. In appearances associated with the successful landing of Philae, Taylor in-
advertently stole the spotlight with a handful of sexist comments and his wardrobe – a button-
down shirt adorned with caricatures of scantily-clad women. A public relations debacle was
afoot, and the initial shine of the Rosetta mission was sullied. Taylor eventually offered a mea
culpa for his actions but not before he was persecuted online by journalists, bloggers, and the
general public through a trending hashtag #shirtstorm (Peterson 2014).
    This event epitomizes the brave new world of science communication. In its omnipresence,
the new digital media ecosystem has made science more transparent and accessible than ever
before. News of scientific findings travel swiftly. Scientific organizations and scientists them-
selves can communicate in novel, potentially empowering ways. And, as in the case of
#shirtstorm, messages from the scientific community have the potential to be amplified, shared,
and reacted to at a scope that was unimaginable merely 10 years ago.
    But how did we get here? The goal of this article is to provide an accessible answer to this
question, and to orient readers to the fertile area of research that examines the science–media
relationship and scientists as public communicators. Achieving this goal requires me to (i) re-
count the relationship that has traditionally existed between scientists and media professionals
(i.e. journalists), (ii) explain how this relationship has evolved over recent years with the advent
of sociocultural changes and a shifting media landscape, and (ii) highlight what I believe are
some of the most salient and exciting areas for future research examining scientists’ efforts to
engage with the public. My goal is not to provide an overview of the broader discipline

The scientist–journalist relationship
Journalists have traditionally been the gatekeepers of scientific information, charged with surveil-
ling and disseminating science news in ways that are (ideally) accessible, instructive, and interesting.
The relationship between scientists and journalists, however, is anything but straightforward. On
the one hand, both occupations have needed each other; scientists have depended on journalists
to communicate their research innovations and potential implications beyond the walls of the lab-
oratory, and journalists have relied on scientists to cooperate with them so that they can craft news
about science that is accurate and compelling. Yet, historical examinations of this relationship reveal
two occupations whose interactions have often been characterized by feelings of ambivalence, mis-
trust, and sometimes even disdain (Burnham 1987; Gregory and Miller 1998).
    Consistent with broader public concerns about the credibility of news media (PEW Research
Center 2012), scientists have often been critical of journalists, perceiving their work as being
oversimplified (Goodell 1977), inaccurate (Carsten and Illman 2002; Dunwoody 1982; Fellgett
1984; Goodell 1977), highly sensational or alarmist (Goodell 1977; Gorney 1992; Gregory and
Miller 1998; Schnabel 2003; Weigold 2001), focused on individuals (Goodell 1977), and falsely
balanced to give inappropriate equal weight to maverick scientists (Nelkin 1995; Weigold
2001). Surveys of scientists have continually unearthed these perceptions in recent decades.
One US-based survey conducted in the late 1990s revealed that only 11 percent of the scientists
interviewed had a great deal of confidence in the press, that 22 percent had hardly any confi-
dence in journalists, and that 90 percent thought few reporters understand the nature of science
(Hartz and Chappell 1997). UK-based surveys have found similar critical sentiments, one of
which revealed that only 10 percent of the scientist respondents believed that media coverage
of science helps to improve the public’s understanding of science (Corrado et al. 2000). Indeed,
scientists commonly attribute the public’s lack of scientific knowledge and un-scientific beliefs
to the news media (De Boer et al. 2005; Young and Matthews 2007).
    The coin f lips both ways, however. Journalists also frequently harbor negative perceptions
about scientists, commonly regarding them as being uncooperative (e.g. they do not want to ex-
plain their research in a language/method that non-scientists will understand and find interesting)
(Dunwoody and Ryan 1985; Goodell 1977; Treise and Weigold 2002), arrogant or defensive
(Bragg 1998), narrowly focused and self-absorbed (Weigold 2001), difficult to contact and slow
to respond (Dunwoody and Ryan 1985; Weigold 2001), and too interested in promoting a par-
ticular agenda or viewpoint and controlling the story content (Dunwoody and Ryan 1985). Ad-
ditionally, journalists often perceive scientists as improperly trained and prepared to interact with
journalists (Russell 2006).
    These negative perceptions are commonly attributed to the different norms and values un-
derlying both professions (Gregory and Miller 1998; Weigold 2001). Nelkin (1995) articulated
some of these fundamental normative differences. She noted that scientists see news as a product
of long-term, labored, vetted research, while journalists see news as current, dramatic, and en-
tertaining. In terms of sources, she observed that journalists desire celebrity science sources that
talk beyond their field of expertise, while scientists tend to dislike these types of sources. Nelkin
also explained how they differ in their communication style. Scientists, for example, tend to
communicate with caveats, context, and qualifiers, while journalists want their communications
to be simple and clear. Journalists also see value in amplifying findings (e.g. “breakthroughs”), person-
alizing research, and spotlighting conf lict and short-term contexts, whereas scientists tend to place

Scientists control over the news

Considering their predominately critical view of journalists, it is perhaps not surprising that the
scientific community sought to exert more inf luence on the process and product of science
journalism (Gregory and Miller 1998). According to some social scientists, in the mid- to
late-20th century, the scientific community realized that taking a more active role in the dis-
semination of scientific information to the public could ultimately function as self-preservation
for the scientific endeavor itself. For example, Hilgartner (1990) argued that scientists realized
that their public communication efforts could be a means to protect and/or gain credibility
and funding. Scientists began taking a more active stake in managing public communication be-
cause they assumed this raised their visibility for securing research funds, helped promote favor-
able public policies toward scientific and technological innovation, and helped secure prestige
for scientists in society (Nelkin 1987; 1995).
    This perspective is evident in what are now some commonplace tactics through which scientific
institutions exert some control of media coverage. For example, scientists commonly seek to cultivate
media attention by staging pseudo-events (i.e. press conferences) that are strategically designed to
drum-up press interest and subsequent coverage of scientific research (Goodell 1977). The scientific
community also regulates the interactions between journalists and scientists via the “Ingelfinger rule”
and the press embargo. The Ingelfinger rule “refers to a policy that states that a given journal will not
publish a scientific paper that has been already disseminated, particularly through the popular press”
(Kiernan 2006:18). This rule enjoys widespread support among journal editors and effectively con-
strains journalists’ ability to exert power over the reporting of scientific information. Press embargoes
are mechanisms through which scientific journals grant advance copies of their articles to certain jour-
nalists, who then must agree not to report the results until an agreed upon deadline (Kiernan 2003).
    Scientific organizations also inf luence media coverage of scientific innovations through their use
of public information officers (PIOs). PIOs work as intermediaries between scientists and journalists
and are charged with helping to translate scientific research into effective – and favorable – news re-
ports (Rogers 1986). This ‘translation’ often comes in the form of press releases, video releases, and
other pre-packaged PR materials, which are all communications produced by PIOs and sent directly
to media outlets to announce notable information (e.g. new discoveries, events). Journalists, it seems,
increasingly depend on pre-packaged information from science PIOs to aid their reporting. For ex-
ample, a recent independent analysis of BBC’s science coverage found that 75 percent of its broadcast
science news stories stemmed from source institutions’ press releases (BBC Trust, 2011). And recent
surveys and interviews of science journalists suggest that they no longer feel they have time to do
original, investigative science reporting (Williams & Clifford, 2009) and rely heavily on press releases
from scientific journals, such as Science and Nature (Granado, 2011).
    In sum, scientists and media professionals have historically had a complex relationship. Tradi-
tionally, scientists have depended on journalists to communicate their work to the masses, and
journalists have relied on scientists to be responsive to their efforts to do so. Yet, for reasons ex-
plored above, their interactions are often rocky, and scientists have commonly lamented what
they regard as the poor quality of science journalism. The scientific community, in part seeking
to alleviate these perceived shortcomings, increasingly sought to shape public messages about
science. Their initial efforts resulted in a number of commonly used mechanisms – among them,
press events, press embargoes, the Ingelfinger rule, news releases, and the proliferation of PIOs – but
they represent only the tip of the iceberg. In the next section, I turn our attention to how the

The public communication of science
Understanding the current status of the science–media relationship requires that we go beyond
the dynamic that has traditionally existed between these two professions. We must also consider
the salient sociocultural pressures and changes to the media environment that have evolved this
relationship to its present day form.

The “gap” between science and the public
Since at least the mid-20th century, thought-leaders have noted a troubling disconnect between
science and society (Gregory and Miller 1998; Mooney and Kirshenbaum 2009; Snow 1959).
Evidence of a continued gulf between scientists and the mainstream public is not hard to find. For
example, some indications suggest Americans may be somewhat disengaged and ill informed when
it comes to science. Aggregated US survey data published in the bi-annual “Science and Engineering
Indicators” volumes has consistently shown that many Americans provide incorrect answers to ques-
tions about basic factual knowledge of science and the scientific inquiry process (National Science
Board 2014). Other national surveys have shown that large portions of Americans believe that scien-
tific findings are hard for them to understand (Ho et al. 2008), that they have shown limited aware-
ness of and knowledge about specific scientific issues, such as nanotechnology (Lee et al. 2005), and
that they hold views about science-related issues (e.g. genetically modified food, climate change) that
are starkly different than those of scientists (Pew Research Center 2015).
    Although these statistics may unnerve the scientific community, scientists are frequently la-
beled as being out of touch with the public. Many scholars have noted the insular tendencies
of the scientific culture and explored how they have impeded scientists’ engagement with
non-scientists about their work (Brown et al. 2004; Dunwoody 1986; Gascoigne and Metcalfe
1997; Goodell 1977; Hagstrom 1965; Traweek 1988). And much fuss has been made over time
about the paternalistic, one-way communication approach that has typically been employed by
scientists’ in their attempts to connect with public audiences (Lewenstein 1992; Logan 2001;
Nelkin 1995; Weigold 2001). This approach – commonly referred to as the “deficit model”
– has hampered the science–public relationship, and its failings have inspired considerable aca-
demic focus on the development of alternate ways to think about and improve science–public
relationships (e.g. Miller 2001; Wynne 1996).
    There are some noteworthy reasons to believe that the disconnect between science and society has
been growing in recent years. Society, for example, is increasingly dependent on innovations born
from science and technology to address many of humankind’s greatest challenges, including the
development of viable alternate energy sources, disease prevention and treatment, and curbing
climate change (Dean 2009; Leshner 2003; Meredith 2010). However, many of the emergent tech-
nologies poised to address some of these challenges (e.g. synthetic biology, nanotechnology) intro-
duce complex ethical, legal, and social considerations that citizens often see as more important
than the science itself (Priest 2008). These emerging sciences may offer much promise, but they also
pose radical ontological, political, and religious implications that can complicate and intensify public
responses to them.
    Concurrently, media are undergoing dramatic changes that are weakening their traditional
role as the primary sources through which the public most frequently encounter science. The
abundance, quality, and reach of traditional science journalism have substantially eroded as a result
of the rise of the Internet and digital media technologies (Brumfiel 2009; Goede 2015; Russell

resulting in shrinking newshole for science (Pew Research Center 2008a) and a smaller pool of jour-
nalists who specialize in covering scientific research (Dudo et al. 2011). Concurrently, the percentage
of Americans who follow news about science and technology “very closely” has declined in recent
years (e.g. from 22 percent in 1998 to 16 percent in 2012) (National Science Board 2014). So, at a
time when scientific issues are becoming increasingly relevant, traditional media are devoting less
time and resources to monitoring these issues for the public.

Scientists as public communicators

The trends recounted above have caught the attention of scientific leaders who are increasingly
urging the scientific community to improve their efforts in public science communication. Cer-
tainly, these calls are not new; they can easily be traced back decades to efforts in the United
Kingdom that culminated in inf luential reports (see, for example, Bodmer 1985; Wolfendale
Committee Report 1995) emphasizing the importance of supporting scientists’ outreach (Miller
2001; Royal Society 2006; Sturgis and Allum 2004). But it seems that these calls are perhaps
louder and more inf luential than ever before (Dunwoody et al. 2009). For example, scientific
leaders from major US scientific organizations, including the American Association for the
Advancement of Science (Leshner 2003, 2015; Rowland 1993), National Academies of Science
(Cicerone 2006, 2010), and Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution (Reddy 2009), have all
passionately asked their scientific colleagues to improve their communication skills and engage
the public in meaningful dialogues about the challenges facing society and the role science and
technology will play in meeting these challenges.
   This growing attention presents at least two key questions: Have scientists heard these pleas?
And, specifically, to what extent are scientists engaging in public communication activities? For-
tunately, scholarship exploring these questions has proliferated in recent years. While some of
these studies have found evidence of continued struggles within the scientist–public relationship
(e.g. Brown et al. 2004; Davies 2008; Kohut et al. 2009; Neresini and Bucchi 2011), much of this
research has also unearthed findings that suggest improvements – or, at least, the potential for
them. Numerous surveys, for example have found that scientists engage in public communication
activities – whether directly to the public or indirectly via interactions with the media – more of-
ten than is commonly assumed (Corrado et. al. 2000; DiBella et al. 1991; Dunwoody and Ryan
1985; Dunwoody and Scott 1982; Jensen et al. 2008; Kreimer, et al. 2011; Pearson, 2001; Pearson
et al. 1997; Royal Society 2006; Torres-Albero et al. 2011; Wilkes and Kravitz 1992). One multi-
national survey, for example, found that 30 percent of the scientist respondents said they had been
engaged in more than five media contacts in the past 3 years while an additional 39 percent re-
ported having one to five media contacts (Peters et al. 2008).
   Many of these surveys have also found positive shifts in scientists’ valuing of public commu-
nication. For example, while scientists often regard public communication as a professional
responsibility (Gascoigne and Metcalfe 1997), they also often identify public communication
as an enjoyable activity (Corrado et al. 2000; Dunwoody et al. 2009; Martin-Sempere et al.
2008) that helps them to raise public interest in science (DiBella et al. 1991; Martin-Sempere
et al. 2008; Peters et al. 2008; Royal Society 2006). And scientists frequently rate their own ex-
periences interacting with media positively (Besley and Nisbet 2011), with one recent survey
finding that nearly two-thirds of the nanoscientist respondents felt that their contacts with the
media had a positive impact on them professionally (Dudo et al. 2014). Research in this area
has also helped to identify a number of factors that are associated with scientists who engage more
frequently, such as communication self-efficacy and autonomy, attitudes, scientific status, presumed

    In addition to the insights emerging from this f lurry of research, concrete efforts to aid scientists’
outreach have also increased. One substantial change is the growth of training designed to help sci-
entists communicate and work with media (Brown et al. 2004; Gold 2001; Miller and Fahy 2009;
Ossola 2014; Reddy 2009; Russell 2006; Smith et al. 2013). Scientific institutions, for example, have
increased online communication training resources, compiled guidelines for scientists to assist their
interactions with media (Peters et al. 2008b), and increasingly include a required public communica-
tion (i.e. “broader impacts”) component in the research they fund (Meredith 2010a; Roberts 2009).
The Alan Alda Center for Communicating Science at Stonybrook University (http://www.
centerforcommunicatingscience.org) and the American Association for the Advancement of Science
Center for Public Engagement (http://www.aaas.org/pes) are two particularly notable examples of
innovative training outlets devoted to helping scientists grow as public communicators. Scientist-to-
scientist advice about improving public communication also is increasingly common within the pages
of scientific and industry publications (e.g. Baum 2010; Condit 2007; Varner 2014).
    Student scientists are now reaping the rewards of pedagogical shifts that emphasize
communication training (Craig et al. 2008; Mogull 2008; Nisbet and Mooney 2005). For
example, all new doctoral students in environmental and climate scientists at Stockholm
University are offered training in speaking with the media (Reddy 2009), while other major
research universities, including the University of Wisconsin–Madison (Crone et al. 2011;
Dunwoody 2003) and Stanford University (Reddy 2009), offer similar programs to help
bolster the communication capabilities of their graduate students. Concurrently, initiatives
to foster forms of upstream engagement between science and society also seem to be on
the rise, among them various-themed science festivals (Durant and Ibrahim 2011; Winter
2004), science cafes (Clery 2003; Hidaka 2008), and more formal consensus conferences
(Kleinman et al. 2007). Recent scholarship is increasingly helping to evaluate and maximize
these approaches (Kleinman et al. 2011; Powell and Kleinman 2008). Finally, although
tomes about the public communication of science are not new, an explosion of books on
this topic – some with popular appeal – have been published within the last few years
(e.g. Baron 2010; Dean 2009; Hayes and Grossman 2006; Kuchner 2011; Nelsen et al.
2009; Olson 2009; Schwartzkroin 2009; Southwell 2013).
    Overall, the science–media relationship has changed rapidly within the last couple of decades.
Less willing to sit on the sidelines waiting to be asked to weigh-in on scientific innovation and
its societal implications, leaders of the scientific community have urged their colleagues to take a
more proactive approach to the public communication of their research. And, as the aforemen-
tioned discussion suggests, momentum seems to be headed in this direction. We are witnessing
“an increasing orientation of science toward the media today” (Peters 2013:110). Naturally, im-
portant new questions arise as scientists more regularly engage media and laypersons about re-
search. I next highlight some of these key questions.

Fertile areas for research on the public communication of science

Scientists online
The online ecosystem has drastically altered the marketplace for information about science and
scientific issues. When Americans seek information about science in the 21st century, the Inter-
net is a key resource (Brossard 2013). The percentage of Americans citing the Internet as their
primary source of science information has increased steadily since 2001, and in 2012, it became
Americans’ primary source for information about science and technology (National Science

Board 2014). During the same time period, Americans’ reliance on traditional media platforms
(e.g. television and print newspapers) decreased steadily, while the highest ever percentage of
Americans (63 percent) now report they would go online to find information about specific sci-
ence and technology issues (National Science Board 2014). These usage patterns appear to vary
by country. A recent Eurobarometer survey, for example, indicated that websites are
Europeans’ third most frequently used science information source (32 percent) behind TV
and newspapers (European Commission, 2013). Regardless of these platform preferences, how-
ever, it is important to note that much of the science information found online still originates
with mainstream news media (Ipsos MORI 2014).
    At the same time, the online media environment is fundamentally altering the way the sci-
entific community communicates both internally (e.g. with other scientists) and externally
(e.g. with lay persons, policymakers). In a perspective recently published in the journal Science,
Brossard and Scheufele (2013) called attention to this scenario noting that the new realities of
the online information environment “will increasingly force scientists … to rethink the inter-
face between science and the public” (p. 40). In addition to the aforementioned changes to the
science information landscape, Brossard and Scheufele highlighted how aspects of Web 2.0 –
specifically social networking tools like Twitter, Facebook, Google+, etc. – will fundamen-
tally change the interactions among scientists, media professionals, and laypersons. New media
tools, on the one hand, are providing novel opportunities for networked collaboration among
scientists (VanNoorden 2014) in ways that can empower research (Shneiderman 2008). For
example, scientists can now use Facebook to efficiently crowdsource tedious species identifi-
cation tasks (Sidlauskas et al. 2011) and virtual worlds to conduct unique social and economic
research (Bainbridge 2007).
    These same new media tools also empower scientists’ strategic communication possibilities.
The scientific community can leverage these technologies to have greater control over their
public messages, no longer as reliant on middleman (i.e. journalists) (Peters et al. 2014). One
noteworthy example is theconversation.com, a web-based initiative that markets itself as “an
independent source of news and views from the academic and research community, delivered
direct to the public.” This website, which places an emphasis on providing facts and evidence
derived from university research, has an expanding online presence, with editions currently
published in Africa, Australia, the United Kingdom, and the United States. The website Futu-
rity.org is another example of a science-led strategic communication initiative. Futurity.org
launched in 2009 and serves as a clearinghouse for publicizing scientific findings from more than
60 research universities. The site aims to increase the public visibility of each partner university’s
scientific contributions and to help establish direct communications with stakeholders.
    Individual scientists are also more accessible, in terms of both their research and their personal
opinions. Individual scientists also can now engage with lay audiences in ways that can be more
direct, dialogic, personable, and transparent. These tools have the potential to help democratize
science by enabling bi-directional communication that offer non-technical audiences a mode to
share their viewpoints about scientific issues (Delborne et al., 2013; Peters 2013) and even con-
tribute to the generation of scientific knowledge itself (Einsiedel 2014). For example, The New
Reddit Journal of Science (http://www.reddit.com/r/science) offers Reddit users the chance to
chat with scientists in real-time through the popular “ask me anything” (i.e. AMA) platform.
Though formed only recently, this subreddit is already one of the most popular on the site,
boasting a community of more than six million subscribers (Owens 2014).

have f lourished in recent years. Much of this activity has resulted in enthusiastic pleas for scien-
tists to embrace social media tools to help champion the voice of science. Science bloggers
have offered many of these pleas (e.g. Darling 2014), but these pleas are also often present

“critical to the distribution of scientific information … to the general population.” (p. 3).
Bik and Goldstein (2013) operate from this same position in their recent PLOS One article.
They provide a detailed overview of current tools and specific suggestions for how scientists
can use them effectively to communicate their research. They also recount some of the ben-
efits that scientists can reap by using these tools and techniques, which include improving re-
search efficiency and citation rates, enhancing professional networking, and advancing the
broader impacts of their research.
   But to what extent are scientists’ using social media tools? What are their platform prefer-
ences? What motivates them to use (or be willing to use) these tools? And what are some com-
mon outcomes of these efforts? These questions represent what is an incredibly fertile area for
research, but one for which “empirical research … is still scant” (Brossard 2013:14100). Social
scientific research is turning its collective eye toward these questions but have, at this point in
time, merely whetted the appetite and helped to refine research questions. Basic research is still
needed to provide a better descriptive sense of scientists and their use of social media. For
instance, one recent survey unearthed evidence that scientists embrace Twitter in particular
(Yeo et al., 2014), while another survey found that scientists use social media – including Twit-
ter and Facebook – rather modestly to communicate about their research with the public (Dudo
et al. 2014). Besley’s (2014) work identified what may be common motivators of scientists’ will-
ingness to communicate with the public online – specifically, norms, efficacy, and a desire to
contribute to public debate – but additional research is needed to vet and refine these results.

The dark side of scientist communicators

It is essential to note that enhanced public dialogue about science does not inherently stem from
the use of new media tools. Some scholars, in fact, fear that the new media environment may
weaken the professional credibility of science (Weingart 2012). Scientists, for example, can be
far more transparent, using these tools to expose their personalities and belief systems. Popular
micro-blogs (e.g. Twitter) and science-themed blogs (e.g. scienceblogs.com, researchblogging.
org) provide visible public platforms for scientists to express their views on myriad topics. These
platforms have the potential to make science and scientists more accessible, but they also have the
potential to damage public perceptions of science and scientists. Celebrity physician, Mehmet Oz,
learned this the hard way when his recent Twitter forum yielded piles of scathing barbs, including
questions such as “I just got my f lu shot, when can I expect to develop autism?” and “Why have
you not been censured of fired from @ColumbiaSurgery for conduct unbecoming a physician,
scientist, and gentleman?” (http://www.vox.com/2014/11/12/7203269/dr-oz-health-twitter)
The Dr. Oz example is extreme, but it highlights the broader, pressing need to explore how so-
cial media can foment public rancor toward experts and the scientific community. Some research
has sought to examine the social implications of science blogging (e.g. Riesch and Mendel 2013;
Shanahan 2011; Wilkins 2008), but more research is needed.
    The proliferation of strategic communication associated with scientific research can also pose
risks. As mentioned earlier, public information officers (PIOs) have played an important role
facilitating interactions between scientists and external audiences, particularly journalists. How-
ever, PIOs’ inf luence on these relationships seems to be growing, with some scholars noting
how media relations have becoming increasingly institutionalized within scientific organizations

and universities (Peters 2012). This phenomenon, which is commonly referred to as ‘medialization’
(Weingart, 1998), has resulted in a growing body of research that is documenting the increasing sa-
lience of media criteria within the scientific community and research process (Ivanova et al. 2013;
Peters et al. 2008; Rödder 2009, 2011). For example, one recent study (Sumner et al. 2014) ana-
lyzed news releases about scientific and biomedical research emerging from 20 UK-based universi-
ties, the related scientific papers, and the news articles associated with the releases. Their examination
found frequent exaggerations about the science in the news releases and the related news articles,
leading the authors to call for reforms to university strategic communications. Basken (2014)
discussed the Sumner et al. study and other similar examples of hyperbolic science-related PR em-
anating from the University of Colorado at Boulder and Exeter University. Ultimately, these and
other similar examples (see, Williams and Gajevic, 2012) highlight how the increasingly competitive
PR infrastructure found within scientific institutions may lead to systemic use of problematic com-
munication practices.


Scientists’ communication training and goals

As mentioned earlier, there is an upswell of activity relative to training scientists to become
better public communicators. Many organizations now provide communication training to
scientists in the United States (Brown et al. 2004; Gold 2001; Smith et al. 2013), and elsewhere
(Miller and Fahy 2009). As these types of organizations grow and reach more scientists, it be-
comes necessary to assess the nature of their training programs and their outcomes. Evaluative
assessments of these programs can help refine their mechanics in order to maximize their im-
pacts on scientists and the audiences with which they engage. Presently, however, there is little
empirical understanding of how scientists value communication training, nor is there a solid un-
derstanding of science communication trainers’ specific objectives. It may be that much of the
training activity focuses primarily on helping scientists develop discrete media relations skills
even at a time when an emphasis is being placed on science communication that moves away
from simply conveying scientific information and instead emphasizes more dialogic scientist–
public interactions (McCallie et al. 2009). Some recent work, for example, found that European
governmental and university policies continue to emphasize knowledge transmission in their
scientist training (Trench and Miller 2012).
   Besley, Dudo, and Storksdieck (2015) recently sought to evaluate the degree to which
scientists see value in training focused on addressing specific communication goals and the
variables associated with scientists’ willingness to participate in training focused on these
specific goals. They examined five goals: (i) training designed to make science messages
understandable; (ii) attitude-focused training meant to build trust and credibility; (iii)
training meant to demonstrate that one listens to the public; (4) training meant to
demonstrate that one cares about the public’s views; and (5) training meant to frame mes-
sages to resonate with audiences’ pre-existing values. Results indicated that scientists most
value communication training that focuses on message comprehension and establishing
credibility, and least value training that emphasizes how to frame messages that connect with
different audiences. Further, valuing each of the goals was associated with viewing the goals
as being ethical and believing that public engagement is effective. These findings imply that
training organizations still have work to do when it comes to diversifying scientists’ commu-
nication goals. More research is urgently needed to unpack the nature of science communi-
cation training, the communication goals it emphasizes, and scientists’ willingness to
embrace these specific goals.

Conclusion

Salient research questions about the relationships among science, media, and the public abound.
New media may indeed “fundamentally transform the interface between science and society,”
but the results of this transformation are not yet known (Peters et al. 2014:749). Calls for scientists
to become more frequent, facile public communicators are plentiful, especially those beseeching
scientists to wield the power of new media to enhance their connections with new audiences.
Indeed, as I am writing this manuscript, Alan Leshner, the CEO of AAAS, published an editorial
in the journal Science imploring scientists to engage with journalists and the public about polariz-
ing scientific issues in ways that are respectful, transparent, and conversational (Leshner 2015).
And, yet, Peters et al. (2014) note, “… it remains to be seen whether the majority of scientists,
in addition to their duties as researchers, teachers, science managers and committee members, will
add public communication to their routine activities” (p. 752). Media professionals will always
play an important role in disseminating information about science to the public, but our modern
media ecosystem grants scientists more power than ever before to be proactive about their public
communication. Examining how scientists use this power is essential at a time when so many
scientific issues are urgent, complex, impactful, and politicized.
