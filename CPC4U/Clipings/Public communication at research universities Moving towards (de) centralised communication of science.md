                                 Abstract
                                 This research note reports empirical observations on public communication of research institutes within
                                 universities, using data from an international quantitative study in eight countries (N = 2030). The note
                                 aims to contribute to discussions on the role of science communication at research universities. We
                                 observe growing science communication at the institute level, which indicates, at a first glance, a trend
                                 towards decentralised communication of science. We argue that these might be places where science
                                 communication and public engagement can thrive. Rather than claiming to be conclusive, our goal here is to
                                 stimulate discussion on the ongoing changes in the organisational science communication landscape, and the
                                 consequences it may have for practice.


                                 Keywords
                                 institutional public communication, research institutes, ‘Decentralisation Hypothesis’, science communication



                                 1. Introduction
                                 In this research note, we make some observations on ongoing changes in public communication of
                                 research institutes, and discuss what they might represent for universities and public communica-
                                 tion of science, through the lens of science communication research. At the same time, as we col-
                                 late definitions from different literatures, we attempt to define key terms to ensure accessibility and
                                 understanding of key vocabulary used in this note, and we hope that our summary and clarification
                                 might also be helpful in future work.

basic assumption that the institutional communication analysed is a reaction to the new era of ‘aca-

and Oplatka, 2006; Krücken, 2014). We then describe four indicators that emerge from the data
which point to signs of decentralised communication of science, and discuss opportunities and
challenges for science communication practice.

2. Public communication at research universities
Communication among academics or between universities and publics is nothing new. Indeed, out-
reach and community engagement have been the third mission adopted by most universities around the
world (Laredo, 2007; McNall et al., 2009). However, the traditional ‘outreach function’ is taking on an
added urgency and many new formats in a world of global competition among universities for stu-
dents, staff and funding streams, and of increasing demands for ‘public engagement with science’ and
democratic processes of science governance (European Commission, 2013; Nowotny et al., 2013).
   Organisational and corporate communication literature document a gradual process of marketisa-
tion at publicly funded universities, from the 1980s onward, from elite to broader strategic communi-
cation. This literature shows that universities have adopted a promotional attitude to cultivate a brand
name and identity, build and protect a reputation (Wæraas and Sataøen, 2019) and reach intended
publics and even counter-publics (Cunningham et al., 2009). Central to this change was a state policy
paradigm shift to NPM reforms in the public sector to run public organisations in a more business-like
manner, through the adoption of market-oriented models (e.g. Hemsley-Brown et al., 2006; Paradeise
et al., 2009). This adaptation of universities was also understood by many as a way to achieve aca-
demic autonomy and freedom from government regulation (Henkel, 2005).
   The implications of NPM reforms for universities’ communication are reflected in the centrali-
sation and professionalisation of PR and marketing. This is primarily seen in the rise of central
communications offices (communications departments/marketing/PR offices, under different
names) and the ‘management profession’, responsible for managing relationships between the uni-
versity and society (e.g. Krücken and Meier, 2006).
   It is beyond the scope of this note to dig into the history of professionalisation of PR and mar-
keting functions at universities and scientific organisations, and that is not our aim. Yet, it is rele-
vant that it has evolved from an understanding that governmental relations and the support of
society were needed for organisations to thrive: the idea that they needed to pay attention to public
opinion, views, and participation (PR) and to mechanisms to advertise their products (marketing)
and influence policy and funding (lobbying; for example, Grunig and Grunig, 2008; Hutchins and

Tindall, 2016). Professional communication offices and officers thus have been seen as necessary
in gaining that support and trust (Borchelt and Nielsen, 2014; Grunig and Grunig, 2008). One
could say that similar forces have pushed a development of ‘traditional outreach’ to take on board
‘public engagement with science and policy’ ideals (House of Lords, 2000) that have entered the
university landscape in recent years (e.g. Krabbenborg and Mulder, 2015).
    Although still scarce, the PUS literature sheds light on how science communication is entering and
shaping the university landscape – what we could perhaps call the science communication function of
universities, that is, the communication efforts and activities employed by a university to communi-
cate and engage publics in research.2 With an empirical focus primarily on aspects of science mediali-
sation, the available research shows an intensification of media communication and PR products over
the years (Autzen, 2018; Boumans, 2018; Koso, 2021; Vogler and Schäfer, 2020), and increased use
of websites and social media channels to disseminate research contents (Fähnrich et al., 2020; Metag
and Schäfer, 2019). The literature also points to a move towards professionalisation of science com-
munication in universities and scientific organisations. Some now employ ‘science communicators’
and ‘public engagement professionals’ to engage publics in research and support researchers in com-
municating science (e.g. Wellcome Trust, 2015), dedicate resources and have policies in place for it.
Despite lacking a defined professional identity, and with science communication tasks clashing with
marketing, PR and administrative tasks (e.g. Schwetje et al., 2020; Watermeyer and Lewis, 2018),
communication professionals associate the attainment of their university goals with contributing to
the social role of universities and civic engagement (Elken et al., 2018; Koivumäki and Wilkinson,
2020); but also perceive public engagement servicing more instrumental goals specific to research
governance (e.g. Research Excellence Framework (REF) in UK universities), and facing issues of
support (e.g. funding, training) and skills (Watermeyer and Lewis, 2018). Moreover, studies with a
focus on scientists point to the positive impact that these professionals and the organisational context
more broadly (e.g. funding and policies) can have on scientists’ engagement with publics, particularly
the media and journalists (e.g. Horst, 2013; Marcinkowski et al., 2014; Entradas and Bauer, 2019).
    The need for medialised communication and attention-seeking has not only led scholarly discus-
sions on the limited role that science communication has played at central/PR offices of universities
and the ‘push communication’ adopted for promotion (e.g. Marcinkowski and Kohring, 2018), but also
raised concerns on the consequences of this medialisation for the university itself and the autonomy of
science; that is, there are questions about whether it empowers the university by increasing the visibil-
ity of science and scientists in the public sphere, or denigrates it by turning efforts towards the opera-
tional requirements of competitiveness to the detriment of pursuing their focal research goals (e.g.
Peters et al., 2008; Weingart and Maasen, 2007).
    This literature situates science communication in an internal conflict with central offices’ tasks
and priorities, leading to a question: what if the role of science communication is at levels other
than central communications structures? Science communication may be seen at the level of
research institutes, not least given their role in producing knowledge, maintaining privileged rela-
tionships with stakeholders, and attracting funding, which gives them not only a need, but also puts
them in a better position to communicate about their area of study and engage publics in research.
This could be an obvious response on the part of institutes, which may feel that their work is not
given enough coverage by central communications, or part of a wider institutional governance
policy to reach broader coverage. Whatever the driver might be, in the context described, it makes
sense to expect some type of distributed communication of research within universities, and a
move away from a more centralised communication strategy at communications/PR offices to
decentralised communication at other levels of the university.
    We borrow the concept of (de)centralisation from the management literature (Mahoney, 1997;
Williamson and Chandler, 1964). The concept has since been used in various contexts, including

governance policy (e.g. education, public health), public administration, law, economics and others,
with debates often around the advantages of centralised versus decentralised decision-making (e.g.
Thanh et al., 2016). In its original meaning, decentralisation involves moving decision-making away
from central control and giving some level of power to others within the organisation. Here, we use
the term ‘decentralisation’ in the context of communication to refer to distributed communication of
science (see Note 1) through different levels of the university (both central and meso); we assume that
it embraces its original meaning of decentralised decision-making, not least because research insti-
tutes are likely to be financially autonomous structures in many universities, which also gives them
some level of decision-making power over their communications, for example, hiring staff.

3. Methods
Data and data collection
The data are from the international study MORE-PE: MObilisation of REsources for Public
Engagement, which mapped public communication practices of research institutes within publicly
funded universities in eight countries: Italy, Germany, Portugal, the Netherlands, the United
Kingdom, the United States of America, Brazil and Japan. The study used probability samples of
institutes stratified by the six Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD,
2015) areas of research (N = 200 institutes per research area in each country, N total = 1200 insti-
tutes per country), so that findings would not be biased by areas more likely to engage in public
communication. We contacted N = 8033 institutes, in all areas of research, and received N = 2030
responses, for a response rate of 25% (see Supplemental Table S1, for sample description). The
institutes surveyed were medium to small in size (M = 116 researchers per institute, SD = 406.9;
median = 43), capturing on average between €250.000 and 500.000 for research annually. Responses
are from representatives of the research institutes, with a good grasp of the unit’s functioning and
activities, either the director/head or responsible communications representative. One question-
naire was collected per institution (please see Entradas et al., 2020 for full description of methodol-
ogy including sample design and data collection).


4. Results
Indicators of decentralised communication of science
The argument is built on four indicators of development of public communication at the institute
level. These are: (1) the time of creation and evolution of the activity in recent years, (2) the port-
folio of activities organised and audiences addressed, (3) the resources (e.g. staff, funding) and
policies/guidelines in place for science communication and (4) the goals of communication. Our
goal is not to comparatively access public communication across countries, which we have done
elsewhere Entradas et al., (2020), but to use more extensive and robust data to show that the
changes observed in the academic landscape are not unique to a single country, but are likely an
international phenomenon across countries with different national traditions of public engagement
in science (Mejlgaard et al., 2018), scientific systems and R&D resources. As such, we present data
breakdown by countries in the tables, and refer to the most significant differences.

Time of creation and evolution of the activity over time. The first is an indicator of the period in time
when research institutes started doing science communication, and how this activity has evolved in
recent years. Half of the surveyed institutes reported engaging in some type of public communica-

Portfolio of activities and target audiences. A second indicator is the portfolio of activities that insti-
tutes organise and the varied range of audiences they aim to reach. The reported activities ranged
from public lectures (the average institute organising around 10 public lectures per year, with the
large majority of the institutes organising lectures (84%, M = 10, SD = 15)), to open days (76%,
M = 5.5, SD = 11), talks at schools (63%, M = 4.4, SD = 9.2), events with local stakeholders, such as
business and industry (59%, M = 3.0, SD = 6.4), public exhibitions (56%, M = 2.3, SD = 5.4), science
cafés and public discussions (47%, M = 2.1, SD = 5.1), science festivals (35%, M = 1.5, SD = 3.8)

    Media contact was also frequent, with the average institute giving around seven interviews for
newspapers (76%, M = 6.5, SD = 11.1), writing around the same amount of press releases annually
(70%, M = 6.5, SD = 11.6), while also writing articles for magazines and newspapers (73%, M = 5.8,
SD = 11.1), and giving interviews for the radio (63%, M = 3.9, SD = 8.5) and TV (61%, M = 3.5,
SD = 8.0). New media channels were less in use at this level of organisation with many reporting
never using Facebook (46%; 13% used it daily) or Twitter (60%; 10% used it daily), yet the insti-
tutional website is a frequent online means with around 88% of the institutes updating it regularly
(M = 55 updates per year, SD = 81); 37% used YouTube (M = 6.6, SD = 27) and 14% used podcasts
(M = 2.3, SD = 15). Moreover, the pattern of activities is similar across countries, with a slight vari-
ation in intensity for some of the reported activities (mostly new media), described elsewhere
(Entradas et al., (2020); see Supplemental Table S2). What is important to note, however, is that in
all countries there is a broad range of activities ongoing. As a benchmark, the median institute
reported 25 media interactions (M = 46, SD = 1.5), 21 public events (M = 33, SD = 0.9) and 52 online
interactions per year (M = 146, SD = 4.8). Institutes addressed various audiences, more often the
general public (50% addressing them frequently), schools and students (45%); less frequently,
industry (37%), journalists (34%) and policy-makers (21%). These are signs of decentralised com-
munication at the institute level.

   It is interesting to note that while media and journalists are not institutes’ main target audiences,
the high focus on media activities denote some aspirations for increased media coverage, also seen
in views about media reporting of their research: the vast majority of institutes (78%) agreed that
‘media visibility is important for their [our] research’, and that ‘media should give more visibility
to their [our] research’ (62%), recognising media relations as part of their tasks (Table 3). These
aspirations are visible in institutes across countries, yet greater aspirations are found in Italy,
Portugal and Brazil, possibly, a result of the low coverage of science in the media and few science
journalists in these countries (Bauer et al., 2012).

Further signs are the resources employed by institutes – communications staff and funding, and
policies in place for public communication.

Communications staff and specialisation. In our sample, around a third (38%) of the institutes
employed communications staff within the unit, and around half used the central communications
offices to disseminate their news; except in Portugal and Germany, where more institutes employed
staff within the unit (p < .05). Specialisation is overall low. In those institutes employing commu-
nications staff, an interesting trend emerges: in half of them (around 20% of all institutes), com-
munications staff are highly professionalised (possess a degree in a communication-related field)
and fully dedicated to communication tasks; in the other half (20% of all institutions), communica-
tions staff are responsible for a mixture of tasks, including administrative, unit’s events and other
activities including communications tasks, and are also less specialised, having either attended
short courses in communication or trained on the job.

Funding for public communication. Funding for science communication activities is overall low, with
the average institute spending around 3% of their research budget on communications. About 57%
spend between 1% and 5%, and about 6% of the institutes reported spending > 5% on public com-
munications. And while this is a characteristic across countries, spending is higher in Portugal, the
United Kingdom and the United States when compared with Brazil or Japan.

Policy/guidelines for public communication. About half of the institutes have policies, guidelines or plans
for public communication, and 45% say that despite not being guided by a policy, they nevertheless
engage in public communications. These general trends are found across countries, with some varia-
tion. For example, more institutes in Portugal and Italy reported having a policy (73%) and less
institutes in the United Kingdom or the United States (35%) have one. Regardless of these variations,
these signs of professionalisation are visible in all countries, but clearly, institute-based science com-
munication professionalisation is early in its development and roll-out (Table 3).

Goals of communication and expected outcomes. Our last consideration refers to the goals and out-
comes that institutions foresee from their public communication efforts. From a list of eight ration-
ales to carry out communication activities for non-specialists, the most important reasons indicated
by institutes seem to suggest public interest rather than self-interested goals. The overwhelming
majority of institutes across all countries reported engaging primarily ‘to disseminate research
results to broad audiences’, and ‘to respond to the university mission’ and ‘national policies’.
Rationales such as ‘get public support for research’, ‘raise research profile’, and ‘attract funding’
were seen as less important. We find some variation in these goals across countries, for example,
in the United Kingdom, Portugal and the Netherlands, responding to national policies assumed
more relevance than institutional missions – perhaps, a consequence of the strong policy environ-
ment for public communication in these countries – and in the United Kingdom and the Nether-
lands, the goal to raise the institute’s profile gained more prominence (Table 4).

suggests that the observed developments are at early stages in many institutions and signal a need
for science communication at this level. The data show that institutes allocate time and resources
to public communication as indicated by the varied portfolio of activities organised for external
publics, the communications staff employed, and the plans and policies for communication in
place in many of the surveyed institutes. These are signs of distributed communication of science,
and point to a tendency for decentralised communication of research in many universities. These
signs are found across countries despite some differences, further suggesting that (de)centralisation
is a characteristic of the organisation rather than a country specification.
    The data also suggest that the degree of decentralisation may vary. However, with the available
evidence, we can only speculate about forms that (de)centralisation may take. For example, if we
were to consider the presence and absence of communications staff within an institute as an indica-
tor of autonomy and decision-making power, we could think of possible forms of (de)centralisa-
tion. This indicator is obviously limited, but it helps to set the discussion on possible approaches
that may be chosen by organisations to talk about their research.
    Institutes might be fully in charge of their communications, have full decision-making power
and autonomy over their communications strategy, employ their staff and organise their own activ-
ities (full decentralisation); in our sample, this could potentially correspond to a maximum of 40%
of the institutes if institutes had full control, which the data cannot prove. There might also be cases
where institutes share control over their communications (partial (de)centralisation), for example,
institutes that despite having autonomy over activities and resources, work on a common frame-
work with the university communications’ strategy; or institutes that are less professionalised and
use central communications for disseminating news and events; that is, partial decentralisation
implies some interdependency between different levels of the university, whether it concerns
resources or hierarchy. In our sample, about a third of the institutes used central offices for dissemi-
nating their activities. Also, full and partial centralisation can co-habit within the same university;
there might be institutes that have full autonomy and resources and others that do not. And finally,
some, most likely smaller, universities might want full control over all institutional communica-
tion, and have a centralised communication strategy and decision-making power (full centralisation);
the data suggest that full centralisation might be more rare, as most institutes get involved in some
type of public communication. Thus, situations are likely to be complex, and as such decentralisa-
tion and centralisation approaches should not be understood as a binary, but opposite ends of a
continuum upon which institutes are more or less in charge of their communication activities.
    Certainly, not all forms would be useful for resource sustainability of a university. The value of
a particular form of management of the science communication function will depend in part on the
characteristics of the university, the resources they have available and how they see their role in the
public debate Entradas et al., (2020). There is also a compromise between two opposite effects of
decentralisation: on one hand, decentralisation might lead to a better use of resources distributed
throughout the lower levels of the organisation and broader coverage of institutional communica-
tion; on the other hand, it involves a loss of control for the upper-level management on what is
done, and might run the risk of haphazard institutional communication and difficulties in harmo-
nising the institutional message and image. Our findings suggest nevertheless that the ongoing
changes may most likely be characterised as a tendency for research institutes to become more
fully in charge of their communications, hire their professionals and have their own communica-
tion strategy. The decentralisation hypothesis then poses:

     H1. There is a growing tendency towards decentralised communication of science at research
     universities.

    Our second observation concerns the role of science communication at the meso-level structures
and the value of (de)centralised communication of science for the university. From the portfolio of
activities and target audiences, we can say that institutes are putting their efforts into delivering
activities rather than establishing media relations, regardless of what their aspirations for media
coverage might be. Moreover, the activities carried out at this level include, yet to a lesser extent
than one-way activities, two-way communication (e.g. public debates, science cafés, policy advis-
ing/making events). This, and the reported goals of ‘disseminating research to the public’ and
‘responding to the institutional mission’, point to primarily internal motivations to contribute to the
civic goals of science communication and public engagement as in knowledge, education and
enlightenment, rather than self-interest and promotion. These seem different ambitions from those
of central communications, mainly dealing with media and PR tasks. Research institutes might
pursue their goals, as identified here, and continue growing their efforts in science communication,
thus contributing to suppressing the limited role science communication has had in central offices
(e.g. Marcinkowski and Kohring, 2018).
    Science communication may then have more space to develop at the institute level. Research
institutes carry out a broad range of activities and maintain privileged contact with audiences,
which otherwise would not have been done by central offices. At the same time, communication
may be more credible and authentic when scientists communicate directly with publics, who are in
turn the main contributors of public communication at the institute level as Entradas et al., (2020).
One could then argue that decentralised communication of science could favour the embedding of
science communication and public engagement tasks in university practice and culture. However,
in situations where the broad university communication strategy is strongly driven by ideals of
visibility, tensions could emerge at the institute level when pressed to follow the same ideals. This
could counteract the good intentions of research institutes to engage in science communication,
with consequences for science communication that becomes yet another tool for institutional visi-
bility, also at the meso-level.
    Finally, the evidence provided here cannot yet prove this thesis. Longitudinal data on these
indicators, and on the activities and goals of central offices, as well as relationships and work coor-
dination between the various communications structures, will help understand the autonomy of
institutes, and how the science communication function is developing at universities. For instance,
do meso structures work under a common communication strategy or fully independently? Do they
have their own online channels for free use? Do their communication products require approval
from central communications? Answers to these and other questions will provide clues to whether
science communication is embedding itself at the research institute level and institutes are embrac-
ing the responsibility fully, or if these are dispersed tasks that the central level is off-loading
because they are unwilling or unable to carry them out. One thing seems clear from this study:
there is work in progress at the level of research institutes, which is likely to foster a growing
capacity for decentralised communication of science at universities.
